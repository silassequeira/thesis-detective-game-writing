# 6. Early Design Rehearsals

## 6.1 Protocol and Physical Materials

The early design rehearsals were facilitator-led, low-fidelity role-playing sessions built to test the Two-Slot Epistemic Interaction System and the investigative experience behind it. These sessions examined whether participants could read case evidence, build provisional theories, and communicate investigative goals by pairing one or two evidence objects during questioning and confrontation. They also looked at whether participants could use the dream sequence to form and revise a case theory. Throughout this chapter, these low-fidelity workshop sessions are called *rehearsals*.

These sessions served as an early test of the project’s narrative and interaction design before building a digital prototype. Evaluating a finished digital interface or proving the final effectiveness of the game systems fell outside their scope.

Each rehearsal opened with a short briefing. Participants heard an overview of the fictional case, took on the role of Detective Stoker, and learned that their goal was to prepare a well-supported accusation for Captain Miller. A tutorial sheet and verbal explanation introduced the Two-Slot Epistemic Interaction System. Participants were encouraged to move beyond collecting documents and pick one or two items linked to a specific question, contradiction, suspicion, or confrontation based on their reading of the clues.

Participants began with a small set of starter reports and printed images ([Figure X]). Further documents were available on request through the simulated police evidence system (see 6.2.3). Diegetically, the investigation took place over a single afternoon shift, giving Detective Stoker limited time to review files, question suspects, request reports, and prepare an accusation.

Role-play led by the facilitator stood in for several parts of the proposed digital system. The facilitator played multiple characters, including the fictional evidence-locker assistant "João" (acting as the police computer), precinct supervisor Captain Miller, and both suspects, Elara and Kaelen. Inside the story world, the facilitator could supply extra narrative details or interactions that fit the fictional setting. When a participant made an unavailable, premature, or out-of-scope request, the facilitator answered in character. Where appropriate, the facilitator also directed participants toward valid evidence channels or gave a plausible story reason for the refusal.

Once the rehearsal started, participants could ask to question either suspect. They formulated their own questions during interrogations while grounding each inquiry or confrontation in one or two evidence objects. The role-played characters answered based on prepared profiles detailing what each person knew, believed, hid, and was willing to share. Responses included clarification, denial, evasion, emotional resistance, partial disclosure, or new information.

Some reports needed extra processing and arrived after a delay (see 6.2.5). As the session neared its end, Captain Miller reminded participants of the remaining time. Sessions closed when participants felt ready to present their findings or when time ran out. Participants then laid out their theory to Captain Miller, naming the responsible suspect or suspects and explaining the supporting evidence. Outcomes depended on whether the presented case was coherent, grounded in evidence, and institutionally sufficient, with no requirement to uncover a single hidden truth.

Throughout each session, the facilitator tracked interactions in structured logs and observation notes. Participants filled out an evaluation questionnaire afterward ([Appendix X]).

### 6.2.1 Case Setting and Participant Role

The rehearsal took place in Oakhaven Central, 4th District Station, in the Gallows Creek neighborhood of the fictional city of Oakhaven. Confining the case to a police station removed the need for open-world exploration while keeping the institutional workflow for requesting, classifying, interpreting, and acting on evidence.

| |
| ##### Figure 38 - Four-room station layout |

Four connected spaces represented the station (Figure 38), comprising the Evidence Room, the Interrogation Room, the Observation Room, and the Corridor. Inside the Evidence Room, participants used the simulated police computer to request reports and review case files. The Interrogation Room held questioning with suspects Elara Vance and Kaelen "Kae" Ward. The Observation Room housed Captain Miller and Officer "Buck" Buckley, giving participants an institutional channel for requests that the evidence system could not handle. The Corridor linked these rooms within the fictional precinct.

Participants played Detective Julian Hammond Stoker, a newly appointed homicide detective (5.3.1). The scenario separated personal suspicion from institutional judgment, meaning a player might believe a suspect was guilty yet still lack enough evidence to justify an accusation to Captain Miller.

The facilitator worked from the Chapter 5 case model (5.2), which kept role-play consistent while leaving interpretation open. Participants never saw this full model. Their investigation drew strictly on the participant-accessible materials, including the starter dossier, requested reports, physical evidence representations, institutional memoranda, dialogue responses, dream material, and the results of their own decisions.

### 6.2.2 Evidence Reports as Indexical Clues

Following 4.2.1, reports and objects functioned as indexical clues. A toxicology result could show intoxication without settling intent, and a witness statement could place someone near the scene without proving motive, causation, or guilt. A police memorandum could pass along procedural details while hinting at institutional pressure for a clean, prosecutable reading of the case. Red-herring reports also appeared in the file, pushing participants to judge relevance before committing to a theory.

The evidence corpus covered forensic reports, toxicology results, witness statements, police memoranda, behavioral observations, medical records, and physical-evidence items ([Appendix X]). Evidence could support multiple readings, though not every reading held equal weight. Participants still had to anchor their theories in the available material and defend their reasoning during dialogue and the final accusation. What mattered was what an item implied when weighed alongside other traces in the case.

### 6.2.3 Evidence Corpus and Institutional Requests

Participants began with a limited case folder setting out the basic conditions of the investigation. This starter set held arrest reports for Elara Vance and Kae Ward, the homicide incident report, an official police memorandum, and the witness statement of Meredith Gable. Players also received physical and visual items, including the firearm, fired projectile, wet hooded sweatshirt, Somnoryl-μ vials, prescription vial, victim’s wallet, footwear impressions, crime-scene photograph, surveillance image, suspect portraits, and the previous detective’s note.

These initial materials pointed toward competing interpretations right away. The firearm and Kae’s history supported theories around weapon control, robbery, violence, or concealment. Elara’s condition, bare feet, wet clothing, and altered state pointed toward physical presence, intoxication, impaired agency, or unreliable memory. Witness testimony, footwear traces, the surveillance gap, and crime-scene photos introduced doubts about timing, visibility, movement, and environmental clues.

Four institutional channels supplied additional reports, namely the Forensics Laboratory, Medical Unit, Controlled Substances Unit, and Evidence and Custody. Printed tapes stood in for these channels at the table. Each tape served as a physical marker showing which department a participant wanted to consult.

### 6.2.4 Dream Sequence Rehearsal Materials

Two physical items granted access to the dream sequence, Detective Stoker’s auto-injector and a Somnoryl-μ vial. Placing both items into the Two-Slot board allowed participants to take the substance ([Figure X]). Entering the dream remained completely optional, since participants could investigate the homicide, question suspects, request institutional reports, and deliver a final accusation without it. It offered an extra investigative path for players who saw the drug, altered consciousness, or Elara’s state as relevant to their emerging theory.

Once the dream sequence began, the facilitator described what Detective Stoker saw. Participants could follow a light toward an entity that spoke cryptically about the truth. The scene offered a symbolic mode of interpretation that linked to Thorne’s note and hinted at a wider mystery, leaving direct proof of guilt to waking records.

That boundary mattered to the rehearsal design. Waking evidence stayed primarily indexical, offering material traces to support claims about presence, chronology, intoxication, motive, access, concealment, or contradiction. Dream material worked more ambiguously. It could suggest links between dependency, impaired consciousness, fear, parasomnia, manipulation, or broken memory, yet it could never confirm those ideas on its own.

### 6.2.5 Time Constraint and Deferred Evidence

Briefings informed participants that they had one afternoon to investigate the case, question suspects, request reports, and submit an accusation. This setup operationalized the Window of Opportunity, or *kairos*, system (4.2.4). Under this constraint, the timing of every choice carried weight, forcing players to decide whether to wait for a delayed report, chase a particular theory, question a suspect before confronting them, or stop gathering clues and commit to a charge.

Certain reports arrived immediately through their institutional channel, whereas others needed extra processing. Delayed materials included trace analysis of Elara’s injuries (E4-TRACE) and crime-pattern files on the wider robbery series (F1). After requesting a delayed document, participants had to choose between waiting, investigating elsewhere, or moving forward with what they already held.

This design addressed a core tension in ambiguous detective games. Players needed room to explore competing theories, yet unlimited time and open access to every file would erase any need to prioritize or act under uncertainty. Without a clock, players could keep pulling new reports without carefully reading the ones in front of them. The deadline pushed participants to weigh the value of chasing another lead and judge when their case was ready for Captain Miller.

Captain Miller reminded participants of the remaining time at intervals. Near the end of a session, a more intense noir-jazz track conveyed growing urgency without altering available evidence or imposing mechanical penalties.

### 6.2.6 Patience, Escalation, and Forced Confrontation

Alongside the afternoon deadline, the Patience System (4.2.3) set social limits on interrogation, turning conversation into a site of epistemic resistance.

Each role-played character carried a notional patience threshold. Participants could open with calm *Inquiry* to seek clarification or test a hypothesis, or escalate to *Confrontation* when challenging a lie, contradiction, or omission. Both modes worked when backed by relevant evidence, clear links between paired items, and a coherent investigative goal.

Patience dropped when participants pushed mismatched evidence, recycled irrelevant items after hitting a dead end, or tried to force an admission without an evidential argument. The system accommodated alternative interpretations and wrong theories, drawing a clear line between grounded inquiry and arbitrary accusation, repetitive pressure, or coercion.

Characters expressed this loss of patience through role-play with no numerical meter on display. As patience wore thin, a suspect grew defensive, evasive, frustrated, distressed, or silent. When a participant kept pressing an unsupported angle, Captain Miller could step in and send them back to review the evidence.

Stronger escalation carried sharper consequences. Participants could cite the revolver as evidence during questioning, yet using it to intimidate a suspect triggered an immediate forced confrontation. This rule prevented coercion from serving as a shortcut to information, forcing participants to present their case to Captain Miller on the spot with whatever evidence they had gathered. (Actions by occasional bystanders in the room did not count as game actions and never triggered this penalty.) Kae’s profile also included a misleading protective confession if participants built a sustained confrontation pinning responsibility on Elara (see 5.4.4).

## 6.3 Sessions and Participants

The early design rehearsal comprised 13 facilitator-led, low-fidelity role-playing sessions with 19 unique participants aged 20 to 26. Convenience sampling via direct messages and informal conversation arranged bookings around participant availability. Because sessions required no prior experience with detective games, tabletop role-play, or ambiguous fiction, participants arrived with varying familiarity with investigative play.

Sessions ran in everyday locations, including university classrooms, participant homes, and one bar, reflecting practical scheduling needs. Formats covered solo, duo, and trio play.

Two participants joined twice. Steph’s first session (S04) ended shortly after she entered the dream sequence, so she had seen little of the case before S13 two weeks later. Titi’s first session (S05) stopped early after limited reading, with S06 following four days later. Both follow-up sessions count as substantially fresh runs, though some prior familiarity cannot be ruled out, especially for Titi. Session codes also do not follow strict calendar order; S08 took place across 13 and 16 February in the logs, while S04 ran on 21 February.

Sessions were scheduled to run until conclusion, up to a limit of about six hours. Actual length varied with group size, how deeply players dug into the files, engagement with the dream, interrogation length, and whether a final confrontation occurred. Gui’s session (S08) stood out as an exception, stretching across three blocks on 13 and 16 February for roughly nine to ten hours, well past both the single-afternoon framing and the six-hour cap. Because exact start and finish times were missing from some logs, session duration serves as an approximate constraint.

## 6.4 Thematic Analysis

This section examines how participants engaged with the rehearsal and what their behavior implied for the digital prototype. Following the limits noted in 6.3, these themes serve as formative Research-through-Design insights for shaping the digital build.

### 6.4.1 Analytical Approach and Coding Process

Analysis of the rehearsal material tracked both the final verdicts and the paths participants took to reach them, covering how they approached the case, read the evidence, questioned suspects, and dealt with prototype constraints. A player who secured a favorable outcome might still have misunderstood a mechanic or leaned on a narrow line of proof. Conversely, someone whose session ended early could still display thoughtful theory-building, deep engagement with the dream sequence, or sharp critique of the system.

The analysis combined three qualitative data sources.

1. Detailed interaction logs produced during each session recorded evidence requests, departmental tape combinations, report acquisitions, delayed-report requests and waiting decisions, dialogue actions, uses of the Two-Slot interface, dream-sequence entry, stated theories, responses to time reminders, final accusations, and session outcomes.
2. Facilitator observation notes captured participant comments, moments of uncertainty, physical organization habits, emerging theories, emotional reactions, and moments when prototype limits shaped the session.
3. Ten anonymous post-session questionnaires ([Appendix X]) gathered participant reflections on system clarity, evidence use, perceived agency, dialogue, frustration, narrative immersion, outcome satisfaction, and suggested improvements.

Coding followed a hybrid deductive–inductive approach. Deductive categories grew out of the research questions and the rehearsed game systems, producing initial groupings for system comprehension, evidence interpretation, engagement, temporal decisions, and final resolution (Table 6.3). Repeated reading of the logs, observation notes, and questionnaires added inductive codes for physical organization, out-of-scope requests, exploit attempts, environmental exploration, meta-level comments, and differences between solo and group reasoning. Individual codes like *Aha Moment*, *Missed Clue*, *Dead End*, and *Red Herring* helped unpack specific session dynamics without standing as separate themes.

Six recurring themes emerged from the analysis, covering evidence as theory-building material (6.4.2), system legibility and investigative boundaries (6.4.3), dialogue as epistemic resistance (6.4.4), dream–awake interpretation and narrative immersion (6.4.5), collaborative and divergent practices (6.4.6), and *kairos*, uncertainty, and institutional judgment (6.4.7).

### 6.4.2 Theme 1: Evidence as Theory-Building Material

Across the sessions, participants actively turned reports, images, and physical objects into building blocks for provisional case theories. Evidence gained meaning when players compared documents, arranged items across the table, linked traces to suspect testimony, or used them to back an inquiry or confrontation.

Several participants built their own systems for externalizing the investigation. They grouped related reports, wrote evidence codes on notes, sorted files by suspect or clue type, and sketched informal spatial diagrams to compare explanations. These habits showed players trying to tame the growing file count by making links between traces visible.

Helen and Andrew (S09) offered a clear example of this externalization. They spread files across three rows of tables, sorted piles by perceived links, and stuck identifying notes onto individual sheets. Working through a Motive, Means, and Opportunity framework, they tried to weave ballistic trajectory, suspect profiles, witness accounts, drug records, and police reports into one account. They even sketched a diagram matching the downward bullet angle against the suspects' heights to turn a forensic detail into a causal claim about the shooter.

Other sessions showed similar spatial and note-taking habits. Rodri (S03) mapped out suspects and evidence in a chalkboard-style layout, while Julie and Steph (S13) split reading and note-taking before planning their theory (see Theme 5). Players relied on chronology, spatial sorting, notes, suspect comparisons, and spoken debate to keep the case manageable.

The request mechanic pushed participants to define specific questions before receiving extra documents. Those who used the institutional tapes with clear intent started from a question or hunch, pairing a departmental channel with an evidence object to pinpoint what needed closer inspection.

Jane and Mafs (S11) demonstrated this iterative cycle. After early questioning with sparse evidence fell flat, they went back to the files, ordered new medical and toxicological reports, and traced links between Elara’s condition, drug use, and the restricted Somnoryl-μ records. Their final charge, holding Elara responsible for manslaughter and Kae for obstructing evidence, grew out of repeated cycles of document review, questioning, and revision.

Building a theory meant revising ideas as new clues arrived. Several participants suspected Kae early on because of the gun, his criminal record, his robbery history, and his apparent lies to police. Later files often shifted their focus toward Elara’s bare feet, injuries, wet hoodie, drug use, and broken memory. Gui (S08) pulled extensive records on both suspects, gathering traces of Elara’s presence at the scene alongside Kae’s weapon history and confession tape, yet he settled on convicting Kae of murder. Access to the same evidence corpus led players to different endings based on which traces they prioritized and connected, matching the project's aim of constrained yet open interpretation.

Questionnaire answers echoed this pattern. Participants repeatedly picked moments when scattered details clicked together as their favorite part of the session. They noted how "simple and minor details" opened up wider links across the case, praising "the build up of the evidence but mostly receiving documents with more information" and "getting more information about previous reports" ([Appendix X]).

Not every player wove the files into a coherent narrative. Some fixated on a single clue like footwear impressions or the firearm without tying it to the broader case, while others chased red herrings or looked for external leads outside the rehearsal's scope (see Theme 2).

Lacking a built-in mind map never stopped participants from externalizing their reasoning, as the physical table invited sorting, note-taking, sketching, and discussion. For the digital prototype, the priority is keeping links between traces visible without forcing a single board layout or automating deduction. Players need to revisit reports, compare clues side by side, spot links they have already found, and ground dialogue directly in those pairings while still drawing their own conclusions.

### 6.4.3 Theme 2: System Legibility, Friction, and Investigative Boundaries

Unlike Themes 1, 3, 4, and 6, which examine whether the underlying design propositions succeeded, this theme examines whether the low-fidelity materials communicated those propositions clearly enough to be fairly tested.

Most participants grasped the basic premise of the Two-Slot Epistemic Interaction System, seeing that paired evidence could request new files, frame an inquiry, or back a confrontation. Still, grasping the concept rarely guaranteed smooth execution. Players often struggled to tell which items could pair together, which characters or channels remained open, what questions the rules allowed, and why an attempt returned nothing useful.

Several participants picked up the evidence channels quickly, matching departmental tapes to toxicology, medical, forensic, custody, and background requests. Even so, failed pairings left them guessing. Hearing flat responses like "None" or "Nope" failed to tell an invalid department request apart from a duplicate file, an unrelated pairing, or locked information. Without clearer signals, failed attempts started to feel arbitrary.

This ambiguity sharpened during suspect interrogations, shaped heavily by a mid-study rule change. Four early runs (S04, S09, S11, and S12) happened before dialogue limits were tightly defined. With loose boundaries between grounded questioning and open prompting, some players asked broad questions barely tied to the slotted cards. Steph (S04) repeatedly pressed Kae using E3 and E19, while Helen and Andrew (S09) asked questions detached from their selected evidence. Once formal dialogue boundaries took hold from S03 onward, participants stayed closer to the intended mechanic, though rejected prompts created more noticeable friction.

John and Fred’s session (S12), held before those limits settled, revealed the resulting clash in expectations. John wanted to build context step by step, whereas Fred pushed aggressive "bad cop" tactics and asked if he could bluff, a role-play move Rodri also tested in S03 (see Theme 3 for S12’s full escalation). Their frustration grew out of the gap between open-ended tabletop role-play and the strict logic of evidence-based questioning.

Participants regularly made requests outside the rehearsal’s scope. They asked to call Arthur Penhaligon’s wife, dial phone numbers from the victim’s wallet, trace the gun's serial number, pull financial records, re-interview the witness, check outside police files, or order extra forensic tests. Every request made sense from a detective's viewpoint, showing that players were inventing plausible leads from their theories. Yet these moments exposed how little the tabletop prototype could simulate or signal its real boundaries.

Physical friction also dulled system legibility. Some participants had to jot evidence codes onto sticky notes before bringing items into interrogation, and others forgot they could speak with Captain Miller because he had no portrait card. Questionnaire comments confirmed these gaps. One respondent wrote that "the rules were understandable but with the amount of information some aspects might end up being forgotten," admitting they "forgot we could interact with the Captain as there was not a picture as the rest of the characters" ([Appendix X]). Another called for "more visual support to some features, such as the intractable characters, so we can always check what characters we can interact with" ([Appendix X]). A third recalled mid-game confusion, noting, "During most of the session, I completely forgot about the Inquiry and Confrontation features," and recommending explicit "Buttons displaying Inquiry and Confrontation so users always know there's two options of dialogue" ([Appendix X]).

Prototype and facilitation slip-ups blurred the line between intentional ambiguity and accidental confusion. An early draft report mistakenly implied Elara worked at Arthur’s workplace, sparking an unintended motive theory, while S03 contained an "Arthur works on end of the week" file error. Other runs saw medical notes on Kae’s injection marks clash with signs of active drug use, a wrong image handed out instead of a report, Thorne’s note arriving late in S04, printed pages skipped in S01, and an unclear Luminaquel (E13) non-use window in S09. Three facilitation mistakes proved especially consequential. In S12, Kae failed to deliver his scripted confession during a valid confrontation; in S10, the facilitator nudged Lena toward L1 after hearing her dealer theory; and in S07, the facilitator accepted Kate and Chico’s sleepwalking theory from the dream–note link without demanding full waking proof or dismissing the thin argument. Those lapses reflect content-consistency and facilitation limits, showing nothing against participants' ability to handle ambiguity.

This theme separates *productive ambiguity* from *unproductive opacity*. Productive ambiguity occurred when reports supported several plausible theories and participants had to decide how to relate them. Unproductive opacity occurred when participants could not identify available actions, understand why a request failed, distinguish between *Inquiry* and *Confrontation*, or tell whether a contradiction originated in the fiction or in the prototype materials. The former supported epistemic agency; the latter risked turning reasoning into trial-and-error.

For the digital prototype, the interface needs to make affordances and feedback explicit while keeping interpretation open. Players should never have to memorize evidence codes, hunt for hidden characters, or guess why a pairing failed. The Two-Slot screen needs to separate available evidence, institutional channels, *Inquiry*, *Confrontation*, and final accusation visually. It should also supply clear failure messages, such as invalid relation, wrong department, or already obtained, alongside indicators of character availability and patience without dictating what any clue means.

### 6.4.4 Theme 3: Dialogue as Epistemic Resistance

The rehearsal treated dialogue as a site of epistemic resistance. Participants moved forward by grounding each question in one or two evidence items to express what the link between those traces implied, never by exhausting generic prompts. For instance, pairing Kae’s initial police interview with Elara’s employment verification let a player accuse Kae of lying about Elara needing to work the next morning, a claim born from comparing both documents side by side.

Participants found dialogue most rewarding when an NPC’s reaction followed directly from their own reasoning. One questionnaire respondent recalled confronting Kae over an inconsistency, writing that "Kae denied leaving his home, but showing a chain of evidence against it made him get more nervous. It felt satisfying" ([Appendix X]). Another echoed that "Catching them in a lie was rewarding and satisfactory as it resulted from our work speculating and the gathered evidence" ([Appendix X]). As S11 showed (Theme 1), players could also cycle back and forth between reading files and questioning suspects when broad early prompts fell short.

Dialogue also tested whether an evidential theory could hold up against a suspect’s self-protective story. Kae could deny involvement, point toward Elara, hide his weapon history, or release partial truths. Elara could reply with uncertainty, disorientation, denial, broken recall, emotional distance, or admissions about drug use and the dream. Each answer added fresh material to interpret, leaving the player to decide what it proved.

The Patience System (6.2.6) enforced this resistance by penalizing unsupported pressure and repetitive, ungrounded questioning while allowing wrong theories. Pressing a suspect repeatedly without solid evidence could bring Captain Miller in to halt the interrogation. In Gui’s session (S08), after he confronted Elara with E4, E4-TRACE, and Kae’s confession tape, Captain Miller stepped in to stop further pressure and sent Gui back to review the files.

John and Fred’s session (S12) showed what happened when coercion replaced evidential reasoning. Even after collecting substantial forensic, drug, and background records, the pair split on strategy. Fred pushed an aggressive "bad cop" style and asked about bluffing (see Theme 2), while John leaned toward contextual questions. At a breaking point, John mimed loading the spent bullet (E2) into the revolver (E1) and pointed it at Elara. That coercive stunt triggered the forced-confrontation rule, cutting the investigation short before a coherent case existed and ending in Detective Stoker’s dismissal.

Opaque resistance quickly bred frustration. Players grew annoyed when they laid down seemingly damning evidence only to hit a stonewall, as one respondent noted when writing that "sometimes this feeling was immediately shut down when the answer to the caught lie was 'I don't know' or 'I don't remember'" ([Appendix X]). Resistance feels meaningful only when players can tell why a character is pushing back.

Dialogue works best as an epistemic obstacle when three conditions hold. First, players need to form and express a clear intention grounded in evidence. Second, NPC replies must register that intention by denying, deflecting, revealing, or reacting emotionally to the pursued theory. Third, feedback has to be clear enough to tell a valid narrative block apart from a system failure. When those conditions held, dialogue helped participants test and revise theories; when they failed, players repeated cards, chased out-of-scope leads, or disengaged.

Kae’s false-confession path shows how complex this interpretive setup became. Built as an epistemic trap around loyalty, manipulation, and institutional storytelling (5.4.4), his protective confession gave players a tempting but misleading lead. Five sessions triggered this path (S02, S03, S08, S10, and S11), with only S12 missing it due to a facilitation error (see Theme 2). Of those five runs, three participants (S03, S08, and S10) went on to convict Kae, while players in S02 and S11 saw past the confession and held Elara responsible for manslaughter. The trap consistently succeeded in complicating institutional judgment.

In the digital prototype, dialogue should keep responding to links between paired items while making the tie between player intent, NPC pushback, and new disclosures more legible. Careful *Inquiry* should yield partial clarifications or fresh contradictions, whereas *Confrontation* should carry visible social risk and require stronger evidential backing.

### 6.4.5 Theme 4: Dream–Awake Interpretation and Narrative Immersion

The dream–awake loop extended the investigation beyond institutional documents and suspect testimony by introducing a parallel mode of inquiry grounded in symbolic association, altered memory, and uncertainty concerning consciousness. Because entering the dream sequence via the auto-injector and Somnoryl-μ vial (see 6.2.4) was voluntary, the rehearsal could observe whether participants approached it as a meaningful investigative opportunity, an act of curiosity, an emotional risk, or an unnecessary diversion.

Dream engagement varied substantially across sessions. Some participants entered the dream after pursuing pharmacological material surrounding Somnoryl-μ; others entered when Elara’s dialogue challenged Detective Stoker to experience the substance directly. In these cases, the dream became part of an ongoing theory about Elara’s memory, physical condition, and possible dissociation.

Jane and Mafs (S11) integrated dream and waking interpretation closely. Reviewing Elara’s medical files, toxicology, and Somnoryl-μ reports, they took Elara’s challenge out of curiosity. Combining physical traces with Jane’s own familiarity with sleepwalking, they formed a somnambulism theory even before finding the restricted document that named that possibility directly.

Kate and Chico (S07) made a unique leap after entering the dream, becoming the only pair to connect the dream entity to the previous detective’s note and ask about the earlier case. As noted in 6.4.3, however, the facilitator approved their sleepwalking theory without demanding full support from waking records. S07 shows both the associative reach of the dream–awake link and the risk of letting symbolic insight replace waking proof.

Timing also shaped how players used the dream. Ricky (S02) entered late in the run when Elara dared him during a final interrogation. The sequence reframed what he had gathered and helped shape his final charge against Elara for manslaughter and Kae for obstruction. Steph (S04), by contrast, took the drug out of frustration during stalled questioning. She found the dream gripping, yet struggled to tie it back to the murder or the case reports. Other players skipped the dream altogether or remained unsure whether its imagery represented evidence, metaphor, memory, or hallucination.

Questionnaire responses indicated that the dream sequence was often memorable. Participants described their most satisfying moments as "when I realized the game was more complex than a simple investigation, for example [sic] when i [sic] tried the drug," "taking the drug and confirming my theory that the girl was out of her mind during the murder," and when "the girl talked about the use of the drugs," which felt "like a strong lead" ([Appendix X]).

Such varied responses match the intended role of the Dream–Awake Epistemic Loop. Guaranteeing a better ending for entering the dream would turn symbolic inquiry into a mandatory gate. Operating as an optional interpretive lens, its value depended on how players connected symbolic traces back to waking files. Still, the rehearsals exposed a gap between story immersion and investigative legibility. Without clearer bridges back to waking actions, dream material risked feeling vivid yet detached from the core mechanics.

The digital prototype should keep dream material symbolic while preserving the line between poetic suggestion and institutional proof. Dream encounters should open fresh, non-conclusive lines of waking inquiry. Seeing imagery of dissociation or lost bodily control, for example, could prompt players to revisit Elara’s toxicology, medical intake, and foot-trace reports, unlocking new questions about memory and awareness without confirming sleepwalking or erasing guilt. When waking evidence spurs dream inquiry and dream imagery complicates waking files, players return to reports and dialogue with revised theories that support both immersion and epistemic agency.

### 6.4.6 Theme 5: Collaborative and Divergent Investigative Practices

While the rehearsal focused on testing evidence and dialogue mechanics, contrasting solo, duo, and trio runs revealed distinct play habits. Group sessions shared cognitive work and sparked joint insights, yet they also brought disagreement, clashing strategies, and lower individual autonomy.

Several pairs divided practical and mental tasks on their own. Julie and Steph (S13) split the files, with one person reading documents aloud while the other took notes, before planning a joint theory that led to a coherent manslaughter charge against Elara and a gun-possession charge against Kae. Helen and Andrew (S09) also split reading and note-taking while sorting evidence across tables (see Theme 1).

Collaboration also generated cross-domain insights. In S11, Jane’s personal familiarity with sleepwalking combined with Mafs’s engagement with detective-fiction conventions to support their somnambulism hypothesis (see Theme 4). In S07, Kate and Chico’s discussion produced the unique link between the dream entity and the previous detective’s note (see Theme 4), though their collaboration hit friction when Kate grew frustrated by Chico’s reluctance to investigate further. S12, John and Fred’s incompatible approaches undermined cooperation altogether, culminating in an early forced confrontation (see Theme 3).

Questionnaire answers showed that teamwork could also box in exploration. One participant remarked that "I don't see the point of having two people play at the same time. I felt like i [sic] couldn't explore my ideas before justifying them to the other person (and them agreeing to try them)" ([Appendix X]). Playing together spreads out reading and debate, yet waiting for consensus can stifle tentative ideas or let one player dominate.

Solo sessions gave individuals fuller control over pacing, file requests, and theory changes, though two solo runs still had bystanders nearby. In S08, a friend joined Gui midway through to weigh evidence and plan next moves. In S06, a friend in the room tried several interactions and grew frustrated before Titi tuned him out, continuing alone to deliver his own prepared confrontation. Among unassisted solo players, Lena (S10) started cautiously with little detective-game background, then grew confident requesting files and grounding dialogue before accusing Kae of murder. Ricky (S02) applied evidence steadily and shifted between Kae-focused and Elara-focused theories as new reports came in (see Theme 6). Familiarity with detective fiction, personal knowledge, risk tolerance, and prioritization shaped investigative style just as much as player count.

In the digital prototype, the core investigation must stay fully playable and clear for a single person. One player needs to pull reports, check dialogue history, sort traces, track open questions, and file a final accusation without help. Because side-by-side discussion can enrich interpretation, the screen should still stay legible enough for a second onlooker to follow contradictions without requiring agreement before the player acts.

### 6.4.7 Theme 6: Kairos, Uncertainty, and Institutional Judgment

The final theme looked at how participants turned incomplete, uncertain theories into an institutional accusation under time pressure. Presenting to Captain Miller demanded an epistemic commitment, not a standard end-of-level quiz answer. Players had to decide which clues they trusted, which doubts they could live with, and whether their case held up institutionally despite lingering contradictions. Working from the same files, they weighed blame differently, pointing to Kae’s gun access and lies, Elara’s physical traces and impaired state, or shared guilt pairing manslaughter with obstruction or weapon charges.

Because the single-afternoon deadline operationalized the Window of Opportunity (*kairos*) mechanic (4.2.4; 6.2.5), investigative actions carried opportunity costs. Reports such as `E4-TRACE` or the wider robbery crime-pattern file (`F1`) involved processing delays, requiring participants to decide whether waiting was worthwhile, whether to investigate elsewhere in the meantime, or whether to proceed with the material already in hand.

Sessions revealed sharp contrasts in how players handled delayed evidence. Gui (S08) ordered `L1` and waited for delivery (later combining it with Kae’s confession tape to unlock `F1`) while chasing other leads as `E4-TRACE` stayed pending. Ricky (S02) waited for `F1` without waiting for `L1`, then spent his remaining minutes near the close of the run on Elara’s dream challenge (see Theme 4). Titi (S06) ordered `E4-TRACE` yet moved ahead before it arrived, treating the skip as a deliberate trade-off on time. Julie and Steph (S13) asked Captain Miller for `F1` and held off on their final confrontation until it came through.

Time pressure bit hardest near the deadline, forcing action despite open doubts. Helen and Andrew (S09) saw the clock running out after extensive table sorting (see Theme 1), squeezed in one last interrogation, and were dismissed for a thin case, showing that investigative legwork differs from building a defensible charge. Rodri (S03) assembled strong evidence against Kae while still wondering about the barefoot figure and Elara’s injuries. As minutes ticked away, he chose to accuse Kae of murder while leaving those loose ends open. Even Gui (S08), whose session spanned two sittings (see 6.3), hit the closing window. Although he suspected Elara and held substantial evidence against her, running out of time to dig deeper led him to convict Kae.

Time pressure could also cause frustration. Reflecting on lingering questions about the barefoot figure and footprints, one questionnaire respondent wrote, "With maybe some more time these doubts could be explained. Could also be me overthinking a simple crime, thinking it's some complex plot twist" ([Appendix X]). Another pointed to their lowest moment as "Não conseguir ter uma resposta definitiva no fim e ter de encontrar mais relatórios em pânico" (not being able to reach a definitive answer at the end and having to find more reports in a panic) ([Appendix X]).

Player frustration does not automatically mean the Kairos mechanic failed. An investigation built on ambiguity cannot let players clear every contradiction for free without erasing prioritisation and interpretive responsibility. Applying Theme 2’s distinction (6.4.3), time pressure turns *productive* when players understand their options and choose between meaningful trade-offs. It turns *unproductive* when murky boundaries or prototype glitches burn the clock and make the deadline feel like arbitrary punishment. John and Fred’s forced dismissal (S12) falls outside a clean *kairos* outcome because fuzzy dialogue limits and Kae’s missing confession shaped that escalation (see Themes 2 and 3).

Judgment under uncertainty also carried an ethical edge. Doubts around Elara’s awareness and capacity, Kae’s concealment and protective lies, and the gap between physical action and legal guilt turned every accusation into an act of institutional responsibility.

For the digital prototype, the Window of Opportunity should operate as a transparent prioritisation structure, leaving opaque timers behind. Diegetic cues like institutional reminders, character availability, processing statuses, and shifting ambient audio should show players which reports are pending, which routes stay open, and when an action may close future options.

## 6.5 Summary of Results

The rehearsals mapped the conditions under which the proposed systems could support player-led interpretation, ahead of formal validation. Table 6.4 lists each theme’s core finding alongside the design change it implies for the digital prototype.

| Theme | Main finding | Implication for the digital prototype |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Evidence as theory-building material | Participants organised, compared, and revised evidence into provisional theories, and reached different verdicts from the same corpus. | Keep relations between evidence visible and revisitable, without automating deduction. |
| 2. System legibility and boundaries | The concept was understood, but unclear affordances, undefined early dialogue limits, and undifferentiated failure feedback turned some reasoning into trial-and-error. | Distinguish failure types (invalid relation, unavailable, already obtained) and show available characters and actions. |
| 3. Dialogue as epistemic resistance | Resistance was satisfying when its cause was legible, and frustrating when it wasn't; coercion led to consequences, not shortcuts, while Kae's protective confession often succeeded as an epistemic trap. | Make the link between evidence, question, and response consistent; keep *Confrontation* riskier than *Inquiry*. |
| 4. Dream–awake interpretation | The dream was memorable and sometimes reframed the case, but participants often lacked a route back to waking evidence. | Let dream material open new, non-conclusive questions in the waking investigation. |
| 5. Collaborative and divergent practices | Pairs shared cognitive labour and sometimes produced unique insights, but also conflict and reduced individual autonomy. | Keep the game fully playable solo, with reasoning visible enough to support co-located discussion. |
| 6. *Kairos* and institutional judgment | Deadlines forced commitment under doubt, even leading participants to convict Kae despite suspecting Elara, which felt meaningful when the action space was clear. | Make pending reports, remaining routes, and consequences of delay legible through diegetic cues. |

*Table 6.4. Summary of thematic findings and design implications for the digital prototype.*

The rehearsal findings showed where each system needed adjustment to support player-led interpretation. Subsequent revisions focused on four linked goals, covering tighter internal consistency in the evidence corpus, clearer links between evidence, departmental requests, dialogue, and player intent, broader narrative and spatial context for finding clues, and stronger tools for externalizing, revising, and communicating theories.

The low-fidelity rehearsal functioned as a formative Research-through-Design stage. It exposed successful interactions and surfaced design flaws before they could harden inside a digital build. The revised narrative and interaction structure presented in the next chapter grew directly out of this cycle of observation, correction, and iteration.
