# 6. Early Design Rehearsals

## 6.1 Protocol

The early design rehearsals consisted of facilitator-led, low-fidelity role-playing sessions developed to explore the proposed Two-Slot Epistemic Interaction System and the investigative experience it was intended to support. The rehearsals examined whether participants could interpret case evidence, construct provisional theories, and communicate investigative intentions by presenting one or two relevant evidence objects during questioning and confrontation. They also explored whether participants could use the dream sequence as part of developing and revising a case theory.

The sessions were not intended to test a finished digital interface or establish the final effectiveness of the proposed game systems. Instead, they functioned as an early rehearsal of the project’s narrative and interaction design before its translation into a digital prototype. The workshop therefore focused on participants’ investigative processes based on how they interpreted evidence, formed theories, requested additional information, tested assumptions through dialogue, responded to resistance from characters, and decided when they had sufficient grounds to make an accusation.

Each session began with a short briefing. Participants received an overview of the fictional case, their role as Detective Stoker, and the task of preparing a sufficiently supported accusation to present to Captain Miller. They were introduced to the Two-Slot Epistemic Interaction System through a tutorial sheet and verbal explanation. Participants were told that evidence should not be treated solely as material to collect, instead, they were encouraged to select one or two items that related to a question, contradiction, suspicion, or confrontation based on their interpretation of what they read and saw from the evidence.

Participants began the investigation with a limited set of starter reports and printed images (Image X). Further documents could be requested through the workshop’s simulated police evidence system as participants developed hypotheses or identified gaps in their understanding of the case. The full evidence corpus was therefore not available from the outset. This structure allowed the rehearsal to examine how participants decided what information to seek next, whether they could formulate requests from emerging theories, and how they responded when particular material was unavailable or delayed.

The investigation was framed diegetically as taking place within a single afternoon shift. Captain Miller informed participants that Detective Stoker had limited time to review the case, question suspects, request reports, and prepare an accusation. In practice, sessions lasted approximately four to six hours, depending on the number of participants, the extent of evidence exploration, the duration of interrogations, and whether participants reached a final confrontation.

Several parts of the proposed digital system were represented through facilitated role-play. The facilitator performed the roles of multiple characters such as "João", the evidence-locker assistant (acting as the game's police computer), Captain Miller, the precinct captain and supervisor of the investigation as well as both suspect Elara and Kaelen. Essentially the facilitator could provide any additional narrative details or interactions inside the story environment that were plausible within the constraints of the fictional setting those characters were a part of. 

For any request that was unavailable, premature, or outside the scope of the rehearsal, the facilitator responded in character and, where appropriate, directed participants towards available evidence channels or provided a plausible narrative explanation on why certain requests were unavailable.

Once the game started, participants could request to question either suspect. During interrogations, they were free to formulate their own questions, but were asked to ground inquiries and confrontations in one or two evidence objects. The selected evidence communicated the investigative relation the participant wished to explore — for example, a challenge to an alibi, a question about access to the weapon, a possible motive, or a contradiction between testimony and documentary material.

The role-played characters responded according to prepared character material detailing what each character knew, believed, concealed, and was willing to disclose. Their responses could include clarification, denial, evasion, emotional resistance, partial disclosure, or new information. Interrogations were also governed by a character’s internal patience threshold. Repeatedly presenting unrelated evidence, attempting to pressure a character without an adequately supported theory, or using coercive actions reduced the relevant character’s patience. When patience was exhausted, the interaction could be interrupted, redirected, terminated, or escalated into a forced final confrontation, depending on the character and the participant’s actions.

Certain requests involved delayed delivery, particularly reports requiring additional laboratory work, trace comparison, or extended institutional processing. Participants therefore had to decide whether to wait for potentially useful information, continue investigating available material, or proceed with the evidence already acquired. Captain Miller periodically reminded participants of the remaining time as the workshop approached its conclusion.

Sessions ended when participants considered their investigation sufficiently developed or when the available time elapsed. Participants then presented their theory to Captain Miller, identifying the suspect or suspects they considered responsible and explaining the evidence supporting that judgment. The outcome depended on the coherence, evidential support, and institutional sufficiency of the case presented, rather than on the discovery of a single inaccessible underlying truth.

Throughout each session, the facilitator recorded participant requests, evidence combinations, dialogue strategies, stated theories, responses to character interactions, and final accusations in structured interaction logs. These records were supplemented by observation notes. Following the session, participants completed an evaluation form (Appendix X) addressing the clarity of the Two-Slot system, perceived fairness, evidence use, dialogue, dream engagement, frustration, perceived agency, satisfaction with their conclusion, and suggested improvements.

## 6.2 Workshop Design & Elements

The early design rehearsal translated the project’s proposed detective-game systems into a low-fidelity, facilitated tabletop format where the workshop was designed to make its central interaction principles enactable:

- evidence could be interpreted in multiple ways;
    
- players could present one or two items to articulate an investigative intention;
    
- dialogue could resist unsupported pressure; symbolic dream material could complicate factual interpretation;
    
- time could make further investigation consequential.
    

The workshop combined a bounded fictional case, printed evidence materials, a physical two-slot interaction board, institutional request channels, role-played character encounters, and a final accusation. Together, these elements created an environment in which participants could form, test, revise, and defend theories about a homicide. The following sections describe the main design decisions and the role each element played within the rehearsal.

  

### 6.2.1 Case Setting, Characters, and Knowledge Distribution

The rehearsal was set in Oakhaven Central, 4th District Station, located in the Gallows Creek neighbourhood of the fictional city of Oakhaven. Restricting the case to a police station reduced the need for open-world exploration while retaining the institutional structure through which evidence could be requested, classified, interpreted, and converted into an accusation.

The station was represented through four connected spaces (Figure 38): the Evidence Room, the Interrogation Room, the Observation Room, and the Corridor. The Evidence Room contained the simulated police-computer system through which participants requested reports and examined case materials. The Interrogation Room was used to question the suspects, Elara Vance and Kaelen “Kae” Ward. The Observation Room represented the institutional perspective of Captain Miller and Officer “Buck” Buckley, and allowed participants to make requests unavailable through the evidence system. The Corridor connected these spaces within the fictional setting.

Participants assumed the role of Detective Julian Hammond Stoker, a recently appointed homicide detective. The scenario positioned the case as a professional test for Stoker: participants were required not simply to identify a suspect, but to formulate an interpretation that Captain Miller would consider sufficiently coherent, supported, and actionable. This distinction separated personal suspicion from institutional judgment. A participant might consider a character responsible, but still lack the evidence necessary to support an accusation.

A complete designer-facing case model was prepared before the rehearsals. This documented the chronology of the homicide, the suspects’ relationship, the history of the firearm, the circumstances of the arrest, the information known by each character, and the conditions under which characters could plausibly disclose, deny, conceal, or misunderstand information. This material enabled the facilitator to maintain consistency during role-play without directly confirming a single correct interpretation.

Participants did not receive access to the complete case model. Their investigation was limited to the participant-accessible case: the starter dossier, requested reports, physical evidence representations, institutional memoranda, dialogue responses, dream material, and the consequences of their own actions. This distinction was deliberate. The workshop was designed to explore whether participants could construct and defend a coherent interpretation from incomplete information, rather than reward access to a hidden definitive explanation.

  

### 6.2.3 Evidence Reports as Indexical Clues

A central design concern was the role evidence would play within the investigation. In many detective games, evidence functions as a confirmation mechanism, where the player obtains a clue, presents it at a predetermined moment, and receives the expected narrative progression. Although this model requires information gathering, the meaning of each item is often fixed in advance by the game’s authored solution.

The rehearsal approached evidence differently. Reports and objects were designed as indexical clues. A toxicology result could establish intoxication without determining intention. A witness statement could establish a person’s apparent presence without proving motive, causation, or guilt. A police memorandum could communicate procedural information while also revealing institutional preferences for a simple and prosecutable interpretation of the case. With the addition of red herring evidence, where players had to filter their relevance to the case or be influenced into perusing another theory.

The evidence corpus included forensic reports, toxicology results, witness statements, police memoranda, behavioural observations, medical documentation, and physical-evidence representations (Annex X). Each item could support several forms of reasoning. It might establish chronology, indicate presence, suggest impairment, expose a contradiction, imply a possible motive, or reveal how a police institution framed a suspect. Its significance emerged through the relations participants constructed between it and other available material.

This approach treated evidence as polysemic without implying that every interpretation was equally convincing. Participants’ theories remained constrained by the available material and by the need to justify their reasoning during dialogue and final accusation. The relevant investigative question was therefore not simply whether an item had been found, but what it implied when considered alongside other traces in the case.

Evidence consequently served both narrative and mechanical functions. Narratively, reports reconstructed a fragmented history of the crime and exposed the different perspectives of the people and institutions involved. Mechanically, they provided resources for forming questions, identifying contradictions, requesting further information, and communicating developing theories through a sequence of evidence combinations during dialogue all of them constrained to the Two-Slot Epistemic Interaction System.

  

### 6.2.4 Evidence Corpus and Institutional Requests

Participants began with a limited case folder establishing the basic conditions of the investigation. This included arrest reports for Elara Vance and Kae Ward, the homicide incident report, an official police memorandum, and the witness statement of Meredith Gable. They also received representations of relevant physical and visual evidence, including the firearm, fired projectile, wet hooded sweatshirt, Somnoryl-μ vials, prescription vial, victim’s wallet, footwear impressions, crime-scene photograph, surveillance image, suspect portraits, and the previous detective’s note.

These materials were designed to support immediate but competing interpretations. The firearm and Kae’s history could support a theory focused on weapon control, robbery, violence, or concealment. Elara’s condition, bare feet, wet clothing, and altered state could support interpretations concerning physical presence, intoxication, impaired agency, or unreliable memory. The witness account, footwear evidence, surveillance gap, and crime-scene material introduced uncertainty concerning timing, visibility, movement, and the reliability of environmental traces.

Additional reports were distributed through four institutional request channels: the Forensics Laboratory, Medical Unit, Controlled Substances Unit, and Evidence and Custody. In the tabletop workshop, these channels were represented by printed tapes. The tapes did not represent literal recordings; they functioned as physical indicators of the institutional department from which a participant wished to seek information.

### 6.2.5 Dream Sequence Rehearsal Materials

Dream access was represented through two physical evidence items: Detective Stoker’s auto-injector and a Somnoryl- vials. Participants could place these items together in the Two-Slot interface and choose to take the substance. This action did not function as a mandatory progression requirement. Participants could still investigate the homicide, question suspects, request institutional reports, and reach a final accusation without entering the dream. The dream sequence instead represented an optional investigative opportunity, available to participants who considered the substance, altered consciousness, or Elara’s condition relevant to their developing interpretation of the case.

When the dream sequence started, the facilitator provided the visual description of what the detective was seeing inside the dream. Followed by the player’s decision to follow the light, which results in a mysterious entity engaging in a mysterious dialogue about the truth.

Participants were not expected to treat the dream as direct evidence that could identify the murderer or establish legal responsibility. Instead, it was intended to introduce a different mode of interpretation hinting at a wider mystery by connecting it directly with Thorne’s note and other narrative details.

This distinction was important to the workshop design. Waking evidence was primarily indexical: it consisted of material traces that could support claims about presence, chronology, intoxication, motive, access, concealment, or contradiction. Dream material was more ambiguous. It could suggest a possible relation between dependency, impaired consciousness, fear, parasomnia, manipulation, or fragmented recollection, but it could not independently confirm any of these interpretations.

Its purpose was to test whether symbolic experience could become a meaningful part of theory formation without replacing the evidential and institutional demands of the waking case. In this way, the rehearsal explored whether participants could move from factual traces to symbolic suggestion and return to the investigation with a revised or expanded interpretation.

  

### 6.2.6 Time Constraint and Deferred Evidence

Participants were informed of the Time constraint and that they had one afternoon to investigate the case, question suspects, request reports, and submit an accusation. This temporal structure operationalised the project’s Window of Opportunity, or kairos, system. Time was not intended to function only as a uniform countdown. It created situations in which the timing of a decision mattered: whether to wait for a delayed report, continue pursuing a particular theory, question a suspect before confronting them, or stop gathering material and commit to an accusation.

Some reports could be accessed immediately through the relevant institutional channel, while others required further analysis or processing. These delayed materials included reports such as trace analysis related to Elara’s injuries (E4-TRACE) and crime-pattern information relating to the wider robbery series (F1). When participants requested delayed evidence, they had to decide whether to wait for it, continue investigating elsewhere, or proceed with the material already available.

The system addressed a central tension in ambiguous detective gameplay. Participants needed sufficient freedom to explore competing theories, but unrestricted time and unlimited information would remove the need to prioritize, commit, and act under uncertainty, since it could lead the participants into requesting exponential amount of reports without reading the ones that were given to them more carefully. The time constraint therefore required participants to evaluate the likely value of further investigation and decide when their theory was sufficiently developed for institutional presentation.

Captain Miller periodically reminded participants of the remaining time. As sessions approached their conclusion, a more intense noir-jazz soundtrack was introduced to signal the changing dramatic state of the investigation. The soundtrack did not alter the available evidence or mechanically penalize players; it supported the affective communication of urgency.

### 6.2.7 Patience, Escalation, and Forced Confrontation

Alongside the overall deadline, the rehearsal incorporated a Patience System to establish social limits on interrogation. The system was intended to prevent dialogue from becoming an unlimited source of information and to position conversation as a site of epistemic resistance. Suspects and institutional characters were not designed to respond indefinitely to repeated accusation, unsupported pressure, or coercive behaviour.

Each role-played character had a notional patience threshold. Participants could use calm inquiry to request clarification or test a developing theory, or confront the character of an inconsistency, accusation, or concealment. Neither approach was inherently preferable. Their effectiveness depended on the relevance of the evidence presented, the relation established between items where applicable, and the coherence of the participant’s investigative intention.

Patience decreased when participants repeatedly presented evidence that did not support the question being asked, continued to use irrelevant material after receiving no meaningful response, or attempted to force an outcome without articulating an evidential argument. The system did not punish alternative interpretations or incorrect theories. Instead, it distinguished evidence-based inquiry from arbitrary accusation, repetitive pressure, or coercive attempts to extract information.

This feedback was communicated through role-play rather than an explicit numerical meter. As patience diminished, a character could become defensive, evasive, frustrated, emotionally distressed, or unwilling to continue answering. Captain Miller could intervene when a participant pursued an unsupported line of pressure, requiring them to return to the available evidence and reconsider their approach.

The system also included stronger forms of escalation. The revolver could be used as evidence in an investigative discussion, but using it as an instrument of intimidation triggered a forced final confrontation. This ensured that coercion did not become an efficient shortcut to information. Participants had to present their case to Captain Miller using the evidence accumulated at that point.

Kae’s character design also allowed for the possibility of a misleading protective confession if participants constructed a sustained confrontation around Elara’s responsibility. This was designed as a narrative slip rather than an authoritative revelation of truth: it could redirect institutional responsibility while creating a further problem of credibility, motive, and interpretation.

The Patience System had three connected purposes. First, it made dialogue a limited social resource rather than an unlimited mechanism for extracting exposition. Second, it encouraged participants to prepare and communicate evidence-based theories before escalating to accusation. Third, it attached narrative and institutional consequences to aggressive or coercive conduct. In this way, the system supported the project’s broader argument that detective gameplay should reward the construction and testing of interpretations while requiring players to take responsibility for how they pursue knowledge.

  

## 6.3 Sessions & Participants (tabela demográfica, duração dos workshops, etc)

The early design rehearsal comprised thirteen low-fidelity, facilitator-led role-playing sessions conducted with 19 unique participants aged between 20 and 26 years over 13 different sessions. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling. The researcher contacted friends through direct messages and informal conversation, arranging workshops according to participant availability. No prior experience of detective games, role-playing games, or ambiguous narrative media was required. Consequently, participants entered the rehearsal with varied familiarity with detective fiction and investigative gameplay.

The workshops took place in everyday settings, including university classrooms, participant homes, and one bar. This variation resulted from practical circumstances and participant availability; the study was not designed to compare settings. Sessions were conducted in solo, duo, and trio formats.

Sessions were planned to until they had a finished case with a limit of up to 6 hours, although their actual duration varied with group size, the extent of evidence exploration, engagement with dream material, the length of interrogations, and whether participants reached a final confrontation. Since exact start and finish times were not recorded consistently, duration is treated as an approximate workshop constraint rather than as a comparative measure.

Not every participant completed the questionnaire, and because the forms were anonymous, responses cannot always be mapped confidently to a specific session or participant. In addition, the facilitator simultaneously performed the roles of game system, meaning that some interaction outcomes depended on facilitation judgment as well as on the prepared workshop rules. These limitations mean that the findings should not be interpreted as statistically generalisable. Instead, they provide qualitative design evidence about how participants encountered, understood, negotiated, and sometimes challenged the proposed investigative systems.

  

## 6.4 Thematic Analysis

This section analyses how participants encountered the low-fidelity detective-game rehearsal and how their interactions informed the subsequent development of the prototype. The analysis examines the processes through which participants interpreted evidence, requested information, organised case material, questioned suspects, entered dream sequences, revised theories, and ultimately committed to an accusation.

The themes presented below should not be understood as statistically generalisable claims about detective-game players. The workshop involved a small convenience sample, varied session formats, informal settings, and an evolving facilitated prototype. Instead, the analysis identifies recurring patterns that reveal how the proposed systems supported, complicated, or failed to support interpretive agency. The findings are therefore treated as Research-through-Design insights in order to be used for refinement and structuring of the later digital prototype rather than final validation of a completed game system.

### 6.4.1 Analytical Approach and Coding Process

The workshop material was analysed to understand not only which final outcomes participants reached, but also how they approached the investigation, interpreted evidence, interacted with the suspects, and responded to the constraints of the low-fidelity prototype. A participant who reached a favorable institutional outcome could still have misunderstood a mechanic or relied on a narrow evidential path, while a participant who was unable to complete the case could nevertheless demonstrate meaningful theory-building, engagement with the dream sequence, or productive criticism of the system.

The analysis drew upon the three complementary sources of qualitative material:

1. The first consisted of detailed interaction logs produced during each workshop session. These records documented evidence requests, departmental tape combinations, report acquisitions, delayed-report requests and waiting decisions, dialogue actions, uses of the Two-Slot interface, dream-sequence entry, stated theories, responses to time reminders, final accusations, and recorded session outcomes
    
2. The second source consisted of facilitator observation notes, including participant comments, moments of uncertainty, material organisation practices, emerging theories, emotional reactions, and instances in which prototype limitations influenced the session.
    
3. The third source consisted of ten anonymous post-session questionnaire responses. These responses provided participant reflections on system clarity, evidence use, perceived agency, dialogue, frustration, narrative immersion, satisfaction with final outcomes, and suggested improvements.
    

The analysis used a hybrid deductive–inductive thematic approach. The deductive component was informed by the research questions and the systems under rehearsal. Since the workshop was constructed to test evidence interpretation, evidence combination, interrogation, dream engagement, temporal pressure, and final judgment, the initial coding framework included categories such as System Understanding, System Confusion, Theory Building, Dialogue Strategy, Dream Engagement, Narrative Inference, Frustration, Temporal Pressure, Deferred Evidence Decision, Forced Commitment, and Ending Outcome. These codes enabled the analysis to identify whether the intended mechanics were understood and whether they supported the form of epistemic agency proposed by the project.

The inductive component developed through repeated engagement with the workshop material. During the review of interaction logs, observation notes, recordings, and questionnaire responses, it became clear that some relevant forms of participant behaviour were not adequately captured by the initial system-oriented categories. Additional codes were therefore used to describe material organisation, requests outside the intended scope of the prototype, attempts to circumvent the interaction rules, environmental exploration, meta-level comments about the workshop design, and differences between solo and collaborative reasoning. Codes including Physical Organization, Out-of-Scope Request, Exploit Attempt, Role Division, Cooperative Synergy, and Cooperative Conflict were particularly important because they revealed how participants adapted the prototype, interpreted its boundaries, and constructed theories together.

The thematic analysis developed six recurring patterns that connected participant behaviour to the project’s research questions. The first concerns the ways in which participants used evidence as material for constructing theories rather than merely collecting information. The second examines system legibility, friction, and the boundaries participants encountered when attempting to request reports or interrogate suspects. The third analyses dialogue as epistemic resistance, including the role of patience, confrontation, and institutional intervention. The fourth considers the relationship between dream engagement, symbolic interpretation, and narrative immersion. The fifth examines how solo and group formats produced different forms of collaborative reasoning and disagreement. Finally, the sixth considers how participants responded to uncertainty when required to transform an incomplete theory into an institutional accusation. Together, these themes provide the basis for the following analysis of how the rehearsal supported, complicated, and refined the proposed detective-game systems.

### 5.4.2 Theme 1 — Evidence as Theory-Building Material

A central pattern across the workshop sessions was that participants did not treat the evidence corpus only as a collection of documents to read or items to unlock. Instead, many participants actively transformed reports, images, and physical objects into material for constructing provisional theories about the case. Evidence became meaningful when participants compared it with other reports, arranged it spatially, connected it to suspect testimony, or used it to justify an inquiry or confrontation.

The physical workshop format made this process particularly visible. Participants were given access to reports, evidence cards, photographs, stationery, sticky notes, clips, and table space, but they were not provided with a pre-authored case board or a prescribed method for organising the case. Several participants nevertheless created their own systems for externalising the investigation. They placed related reports into groups, wrote evidence identifiers on notes, arranged materials according to suspect or evidential category, and created informal spatial diagrams to compare possible explanations. These practices indicated that participants were attempting to reduce the complexity of the evidence corpus by making relationships between traces visible.

The session involving Helen and Andrew (S09) offered a particularly clear example. They spread evidence across three rows of tables, created piles based on perceived relationships, and attached identifying notes to help them track individual documents. They also adopted the Motive, Means, and Opportunity framework while constructing their theory. Rather than accepting the reports as isolated fragments, they attempted to connect the ballistic trajectory, suspect characteristics, witness material, drug evidence, and police reports into a structured account of what may have occurred. Their use of a diagram to relate the downward bullet trajectory to the comparative height of the suspects demonstrated an attempt to convert forensic detail into a causal inference about the possible shooter.

Similar practices occurred in other sessions. Jane and Mafs (S11) began by closely examining the initial reports and establishing a timeline before committing to a suspect theory. Their attention to time, sequence, intoxication, and the conditions surrounding Elara's return to the apartment supported a later interpretation of impaired agency and possible sleepwalking. Rodri (S03) used the available materials to construct a chalkboard-style organisation of the suspects and evidence. Julie and Steph (S13) divided their activity between reading files and recording notes before developing a theory and preparing their final case. These examples show that theory building was not limited to a single preferred play style. Participants used different material strategies, such as chronology, spatial arrangement, note-taking, suspect comparison, and verbal discussion, to make the case manageable.

The evidence-request system also encouraged participants to formulate information needs rather than passively receive an exhaustive case file. Participants who used the institutional tapes purposefully often began with a question or emerging theory. A participant who suspected that Elara's physical condition was central to the case could request medical material relating to her injuries and toxicological state. A participant pursuing Kae's possible involvement could access his initial police interview, weapon record, prior criminal history, or later crime-pattern material. In these cases, the request itself formed part of the theory-building process, where selecting a departmental channel and an evidence object expressed what the participant believed required further investigation.

This process was visible in the more systematic sessions. Jane and Mafs (S11) initially attempted direct dialogue with limited evidence, but after receiving insufficient results they returned to the evidence corpus and requested additional reports. They then pursued connections between Elara's medical condition, toxicology, drug use, and the restricted information concerning Somnoryl-μ. Their final accusation held Elara responsible for manslaughter while identifying Kae's role in obstruction of evidence. The outcome was not produced by a single decisive document. It resulted from a progressive interpretation of Elara's physical presence, impaired state, memory gaps, and Kae's concealment of relevant information.

The workshop also showed that theory construction involved revision rather than only accumulation. Several participants began with an early suspicion of Kae because of the gun, his criminal record, his prior robbery behaviour, and his apparent lies to the police. As they acquired further material, some redirected attention towards Elara's bare feet, injuries, wet hoodie, drug use, and altered memory. Other participants retained their suspicion of Kae even after accessing evidence that could support Elara's involvement. For example, Gui (S08) obtained extensive material relevant to both suspects, including evidence associated with Elara's physical presence and Kae's confession route, but ultimately considered Kae's account more credible and convicted him for murder.

This variation is important. It indicates that the same broad evidence corpus did not mechanically lead all participants to the same conclusion. Participants assigned different relevance to physical traces, prior criminality, toxicology, witness testimony, emotional responses, and suspect behaviour. Their final theories reflected not only what information they accessed, but how they prioritised and connected that information. The findings therefore support the project's attempt to design evidence as a set of constrained but interpretively open traces.

Questionnaire responses reinforced this pattern. Participants frequently described their most satisfying moments as occasions when several pieces of evidence became connected, when an apparently minor report led to another document, or when they used a chain of material to expose a suspect's lie. One participant described the satisfaction of showing evidence that contradicted Kae's claim about Elara's work schedule. Another described the progressive discovery that "simple and minor details" could lead to further information and reveal wider connections within the case. A further respondent described the most satisfying moment as "the build up of the evidence but mostly receiving documents with more information," noting that they "really liked getting more information about previous reports" (Appendix X). These responses suggest that participants valued the process of assembling evidence into a theory, rather than only the final accusation.

However, the evidence corpus did not automatically produce coherent theory construction for every participant. Some participants focused heavily on a single clue, such as footwear impressions or the firearm, without relating it sufficiently to the wider case. Others pursued reports or external information that was unavailable within the scope of the prototype, such as contacting Arthur's wife, calling an insurance company, tracing the weapon's serial number, or obtaining financial records. These interactions revealed that the evidence materials successfully encouraged participants to think beyond the available reports, but they also exposed a mismatch between the investigative possibilities participants expected and the limited scope of the rehearsal.

The absence of a dedicated mind-map interface did not prevent participants from externalising their reasoning. Instead, the tabletop format enabled spontaneous forms of case construction through physical organisation, note-taking, diagrams, and collaborative discussion. This does not demonstrate that a visual case board would have no value in a future game. Rather, it suggests that the more important design requirement is not the presence of a particular interface, but whether the evidence system encourages players to establish, test, and revise relationships between traces. In the rehearsal, this activity emerged through the combination of material reports, institutional request channels, Two-Slot selection, and interrogation.

The main design implication for the digital prototype is that it should preserve the visibility of these relationships without reducing them to an automated deduction system. Players need to be able to revisit reports, compare evidence, recognise previously discovered relations, and understand why a particular combination may support a question or confrontation. At the same time, the system should avoid automatically resolving the meaning of those relations. The evidence interface should support the player's theory-building process while leaving responsibility for interpretation and final institutional judgment with the player.

### 5.4.3 Theme 2 — System Legibility, Friction, and Investigative Boundaries

Unlike Themes 1, 3, 4, and 6, which examine whether the underlying design propositions succeeded, this theme examines whether the low-fidelity materials communicated those propositions clearly enough to be fairly tested.

The workshop demonstrated that participants were generally able to understand the central premise of the Two-Slot Epistemic Interaction System: evidence could be combined to request further information, formulate an inquiry, or support a confrontation. However, understanding the overall concept did not always produce confidence in its detailed operation. Participants frequently encountered uncertainty concerning which objects could be combined, which characters or systems remained available for interaction, what forms of questioning were permitted, and why a particular attempt produced no meaningful response. This created an important distinction between the intended complexity of the investigation and friction caused by unclear system boundaries.

Several participants understood the evidence-request system quickly. They recognised that departmental tapes represented different institutional perspectives and that combining a tape with an evidence object could produce a report relevant to that object. Participants successfully requested toxicology, medical, forensic, custody, and background information through this system. In the interaction logs, these successful combinations often became the basis for more developed theories, particularly when participants pursued the medical and pharmacological material surrounding Elara's condition or the criminal and custody records associated with Kae.

Nevertheless, participants did not always understand why certain combinations were invalid or what made one approach more relevant than another. In some sessions, participants repeatedly attempted combinations that resulted in "None," "Nope," or equivalent negative feedback. While exploratory failure was intended to be part of the investigative process, the workshop revealed that feedback needed to distinguish more clearly between different types of failure. An invalid departmental request, a report that had already been obtained, a combination that lacked a meaningful relation, and a report that was unavailable at that stage of the investigation were not always communicated differently enough. As a result, some participants interpreted a failed combination as arbitrary rather than as information about the logic of the system.

This ambiguity was especially visible when participants moved from requesting reports to questioning suspects. The workshop allowed participants to formulate their own questions, but required those questions to be grounded in relevant evidence. Because the limits of this rule were not always fully defined, especially in the early sessions, some participants attempted to ask broad questions that were only loosely connected to the evidence placed in the slots. Others repeatedly presented a single document, such as a police report or the firearm, without developing a stronger evidential relation to support the pressure they were applying. In these situations, participants could experience the lack of an NPC response as a failure of the prototype rather than as feedback about the weakness of the theory being presented, while others exploited this design vulnerability to gather information without presenting the related evidence to their question.

The session involving John and Fred (S12) demonstrated this tension particularly clearly. Both participants quickly understood the evidence-request channels and actively pursued pharmacological, forensic, and contextual material. However, they adopted incompatible expectations for interrogation. One participant attempted to establish context gradually, while the other pursued immediate confrontations, improvised intimidation strategies, and asked whether bluffing or other role-playing techniques could be used. Because the dialogue boundaries had not been sufficiently formalised, the participants attempted actions that were plausible within tabletop role-playing conventions but outside the intended scope of the prototype. Their frustration was therefore not only a result of incorrect evidence use; it emerged from a mismatch between the open-ended expectations encouraged by role-play and the more bounded logic of the evidence-based interrogation system.

Requests outside the intended scope of the rehearsal were a recurring pattern. Participants asked whether they could contact Arthur Penhaligon's wife, call telephone numbers found in the victim's wallet, trace the firearm's serial number, obtain financial records, contact the witness again, inspect external police files, or access additional forensic material. These requests were often reasonable from the perspective of a detective investigation. They demonstrated that participants accepted the fictional world and treated the case as a space in which further investigative actions should be possible. At the same time, they revealed the limits of the low-fidelity prototype: the workshop could not simulate every plausible police procedure, and participants did not always know which actions had been designed as available affordances. These out-of-scope requests should not be interpreted simply as failures to follow instructions. They are evidence that the rehearsal generated a meaningful investigative mindset: participants were not only attempting to identify the designer's intended combination, but proposing additional investigative routes based on their developing theories. The digital prototype will need to make its boundaries clearer without reducing this sense of possibility.

Material and interface friction also affected participants' understanding of the system. In the physical rehearsal, some participants needed to write evidence identifiers on sticky notes before they could reference an item during interrogation. Others forgot that Captain Miller remained available as an interactable institutional character, partly because his presence was less visually represented than the suspects. Questionnaire responses similarly requested clearer visual indicators for available characters. One participant noted that "the rules were understandable but with the amount of information some aspects might end up being forgotten," explaining that they "forgot we could interact with the Captain as there was not a picture as the rest of the characters." Another requested "more visual support to some features, such as the interactable characters, so we can always check what characters we can interact with" (Appendix X). A third admitted mid-session confusion: "During most of the session, I completely forgot about the Inquiry and Confrontation features," suggesting the interface provide explicit "Buttons displaying Inquiry and Confrontation so users always know there's two options of dialogue" (Appendix X).

These observations identify problems with the representation of the system, rather than problems with the underlying design proposition. The workshop provided evidence that participants could understand evidence combination and derive satisfaction from using evidence to obtain further information. However, the material prototype did not always communicate its possible actions, interaction states, and consequences clearly enough. In a digital implementation, the player should not need to memorise evidence codes, remember hidden available characters, or infer whether a failed request reflects an invalid relation, unavailable information, or a report that has already been acquired.

Prototype errors further complicated the distinction between intended ambiguity and unintended confusion. In one early session, a report incorrectly implied that Elara worked at the same location as Arthur, encouraging a false motive theory that was not part of the designed case. In another session, medical information concerning Kae's injection marks appeared inconsistent with evidence of active drug use, producing confusion about his physical state. A further session received an incorrect image file where a different report was expected, while in another relevant confrontation Kae did not produce the prepared confession response. These incidents must be acknowledged because they influenced participant theories and outcomes. They should be treated as rehearsal findings about the need for content consistency and state-management reliability, not as evidence that participants were unable to reason with ambiguity.

The theme therefore distinguishes between productive ambiguity and unproductive opacity. Productive ambiguity occurred when reports supported several plausible theories and participants had to decide how to relate them. Unproductive opacity occurred when participants could not identify available actions, understand why a request failed, distinguish between inquiry and confrontation, or determine whether a contradiction originated in the fiction or in the prototype materials. The former supported epistemic agency; the latter risked turning reasoning into trial-and-error.

The main implication for the digital prototype is that it should make the system's affordances and feedback more explicit while preserving interpretive openness. The Two-Slot interface should visibly distinguish between available evidence, institutional request channels, inquiry, confrontation, and final accusation. Failed combinations should provide contextual feedback, such as indicating that the selected objects do not establish a relevant relation, that further information is unavailable through the selected department, or that the report has already been obtained. Such feedback should clarify the operational rules without telling players what a clue ultimately means or which theory they should pursue. Similarly, the digital dialogue system should clearly communicate that an inquiry requires evidence-based relevance and that confrontation represents a higher-stakes escalation. The player should be able to recognise which NPCs are currently available, what evidence they have already used, and whether their current line of questioning is approaching a social limit. These improvements would preserve the workshop's central finding: players were willing to investigate actively and think beyond the immediately available content, but they required a legible system that could distinguish meaningful investigative uncertainty from avoidable interface confusion.

  

### 5.4.4 Theme 3 — Dialogue as Epistemic Resistance

The workshop treated dialogue not as a neutral delivery mechanism for information, but as a site of epistemic resistance. Participants could not progress simply by asking every available question or presenting evidence indiscriminately. Instead, they were expected to use reports, physical evidence, and prior testimony to formulate an investigative intention: clarifying an inconsistency, questioning a suspect's memory, challenging an alibi, establishing access to the weapon, or testing a theory of responsibility. The quality and relevance of this evidential relation shaped how the role-played NPCs responded.

This approach distinguished the rehearsal from conventional dialogue structures in which players select pre-authored lines such as "accuse," "press," or "ask about evidence." Participants were free to articulate their own questions, but they were required to ground these questions in one or two relevant evidence objects. The evidence combination therefore operated as an attempt to communicate what the participant believed the relationship between two traces implied. A participant presenting Kae's initial police interview alongside Elara's employment verification, for example, could argue that Kae had lied about Elara needing to work the following morning. The relevant interaction was not simply the possession of either report, but the accusation that emerged from their relation.

Several workshop sessions indicated that participants found dialogue satisfying when the role-played response appeared to follow from reasoning they had constructed. In the questionnaire responses, participants described positive moments when they used evidence to identify a contradiction, observe a suspect becoming nervous, or pressure Kae over an inconsistency in his account. One participant described confronting Kae with evidence that contradicted his statement about Elara's work schedule and experiencing the exchange as rewarding because it resulted from their own speculation and evidence gathering: "Kae denied leaving his home, but showing a chain of evidence against it made him get more nervous. It felt satisfying" (Appendix X). Another described the successful presentation of an evidence chain as satisfying because it made the suspect's denial appear weaker. A third recalled catching the boyfriend in a lie about the girlfriend's schedule: "Catching them in a lie was rewarding and satisfactory as it resulted from our work speculating and the gathered evidence" (Appendix X).

The interaction logs provide similar examples. Jane and Mafs (S11) initially approached dialogue with individual reports and broad questions, but recognised that this did not produce sufficient information. They then returned to the evidence corpus, acquired further medical and pharmacological reports, and used the resulting material to construct more focused lines of questioning. Their later use of evidence relating to Elara's physical traces and Somnoryl-μ prompted Kae to react protectively towards Elara and provided further information relevant to their final theory. Their final outcome, where they held Elara responsible for manslaughter while identifying Kae's obstruction of evidence, was therefore developed through repeated cycles of evidence review, dialogue, and revision rather than through one decisive confrontation.

Dialogue also enabled participants to test whether an evidential theory could survive contact with a character's self-protective account. Kae could deny involvement, redirect attention towards Elara, conceal his weapon history, or offer partial information that complicated the player's understanding of his motivations. Elara could respond through uncertainty, disorientation, denial, altered recollection, emotional detachment, or disclosure related to her drug use and dream experience. These reactions were designed to ensure that evidence did not immediately resolve the case. Instead, a response could become new material for interpretation, requiring participants to assess whether a denial was credible, whether a contradiction indicated guilt, and whether an emotionally distressed response should be interpreted as manipulation, fear, impairment, or incomplete memory.

The Patience System was central to this form of resistance. Participants could use calm inquiry to gather information or escalate to confrontation when they believed they had sufficient evidence. However, repeatedly pressing a suspect with unrelated evidence, attempting to force a response without an argument, or relying on intimidation reduced the character's patience. The system was not designed to penalise incorrect theories; participants could construct theories that ultimately differed from the facilitator's case model. Instead, it was designed to penalise unsupported pressure and repeated interaction that did not demonstrate a meaningful relation between evidence, question, and character.

Captain Miller functioned as an institutional mediator within this system. When participants repeatedly pressured a suspect without sufficient evidential grounding, the Captain could intervene and suspend the interrogation, requiring them to reconsider the available material before continuing. This occurred in one session, Gui (S08), where repeated confrontation of Elara, who was already devastated by his decision to present to her Kae's confession tape, did not produce a coherent investigative direction. The intervention redirected the participant from unproductive confrontation towards renewed evidence review. This was significant because it demonstrated the intended operation of the system: dialogue resistance did not merely block progress, but redirected the participant towards theory construction.

The session involving John and Fred (S12) revealed the limits of this system in its low-fidelity form. Both participants displayed strong interest in the investigation and requested substantial amounts of forensic, pharmacological, and contextual material. However, they adopted incompatible dialogue approaches. John tended to prepare questions and establish contextual information, while Fred pursued aggressive confrontation, suggested interrogation techniques outside the intended system, and eventually used the revolver as a tool of intimidation. The use of the firearm triggered the forced-confrontation condition. Rather than functioning as an effective shortcut to information, this action brought the investigation immediately to a final institutional judgment before the participants had constructed a sufficiently coherent case. The result was Detective Stoker's dismissal.

This outcome demonstrated an important design principle: coercion was not treated as an optimal investigative strategy. The workshop did not reward the participant for increasing pressure through the physical threat of the gun. Instead, it transformed that action into an institutional consequence, requiring the participant to take responsibility for the case they had assembled. This connected the social mechanics of interrogation to the broader concern of epistemic agency. Players were given freedom to construct and express theories, but they were also required to consider the consequences of how those theories were communicated.

At the same time, the rehearsal showed that dialogue resistance could become frustrating when the system's rules were not sufficiently legible. Some participants interpreted an unproductive response as evidence that a suspect was withholding information, while others interpreted it as a failure of the prototype or an indication that they had selected the wrong evidence. Participants were particularly frustrated when they believed they had presented incriminating material but received no new information in return. One participant described this as the answer to a caught lie being immediately shut down: "sometimes this feeling was immediately shut down when the answer to the caught lie was 'I don't know' or 'I don't remember'" (Appendix X). This distinction is important: resistance becomes meaningful only when the participant can understand why the character resists. If the relation between the evidence combination, the question, and the response remains opaque, dialogue may feel arbitrary rather than strategically difficult.

The workshop therefore suggests that dialogue can function as an epistemic obstacle when three conditions are present. First, participants must be able to form and communicate a recognisable evidence-based intention. Second, NPC responses must register that intention by denying, redirecting, revealing, or emotionally reacting to the theory being pursued. Third, participants must receive sufficiently clear feedback to distinguish a productive obstacle from an unintelligible system failure. When these conditions were met, dialogue became a process through which participants tested and revised theories. When they were absent, participants either repeated evidence, moved towards out-of-scope actions, or disengaged from the intended investigative logic.

The intended false-confession path for Kae illustrates a further possibility of this approach. The character architecture allowed Kae to make a false confession if participants constructed a sufficiently strong theory that positioned Elara as responsible for the murder. This confession was not intended to resolve the case as factual proof. Instead, it was designed as a new epistemic problem: Kae's attempt to protect Elara could be interpreted as guilt, loyalty, manipulation, self-sacrifice, or an attempt to control the institutional narrative. Although this specific path was not successfully triggered during the recorded sessions, it remains relevant as a designed affordance later implemented through the Logic Chain system. It should therefore be understood as part of the workshop design rather than as an observed result.

The main implication for the digital prototype is that dialogue should continue to respond to relationships between evidence rather than to isolated item presentation. However, the digital Logic Chain system must make the connection between player intention, NPC resistance, and resulting information more consistent and legible. Inquiry should reward careful theory development through partial clarification or new contradictions. Confrontation should carry greater social risk and require stronger evidence support. Patience should operate as a visible but diegetically meaningful constraint, ensuring that players cannot substitute repetition, arbitrary accusation, or coercion for investigative reasoning.

### 5.4.5 Theme 4 — Dream–Awake Interpretation and Narrative Immersion

The dream–awake loop was designed to extend the investigation beyond institutional documents, physical traces, and suspect testimony. While the waking investigation provided police reports, forensic material, toxicology results, and interrogation responses, the dream sequence introduced a parallel mode of inquiry based on symbolic association, altered memory, emotional experience, and uncertainty concerning consciousness. The workshop explored whether participants could treat dream material as meaningful investigative input without mistaking it for direct factual proof.

Participants accessed the dream sequence by combining the auto-injector with the Somnoryl-μ vial. This action was optional. Participants could complete the investigation without taking the drug, but doing so enabled access to a symbolic encounter connected to Elara's altered state, the history of Somnoryl-μ, and the wider dream-related mystery. The voluntary nature of this action was important. It allowed the workshop to observe whether participants considered the dream sequence a meaningful investigative opportunity, an act of curiosity, an emotional risk, or an unnecessary diversion from the institutional case.

Dream engagement varied substantially across sessions. Some participants entered the dream sequence because they had pursued the pharmacological material surrounding Somnoryl-μ and wanted to understand the drug's effects more deeply. Others entered after Elara's dialogue response challenged Detective Stoker to experience the substance directly. In these cases, the dream was not treated merely as an atmospheric reward. It became part of an ongoing theory about Elara's memory, her physical condition, her relationship to the crime, and the possible effects of intoxication or dissociation.

Jane and Mafs (S11) demonstrated one of the strongest integrations of dream and waking interpretation. Their investigation combined Elara's medical condition, the toxicology evidence, the Somnoryl-μ reports, and the restricted substance material. After encountering the information connected to the drug, they accepted Elara's challenge and entered the dream sequence out of curiosity rather than frustration. Their interpretation of Elara's impaired agency was also informed by the relationship between her physical traces, altered recollection, drug exposure, and the possibility of sleepwalking. Jane's personal familiarity with sleepwalking contributed to their discussion, allowing the pair to construct a somnambulism theory even before accessing the document that made this possibility more explicit.

Kate and Chico (S07) provided another important example. They entered the dream sequence and were the only group to connect the dream entity to the note left by the previous detective. This connection led them to request further information concerning the note and the earlier investigation. Their final case did not rest solely on the dream material; they still used the waking evidence to argue that Elara had acted while impaired. However, the dream sequence expanded their interpretation of the case beyond a simple question of physical guilt. It encouraged them to consider the relationship between Elara's altered state, the detective's own position, and the wider hidden structure implied by Somnoryl-μ.

For other participants, the dream sequence created engagement without immediately producing a usable interpretation. Steph (S04) entered the dream after becoming frustrated with an unproductive line of questioning. She described the dream narrative as compelling, but struggled to determine what it meant for the murder case or how it could be connected to the available reports. This outcome does not indicate that dream engagement failed. It shows that symbolic material can attract attention and generate narrative immersion while still requiring stronger bridges back to the evidential logic of the waking investigation.

Questionnaire responses similarly suggest that the dream sequence was often memorable. One participant described the most satisfying moment as "when I realized the game was more complex than a simple investigation, for exemple when i tried the drug" (Appendix X). Another identified "taking the drug and confirming my theory that the girl was out of her mind during the murder" as the single most satisfying moment of their investigation (Appendix X). A third described the moment when "the girl talked about the use of the drugs" as feeling "like a strong lead" (Appendix X). These responses indicate that the dream sequence contributed to the sense that the narrative world extended beyond the immediately available police reports and suspect testimonies.

However, the workshop also revealed that dream material did not automatically become part of every participant's theory. Helen and Andrew (S09) did not pursue the pharmacological chain deeply enough to develop a dream-related interpretation. Gui (S08) acquired extensive evidence relevant to both suspects but chose not to enter the dream sequence, ultimately focusing instead on Kae's weapon access, criminal history, and confession-related material. Other participants entered the dream but remained uncertain about whether its contents should be understood as evidence, metaphor, memory, hallucination, or information about the wider hidden narrative.

This variation was consistent with the intended role of the Dream–Awake Epistemic Loop. The dream sequence was not designed to provide an obligatory hidden answer or a superior route to the correct conclusion. If entering the dream guaranteed a better outcome, it would reduce symbolic investigation to another progression gate. Instead, the dream was intended to offer an alternative interpretive lens. It could make participants reconsider the reliability of memory, the meaning of Elara's behaviour, the ethical implications of attributing guilt, or the limits of institutional evidence. Its value depended on how participants related symbolic traces to the waking evidence corpus.

The workshop therefore revealed a productive tension between narrative immersion and investigative legibility. Dream material created curiosity, emotional intensity, and a sense that the fictional world contained meanings beyond the immediate crime. Participants often responded to this material as an invitation to think more deeply about the substance, the suspects, and the previous detective's case. At the same time, the symbolic nature of the sequence meant that participants sometimes lacked a clear method for translating dream content into a subsequent waking action. Without such a bridge, the dream could remain narratively compelling but mechanically disconnected.

The design implication is not that dream material should become more explicit or should reveal factual answers directly. Doing so would undermine the intended distinction between symbolic suggestion and institutional proof. Instead, the digital prototype should provide clearer opportunities for players to bring dream-derived concerns back into the waking investigation. A dream image or dialogue fragment could activate a new line of inquiry, make a previously ignored report appear more relevant, alter the interpretive context of a toxicology result, or unlock a specific but non-conclusive question for Elara or Kae.

For example, after encountering symbolic material associated with dissociation or loss of bodily control, the player could be encouraged to reconsider Elara's toxicology report, medical intake, and the trace evidence found on her feet. The system should not state that these materials prove sleepwalking or remove responsibility from Elara. Instead, it should allow the player to formulate a new question concerning memory, awareness, intention, or capacity. In this way, dream material would remain interpretively open while becoming more visibly connected to the evidence-based dialogue system.

The findings suggest that the dream–awake loop can support narrative immersion and epistemic agency when it operates as a reciprocal process. Waking evidence motivates the pursuit of dream knowledge; dream material complicates the interpretation of waking evidence; and the participant returns to reports and dialogue with a revised theory. The low-fidelity rehearsal showed that some participants were able to perform this movement successfully, while others required clearer affordances to understand how symbolic experience could become an actionable investigative question.

### 5.4.6 Theme 5 — Collaborative and Divergent Investigative Practices

Although the workshop was designed primarily to rehearse the evidence and dialogue systems rather than to compare individual and group play, the variation between solo, duo, and trio sessions produced a significant pattern. Participants did not investigate in a uniform manner. Some approached the case through careful individual reading and gradual theory revision, while others distributed the investigative work across multiple people. Collaborative sessions could generate productive exchanges in which one participant's observation enabled another participant's inference. However, they could also create disagreement, reduced individual autonomy, and competing interrogation strategies.

In several duo sessions, participants spontaneously divided the practical and cognitive labour of investigation. Rather than both performing the same task, one participant often read a report aloud while the other took notes, organised materials, searched for a relevant clue, or questioned an emerging interpretation. This was particularly visible in the session involving Julie and Steph (S13). They worked across from one another, with one participant reading evidence files while the other recorded notes and maintained a developing overview of the case. Their division of activity supported the construction of a coherent theory and contributed to their final conclusion that held Elara responsible for manslaughter and Kae responsible for gun possession.

Helen and Andrew (S09) similarly transformed the available physical materials into a collaborative investigation space. They spread reports across several tables, created groups of documents based on perceived connections, labelled material with identifying notes, and used diagrams to examine the ballistic trajectory in relation to the suspects. Their work was not simply parallel reading. The process involved recurring discussion of Motive, Means, and Opportunity, shared attention to the timeline, and the collective construction of possible relationships between the gun, bullet angle, physical evidence, and suspect characteristics.

The strongest examples of collaboration involved what was coded as Cooperative Synergy: moments where one participant contributed an observation that another developed into a more substantial inference. Jane and Mafs (S11) provide a clear case. Their investigation combined close reading of reports, attention to temporal sequence, and discussion of Elara's condition. Jane drew on personal knowledge of sleepwalking, while Mafalda's engagement with detective-fiction conventions helped develop the possibility that Elara had acted in a somnambulant or dissociative state. Together, they arrived at this theory without first accessing the restricted document that explicitly developed the relationship between Somnoryl-μ, altered consciousness, and dream-related impairment.

Kate and Chico (S07) also produced an insight that neither participant appeared to develop independently. After entering the dream sequence, they connected the dream entity to the note left by the previous detective. This connection led them to investigate the previous case more closely and helped them interpret the dream material as part of the wider narrative structure rather than as an isolated surreal event. Their collaboration did not merely make the investigation faster. It enabled a cross-domain relation between dream symbolism, documentary evidence, and the detective's history that did not occur in the other recorded sessions.

Collaborative play did not always improve the investigation. The session involving John and Fred (S12) demonstrated how different investigative expectations could become a source of conflict. John tended to gather contextual information and prepare questions before escalating. Fred more frequently pursued immediate confrontations, suggested improvised interrogation strategies, and attempted to create pressure through the revolver. Their approaches did not converge into a shared theory. Instead, the different methods repeatedly competed for control of the interrogation and contributed to frustration, unclear dialogue use, and a final forced confrontation before a coherent case had been prepared.

Questionnaire feedback also revealed that collaboration could limit individual exploration. One participant in a group session stated that they did not see the value of having two people play simultaneously because they felt unable to pursue their own ideas without first explaining them to the other participant and obtaining agreement: "I don't see the point of having two people play at the same time. I felt like i couldn't explore my ideas before justifying them to the other person (and them agreeing to try them)" (Appendix X). This response is important because it complicates the assumption that more participants necessarily produce better theory building. Shared investigation can encourage discussion and distribute cognitive labour, but it can also require negotiation before action, suppress tentative hypotheses, or cause one participant's preferred strategy to dominate the group.

Solo sessions produced a different form of investigative practice. Individual participants had greater control over evidence requests, interrogation pacing, theory revision, and final judgment. In some cases, this supported sustained and methodical engagement. Gui (S08), for example, accessed a large portion of the evidence corpus, organised material through notes, requested further reports, and pursued an extended interrogation path before ultimately convicting Kae. His final theory remained distinct from the interpretations reached by other participants, even though he had access to evidence that could also support Elara's involvement.

Other solo participants showed that individual control did not eliminate uncertainty or friction. Lena (S10) initially appeared cautious about using the system and had limited familiarity with detective-game conventions, but gradually became more independent in requesting evidence and communicating intentions through dialogue. Her final case against Kae emerged from this gradual learning process, although she did not fully pursue the evidence related to Elara's physical presence. Ricky (S02) also investigated independently and used evidence consistently during questioning, yet moved between Kae-focused and Elara-focused theories as new reports complicated his existing interpretation.

These examples indicate that divergent investigative practices were not simply determined by player number. They were shaped by prior familiarity with detective media, confidence in using the evidence system, willingness to take risks, personal knowledge brought into the session, available time, and the particular reports each participant chose to prioritise. Group format amplified these differences by making them visible through discussion, labour division, agreement, and conflict.

The design implication for the digital prototype is that the core investigation must remain fully playable and intelligible for one person. The formal game system should not require a second participant to remember evidence, identify contradictions, organise material, or challenge an emerging theory. One player must be able to access reports, review dialogue history, organise information, retain questions, compare evidence, construct a theory, and submit a final accusation without external assistance. This is necessary because the intended prototype remains structurally a single-player detective experience: one player controls the detective, selects evidence, initiates dialogue, and takes responsibility for the final institutional judgment.

However, the workshop also suggests that single-player control does not prevent collaborative interpretation. In several duo sessions, participants spontaneously shared the cognitive labour of investigation. They read reports aloud, took notes, organised evidence, proposed competing explanations, and developed inferences through discussion. In some cases, this produced insights that did not appear to emerge from either participant independently. The collaborative connection between dream material and the previous detective's note, for example, demonstrates how discussion can support cross-domain interpretation between symbolic experience, documentary evidence, and the wider narrative structure. The digital prototype should therefore not be understood as exclusively solitary in use, even if it contains no formal multiplayer system. Rather than implementing a separate cooperative mode at this stage, the design should remain compatible with co-located shared investigation. The interface should make evidence, report contents, dialogue history, discovered relations, pending questions, and unresolved contradictions sufficiently visible for a second person to follow and discuss. A player who shares the experience with another person should be able to pause, revisit material, compare reports, explain a proposed connection, and negotiate a possible theory without the system prematurely resolving that interpretation for them.

The workshop also demonstrates that collaborative play requires caution. Discussion can support theory construction, but it can also delay action, restrict one participant's willingness to pursue uncertain ideas, produce competing investigative priorities, or result in one person dominating the interaction. The digital prototype should therefore preserve the formal player's ability to act independently while allowing evidence and reasoning to remain externally legible. The objective is not to design a system in which two players must agree before progress can occur, but to create an investigation that can be independently played and meaningfully discussed. This distinction should be examined in later digital playtesting, where individual sessions could be compared with co-located shared sessions in which one participant controls the game while both participants are encouraged to read, discuss, organise, and interpret the available traces.

### 5.4.7 Theme 6 — Kairos, Uncertainty and Institutional Judgment

The final recurring theme concerned the relationship between uncertainty, temporal constraint, and institutional judgment. The rehearsal did not ask participants simply to identify the factual truth of the homicide. Instead, it required them to decide whether the evidence they had gathered was sufficient to accuse one or both suspects within the institutional setting of Oakhaven Central. Participants were not given complete access to the underlying case model, and they were not expected to resolve every contradiction before acting. They were required to transform a developing interpretation into an accusation that could be evaluated by Captain Miller.

This requirement was central to the workshop design. The final confrontation was intended to represent an epistemic commitment rather than a conventional end-of-level answer. Participants had to decide which evidence they considered reliable, which ambiguities they could accept, whether further investigation was likely to alter their conclusion, and whether their existing theory could be defended as institutionally sufficient. The distinction between complete factual truth and a sufficiently supported accusation was therefore important. A participant could construct a plausible and defensible case without obtaining every available report, while another participant could continue gathering material until the available investigative opportunity had passed without ever presenting a coherent accusation.

The variation in outcomes indicates that participants were not merely following a single hidden route through the evidence. Some gave priority to the firearm, Kae's criminal history, his apparent dishonesty, and his connection to the robbery pattern. Others gave greater significance to Elara's bare feet, the injuries documented during arrest, the wet hoodie, her toxicology results, her fragmented memory, and the trace evidence linking her injuries to the crime-scene environment. Some participants attempted to formulate shared responsibility, interpreting Elara's altered state as relevant to culpability while treating Kae's concealment of the weapon, his false statements, or his protective behaviour as obstruction. The final accusation therefore required participants to decide not only what had happened, but what could be argued from the evidence available to them.

However, these judgments did not occur under conditions of unlimited time. The rehearsal was structured around the premise that Detective Stoker had one afternoon to investigate the homicide, question the suspects, request further information, and present a sufficiently supported accusation. In practice, sessions were planned to run for up to approximately six hours. This temporal limit had a practical function in making the workshop manageable, but it also served a design function. It operationalised the proposed Window of Opportunity, or Kairos, mechanic by making the value of an investigative action dependent on when it occurred.

In this sense, time was not designed as a uniform countdown that merely accelerated the player towards a predetermined ending. Instead, it was intended to create situated investigative opportunities. Participants could request reports that were available immediately, but some materials represented processes that would take time within the fictional police procedure. Trace analysis, archival material, crime-pattern reports, and information obtained through extended interrogation could become available only after a delay. Participants therefore had to decide whether a report was worth waiting for, whether they should continue investigating while it was pending, whether they should pursue another line of inquiry, or whether they already possessed enough evidence to move towards accusation.

These decisions constituted kairotic moments because they involved the relation between an opportunity and its timing. A report requested early enough could still influence a later interrogation or provide support for a final accusation. The same report requested later in the session might arrive only when there was insufficient time to interpret it, test it through dialogue, or integrate it into a coherent case. Likewise, continuing to question a suspect could generate an important contradiction or disclosure, but it could also consume time that might have been used to obtain additional forensic or contextual material. The workshop therefore required participants to assess not only what information they wanted, but when that information would still have practical value.

Several sessions showed participants making explicit or observable decisions of this kind. Gui (S08), for example, requested delayed information connected to the wider robbery pattern and Kae's drug-related contacts. He chose to continue investigating while waiting for these reports rather than immediately moving to a final accusation. This approach enabled him to obtain further material, including Kae's confession tape and information related to the robbery series. At the same time, the session demonstrates that accumulating more evidence did not automatically produce a settled theory. Gui accessed substantial material relevant to both Kae and Elara, but remained more convinced by the case against Kae. Even after acquiring evidence that could have supported Elara's presence at the crime scene, he ultimately accused Kae of murder. The significance of this case is not that additional evidence produced the objectively correct answer, but that delayed information became part of an evolving interpretive process in which the participant continued to reassess the case.

Other sessions demonstrated the opposite strategic decision. Titi (S06) requested the trace analysis associated with Elara's injuries but chose not to wait for the result before continuing the investigation. This did not necessarily indicate impatience or failure to understand the system. It represented a decision that the available evidence, dialogue opportunities, and remaining time could be used more productively elsewhere. The decision to wait or not wait was therefore itself meaningful investigative behaviour. It showed that participants were capable of treating information as something with an opportunity cost rather than as content that should always be collected before proceeding.

The session involving Julie and Steph (S13) similarly showed how delayed information could be incorporated into an organised theory-building process. The participants read and discussed reports carefully, divided the work between reading and note-taking, and prepared a theory before using dialogue to test it. They waited for a relevant report before proceeding with the final confrontation, but this delay did not prevent them from maintaining a coherent direction. Their final accusation held Elara responsible for manslaughter and Kae responsible for gun possession. In this case, waiting functioned as a calculated investment in evidential support rather than an interruption of play. The delayed report was understood as potentially valuable because it related to a theory already under construction.

The most direct evidence of temporal pressure occurred when participants recognised that the available investigation period was approaching its end and altered their behaviour accordingly. Helen and Andrew (S09), for example, became aware of the time, reviewed their evidence, and attempted one final interrogation before being required to present their case. Their final theory was not sufficiently developed, and they were unable to provide a coherent accusation against either suspect. The resulting dismissal of Detective Stoker did not simply indicate that they had failed to solve the case. It showed that they had reached a point at which further inquiry was no longer available and were required to act with the incomplete interpretation they had constructed.

This outcome demonstrates an important aspect of the Kairos mechanic. The window of opportunity does not guarantee that participants will make an optimal decision. Instead, it creates a condition in which the participant must recognise that a decision is necessary even when certainty remains unavailable. Helen and Andrew had organised their evidence extensively, created spatial groupings, taken notes, and attempted to relate the available material through a Motive, Means, and Opportunity framework. Their difficulty did not arise from a lack of engagement with the evidence. Rather, they were unable to transform their accumulated material into a sufficiently supported accusation before the temporal opportunity closed. This distinction matters because it shows that investigative effort, evidence collection, and final commitment are related but not identical activities.

Rodrigo's (S03) session provided another clear example of forced commitment under temporal pressure. He developed a substantial case against Kae, drawing on the firearm, Kae's statements, and other contextual evidence. However, he continued to retain doubts concerning Elara's role, particularly in relation to the witness account and the evidence that she had been found barefoot with injuries consistent with the crime-scene environment. As the available time diminished, he had to decide whether to continue pursuing these unresolved questions or present the stronger case already available to him. He ultimately accused Kae of murder while Elara was released.

Rodrigo's decision is analytically important because it was not a simple error caused by overlooking evidence. His observations indicate that the unresolved questions remained present in his reasoning. The time constraint altered the status of those questions: they did not disappear, but they could no longer be pursued indefinitely. The final accusation therefore represented an institutional judgment made under incomplete knowledge. This corresponds directly to the intended role of Kairos in the design proposal. The player is not asked to wait until all uncertainty has been eliminated. Instead, the player must recognise when the available evidence is sufficient to justify action, while accepting responsibility for the possibility that further investigation might have produced a different interpretation.

Ricky's (S02) session similarly showed how temporal pressure could reshape the final stages of investigation. He engaged extensively with the evidence corpus, used reports to structure questions, revised his theory during the session, and eventually returned his attention to Elara after previously pursuing Kae. Near the end of the investigation, he wanted to continue exploring Elara's involvement and initiated a final interrogation. This interaction led to Elara challenging him to take the drug and enter the dream sequence. Ricky accepted the challenge, entered the dream material, and then presented a final accusation in which Elara was held responsible for manslaughter and Kae for obstruction of evidence. The sequence demonstrates that a limited remaining window can encourage participants to make consequential choices about where to direct their final attention. The dream sequence was not simply an optional narrative diversion in this context. It became a late investigative opportunity that reframed the available evidence and contributed to the participant's final interpretation.

At the same time, the workshop showed that temporal pressure could generate frustration. Gui (S08) expressed a desire to investigate the evidence and question the suspects further, but felt constrained by the lack of remaining time. Questionnaire responses echoed this. One participant, reflecting on their unresolved doubts about the barefoot figure and the footprints, wrote: "With maybe some more time these doubts could be explained. Could also be me overthinking a simple crime, thinking it's some complex plot twist" (Appendix X). Another participant described the most frustrating moment as "Não conseguir ter uma resposta definitiva no fim e ter de encontrar mais relatórios em pânico" (not being able to reach a definitive answer at the end and having to find more reports in a panic) (Appendix X). These responses show that the temporal limit was not invisible or purely administrative. Participants experienced it as part of the investigative condition. They understood that the case could potentially be explored further, yet had to decide whether the available material was enough to support an accusation.

This frustration should not be interpreted automatically as evidence that the Kairos mechanic failed. A detective investigation designed around ambiguity should not allow every contradiction to be resolved without cost, because doing so would reduce the need for prioritisation, commitment, and interpretive responsibility. The relevant question is whether participants understood the source of the limitation and whether they retained meaningful choices within it. When participants knew that time was limited, could identify possible investigative actions, and chose between waiting, questioning, gathering more evidence, or accusing, time pressure contributed to the intended experience of epistemic agency. It made the player responsible not only for interpreting evidence but also for deciding when an interpretation was strong enough to act upon.

Nevertheless, the rehearsal also revealed conditions under which temporal pressure could become unproductive. Some sessions were affected by unclear dialogue limits, uncertain interaction boundaries, prototype errors, or inconsistent facilitation. In these cases, time could be spent attempting interactions that were not clearly supported by the prototype rather than making purposeful investigative decisions. Participants sometimes did not know whether a failed action reflected an invalid evidential relation, an unavailable report, an exhausted dialogue path, or an unintended limitation of the low-fidelity system. When this occurred, the temporal constraint risked being experienced as avoidable friction rather than as a meaningful opportunity cost. This distinction mirrors the productive ambiguity and unproductive opacity discussed in Theme 2, but here it applies specifically to time: productive temporal pressure occurs when the player understands the available action space and must choose between meaningful alternatives; unproductive temporal pressure occurs when the player cannot determine what actions are possible or why an interaction failed, making time feel like an external punishment rather than an investigative condition.

The session involving John and Fred (S12) provides an important example. Both participants showed strong engagement with the case, made numerous requests, and attempted to construct explanations around the drug evidence, the footwear impressions, the firearm, and the possibility that both suspects were involved. However, their investigation was also affected by unclear dialogue boundaries, incompatible collaborative approaches, sustained attention to misleading evidence, and a facilitation failure in which Kae did not produce an intended confession response. Their frustration escalated into the use of the revolver as an intimidation device, which triggered a forced final confrontation before they had constructed a sufficiently supported accusation. The resulting dismissal was partly consistent with the intended consequence of coercive escalation, but it cannot be treated as pure evidence that time pressure alone produced a meaningful kairotic decision. The session was also shaped by prototype limitations that made the dialogue system difficult to interpret.

This qualification is necessary for understanding the workshop findings responsibly. The rehearsal suggests that time can create meaningful investigative pressure, but only when the player can understand the available action space. A participant must be able to distinguish between uncertainty that belongs to the case and opacity that results from the interface or facilitation. Productive uncertainty occurs when several interpretations remain plausible and the participant must decide which evidence to prioritise. Unproductive uncertainty occurs when the participant cannot determine what actions are possible, why an interaction failed, whether information is pending, or whether the system has responded consistently. The former supports epistemic agency; the latter risks turning investigation into trial-and-error.

The final accusation therefore functioned as both a narrative event and an institutional evaluation of the participant's reasoning. Captain Miller did not simply reveal whether the participant had selected the intended culprit. Instead, the Captain assessed whether the participant could articulate a coherent relation between the evidence gathered, the interpretation proposed, and the accusation made. Participants were required to move from individual fragments of information, such as a firearm, a wet hoodie, toxicology evidence, a witness statement, a confession, a trace report, or an inconsistent alibi, to a claim concerning responsibility. The final confrontation made this transformation visible. It required players to take ownership of their theory rather than merely receive the game's explanation of what had happened.

This process also had an ethical dimension. The case involved uncertainty concerning Elara's awareness, memory, and capacity at the time of the homicide; Kae's possible role in providing drugs, concealing evidence, and redirecting responsibility; and the difference between physical involvement, legal culpability, and institutional proof. Participants could not treat the final accusation as a neutral mechanical selection. Their interpretation had consequences for how the suspects were understood: as intentional perpetrators, impaired agents, accomplices, protectors, manipulators, or individuals caught within a larger pattern of drug use and concealment. The workshop therefore positioned accusation as an act of responsibility under uncertainty.

The findings suggest that the Window of Opportunity mechanic can support epistemic agency when it is implemented as a structure of prioritisation and commitment rather than as a simple timer. The relevant design function is not to punish players for reading slowly or to force premature decisions arbitrarily. Rather, it is to make investigative actions consequential by ensuring that time spent pursuing one possibility cannot also be spent pursuing every other possibility. Requesting a report, waiting for analysis, revisiting a contradiction, interrogating a suspect, entering a dream sequence, and submitting an accusation should each carry different temporal and narrative implications. This gives the player responsibility for the path taken through the case without requiring the system to reduce the investigation to a single correct route.

For the later digital prototype, this implies that the temporal system should make opportunities and consequences legible. Players should be able to recognise which reports are pending, which investigative routes remain available, which characters can still be approached, and whether a decision may close or alter a future path. The system should communicate the changing state of the investigation through diegetic means such as institutional reminders, changes in character availability, report-processing status, environmental shifts, sound design, and narrative escalation. However, it should avoid presenting time as an arbitrary external punishment. The aim is to preserve the sense that an investigation is a process of making situated judgments with incomplete information. A delayed report should create a meaningful decision because its potential evidential value is understandable, not because the player has been prevented from accessing information without explanation. Similarly, a final confrontation should feel like the consequence of the player's investigative priorities, accumulated evidence, and previous dialogue choices, rather than an abrupt interruption caused by unclear system rules.

Overall, this theme indicates that the rehearsal supported the project's argument that detective gameplay can be organised around interpretation, prioritisation, and accountable commitment. Participants did not only collect evidence or attempt to identify a hidden answer. They developed provisional theories, pursued and abandoned investigative routes, waited for or bypassed delayed information, responded to approaching deadlines, and made accusations despite remaining uncertainty. The rehearsal does not provide generalisable evidence that all players will experience temporal pressure in the same way. However, it provides design evidence that a kairotic structure can make time part of the epistemic activity of investigation. When supported by clear affordances, reliable feedback, and meaningful alternatives, the Window of Opportunity can transform time from an external limit into a mechanism through which players decide what matters, when further inquiry is justified, and when they are willing to take responsibility for a theory.

## 5.5 Summary of Results

The early design rehearsals provided an initial qualitative evaluation of the proposed detective-game systems before their translation into a digital prototype. The purpose of the workshops was not to validate a finished game, establish statistically generalisable findings, or identify a single optimal investigative path. Instead, the rehearsals examined whether participants could engage with the central design proposition of the project: that detective gameplay can be organised around the interpretation, comparison, testing, and revision of evidence-based theories rather than around the discovery of a hidden designer-defined answer.

The findings suggest that participants did not treat the evidence corpus merely as a collection of documents to unlock or read. Across the sessions, participants actively organised reports, created notes, constructed timelines, grouped material by suspect or theme, drew diagrams, compared contradictory accounts, and developed provisional explanations of what may have occurred. These practices were especially visible when participants externalised their reasoning through physical organisation. Evidence piles, sticky notes, diagrams, tables, clips, and handwritten records allowed participants to make relationships between reports visible and to return to them during later stages of the investigation. This supports the central premise that detective-game evidence can function as material for theory construction rather than as a sequence of fixed progression keys.

At the same time, the workshop demonstrated that interpretive freedom requires a legible interaction structure. Participants could often understand the general logic of the evidence-request system and the value of using evidence during dialogue. However, they also encountered uncertainty concerning which actions were available, which characters could be approached, why certain combinations failed, how inquiries differed from confrontations, and whether an unsuccessful interaction resulted from a weak theory, a system boundary, or a prototype error. These moments showed the importance of distinguishing productive ambiguity from unproductive opacity. Ambiguity was productive when participants had enough information to construct several plausible interpretations and were required to decide which one to pursue. Opacity was unproductive when participants could not understand the operational conditions of the system itself.

The rehearsal also showed that dialogue could function as a meaningful form of epistemic resistance. Participants were not simply given information by NPCs after presenting an item. Instead, the relevance of their evidence, the clarity of their investigative intention, the character being addressed, and the prior state of the conversation shaped the response. When participants could use evidence to formulate a coherent question or challenge, dialogue generated clarification, denial, emotional resistance, partial disclosure, or new contradictions. These outcomes became additional material for interpretation. However, the workshops also revealed that this system required more explicit feedback in the digital prototype. A player should be able to understand that a character is resisting a weak, unsupported, repetitive, or coercive line of reasoning without the game automatically revealing which conclusion they should reach.

Dream engagement provided a further form of interpretive possibility. The dream sequence was memorable for several participants and could encourage curiosity, emotional engagement, and alternative readings of Elara's condition, memory, and relationship to the case. In its most productive form, the dream–awake loop encouraged participants to reconsider documentary evidence through symbolic material without treating the dream as direct factual proof. The workshop also showed that symbolic material did not automatically become actionable investigative knowledge. Some participants entered the dream sequence but struggled to determine how it related to the murder, while others developed more complex theories by connecting dream material to pharmacological evidence, altered consciousness, or the previous detective's notes. The revised prototype therefore needs to provide clearer bridges between symbolic interpretation, waking evidence, and available dialogue actions while preserving the dream's ambiguity.

The sessions involving more than one participant revealed both the value and the limits of collaborative investigation. Some participants spontaneously divided the practical and cognitive labour of the case by reading reports aloud, taking notes, organising evidence, or challenging each other's developing theories. In several cases, discussion supported insights that did not appear to emerge from one participant independently. However, collaboration could also produce disagreement, unequal control over the investigation, conflicting priorities, and reduced opportunities for an individual player to follow uncertain or speculative ideas. The later digital prototype should therefore remain self-sufficient as a single-player experience while making the player's reasoning visible enough to support optional co-located discussion and shared interpretation.

Finally, the workshop established the importance of the temporal structure later described as the Window of Opportunity, or Kairos, mechanic. Participants had to decide whether further reports were worth pursuing, whether delayed information justified waiting, whether an unresolved contradiction required another interrogation, and when their available theory was sufficiently coherent to present to Captain Miller. The deadline did not simply end the workshop. It could transform the status of evidence by making particular investigative opportunities available only for a limited period. Participants were sometimes required to commit to an accusation while retaining doubts, alternative theories, or untested lines of inquiry. This created a form of institutional judgment under incomplete knowledge. The resulting findings suggest that time can support epistemic agency when it creates meaningful prioritisation and commitment, but only when the available actions, pending reports, and consequences of delay are sufficiently legible.

Taken together, the workshop findings did not confirm that every proposed system was already effective. Instead, they identified the conditions under which the systems could better support player-led interpretation. The subsequent revision therefore focused on four connected objectives: improving the internal consistency of the evidence corpus; clarifying the relationship between evidence, institutional requests, dialogue, and player intention; expanding the narrative and spatial context in which evidence could be encountered; and providing better support for the externalisation, revision, and communication of player theories.

The low-fidelity rehearsal was consequently treated as a formative Research-through-Design stage. Its value lay not only in identifying successful interactions, but also in making design limitations visible before they became embedded in the digital prototype. The revised narrative and interaction structure described in the following chapter emerged directly from this process of observation, correction, and iteration.
