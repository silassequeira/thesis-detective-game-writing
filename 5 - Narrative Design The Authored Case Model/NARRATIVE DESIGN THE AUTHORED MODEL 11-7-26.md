# 5. Narrative Design: The Authored Case Model

## 5.1 Chapter Purpose

This chapter establishes the complete designer-facing narrative model that structures the interactive investigation. It presents the authored case as a coherent dramatic system: the fabula of Arthur Penhaligon's death, the distribution of knowledge among characters, the conflicts that drive dramatic action, and the resolution structures that allow different investigative interpretations to converge into meaningful outcomes.

The player does not receive this complete model. They encounter fragments through reports, testimonies, physical traces, and character reactions. This chapter presents the complete structure from which those fragments are derived, along with helping contextualize the findings and design decisions in later chapters.

## 5.2 The Authored Case

The premise of the narrative is derived from an attempt to reduce the scope from the project's initial narrative concept was set in a wider mystery world centered on an addictive dream-inducing substance and the disappearance of a young woman. The player would investigate physical traces across a small town while also entering symbolic dream environments associated with fragmented memory, altered perception, and emotional experience (Figure 32). This initial direction introduced themes that remained central to the project, including unreliable recollection, institutional neglect, altered consciousness, and the relationship between symbolic and factual forms of evidence.

However, the scale of that narrative exceeded what could reasonably be implemented and evaluated through an early playable prototype. It required multiple locations, a large cast of characters, interconnected disappearances, several dream environments, and a wider social setting structured around the shared use of the substance.

The narrative was therefore reconstructed as a single, bounded homicide investigation, where the victim Arthur Penhaligon, was shot with a single 9mm bullet to the head during the evening in the fictional city of Oakhaven. The suspects are being held for the murder, with the initial investigation that was being done by Detective Thorne being left without any resolution. With the player's investigation being based on the traces that were left from the initial investigation.

### 5.2.1 Elara, Kaelen, and Somnoryl-μ

Elara Vance and Kaelen "Kae" Ward (Figure 33) began their relationship two years before the investigation. Both carried personal difficulties. Elara had a fragile relationship with her family and a pattern of emotional dependency on troubled partners. Kae had grown up in instability, learning to approach the world as hostile and unsafe.

Kae introduced Elara to Somnoryl-μ, a fictional drug also known as the Moon Drug (Figure 34), shortly after they began dating. The substance produces vivid dreams that feel indistinguishable from waking experience, responding to the user's desires, memories, and emotional needs. For Elara, it became escape from a waking world she experienced as painful and difficult to control. Her attachment to the drug was connected not only to physical addiction but to the emotional relief it provided.

Kae's relationship with the substance changed over time. He gradually lost the ability to experience the dreams as genuine pleasure and began using smaller doses simply to avoid withdrawal. He continued buying the drug because he believed Elara still needed it. He obtained it from a dealer known as Rico, who allowed him to pay later. This arrangement caused his debt to grow.

### 5.2.2 The Firearm and the Failed Robbery

Kae acquired a Taurus 905 revolver (Figure 34) from a friend of Rico, with the serial number scrapped. The weapon was necessary for protection due to the threats related to his drug debt, and the increase of criminal activity around his residence. Elara did not know he owned it.

Before Arthur Penhaligon's death, Kae attempted to rob him. Kae concealed his face and tried to take Arthur's wallet, but the robbery failed. Arthur resisted, and the confrontation became aggressive. Although Kae did not shoot him, Arthur reported the attempted robbery to the police. Other people in the area noticed the confrontation, creating a previous connection between Kae and the victim.

### 5.2.3 The Night of the Murder

On the night of the murder, Elara and Kae consumed Somnoryl-μ before falling asleep. The drug affected Elara more strongly than Kae since she consumed a higher dosage. At some point during the night, Kae woke after the immediate effects had begun to wear off. Elara was no longer in the room.

Kae waited for her to return. After some time, Elara returned to the apartment. Her clothing was soaked from the rain, and she was barefoot. She appeared detached from her surroundings and did not respond normally when Kae spoke to her. She was carrying the Taurus 905 revolver, even though she did not know Kae had bought it.

Elara did not explain where she had been. She behaved as if she were still partly inside the dream state produced by Somnoryl-μ. She eventually went to sleep without providing a coherent account of what had happened. Kae was frightened by her condition and by the presence of the gun. He also heard police sirens outside and understood that something serious had occurred.

Kae did not call the police or ask Elara directly about the weapon. Instead, he hid the gun beneath the floorboards of the apartment. His decision was motivated by fear, but it also transformed his knowledge into concealment. From this moment onward, he understood that the weapon might connect him or Elara to a serious crime, even though he did not know exactly what had happened.

Arthur Penhaligon had been walking towards his car after finishing a work shift when he was shot. He was not robbed, and there was no clear evidence that he had been pursued. The attack appeared sudden and disproportionate. The shot struck him in the face and killed him immediately. The absence of a stolen wallet or other material motive made the crime difficult to interpret as a conventional robbery.

The only direct witness was Meredith Gable, an elderly woman who lived in the same deteriorating building as Elara and Kae. From her position, she saw a person moving through the rain shortly before or after the shooting. She could identify only broad details, such as the person appearing to be barefoot and wearing a dark, wet hooded garment. She could not see the face clearly because of darkness, rain, and distance.

### 5.2.4 Kae's First Deception

When the police first arrived at the building, Kae answered the door. He stepped into the corridor before the officers could see properly into the apartment and claimed Elara was still getting dressed. The officers asked whether he had seen anyone moving through the area. Kae stated he had been watching television and had not heard anything unusual. He also claimed Elara had gone to sleep early because she had work the following morning.

Kae understood the police were suspicious, but prevented them from immediately seeing the interior of the apartment. His statement was false in several ways. He had not simply been watching television, he knew Elara had returned in a disturbed condition, and he had already hidden the firearm. However, he did not yet know whether Elara had committed the shooting or whether the gun had been used by someone else.

The following morning, Kae told Elara the police would probably return with a warrant. He asked whether she remembered anything from the previous night. Elara did not understand the question in the way he intended. She described the night as a beautiful and pleasurable dream and did not remember leaving the apartment, walking barefoot in the rain, or carrying the gun.

Kae interpreted Elara's lack of memory as evidence she might have been involved in the murder. At the same time, he remained convinced she had not acted with ordinary awareness or intention. His fear developed into a protective decision based on his feeling of being indirectly responsible for the crime, and where his decisions could make Elara be charged as the shooter, and being convicted to a life of misery inside one of Oakhaven's prisons.

### 5.2.5 The Arrest

The police returned to the apartment with a search warrant. During the search, they discovered the Taurus 905 revolver hidden beneath the floorboards. They also found other objects connecting the couple to the night of the murder, including Somnoryl-μ, clothing affected by the rain, and material capable of placing one or both suspects near the relevant locations.

Both Elara and Kae were arrested. Their apartment became part of the crime scene. Kae's criminal record, his prior confrontation with Arthur, his possession of the firearm, and his attempts to conceal evidence created a strong circumstantial case against him. Elara's physical condition, missing memory, bare feet, wet clothing, and possible connection to the witness description created a different but equally troubling interpretation.

The central uncertainty of the case was not whether Elara and Kae were connected to the crime. Both were connected to it. The uncertainty concerned how that connection should be interpreted. Kae had a history of violence, robbery, weapon possession, and concealment, but he did not commit the shooting. Elara had apparently been present at the scene and may have fired the weapon, but she had no conscious memory of the event and was severely impaired by Somnoryl-μ.

### 5.2.6 The Designer's Complete Account

Within the complete narrative model, Elara was the person who shot Arthur Penhaligon. Her action occurred while she was under the influence of Somnoryl-μ and experiencing an altered state that made her perception and memory unreliable. She left the apartment barefoot, carried Kae's hidden firearm, encountered Arthur, and shot him. She then returned home without retaining a stable memory of the event.

Kae did not commit the murder. However, his actions contributed indirectly to the circumstances that made it possible. He bought the weapon, concealed it from Elara, maintained a criminal life without telling her the truth, accumulated drug debt, and allowed both of them to remain dependent on Somnoryl-μ. After discovering Elara in a disturbed state, he hid the weapon and lied to the police. His subsequent protective behavior makes him legally and morally implicated even though he was not the person who fired the shot.

This distinction between direct action and indirect responsibility is the central moral tension of the case. Elara is responsible for the physical act of killing Arthur, but her capacity to understand and control that act is compromised. Kae did not kill Arthur, but his decisions created and concealed the conditions surrounding the murder. The case cannot be reduced to a simple opposition between an innocent suspect and a guilty suspect.

## 5.3 The Knowledge Structure

### 5.3.1 Character Positions

The characters in this narrative have their own identities, motivations, relationships, and limitations that determine what actions are dramatically possible within the investigation. Each character possesses a different understanding of the crime and responds according to their own interests, fears, responsibilities and awareness.

| ##### Figure 35 - Concept Art for Captain Miller (Left) and Detective Julian (Right) |

Detective Julian Hammond Stoker is the player character (Figure 35). He is a relatively new homicide detective who previously gained recognition for solving a series of murders in the rural area where he grew up. His transfer to Oakhaven Central represents a professional opportunity and a test of whether he can function within the larger institution. He is positioned between two models of detective work: institutional procedure and independent interpretation. His severe allergy to mold and dust provides a physical limitation within the story world. His professional position creates a dramatic stake, where Captain Turner is evaluating whether he is capable of handling a homicide case. If Stoker fails to construct a sufficiently supported accusation, he risks losing his position.

Captain Miller Turner is the chief of Oakhaven Central's fourth district station and the institutional authority overseeing the investigation (Figure 35). He serves as Stoker's superior, evaluator, and primary source of procedural pressure. Turner views the justice system as a bureaucratic process that must continue functioning. A successful investigation, to him, produces an identifiable suspect, sufficient evidence, and a completed case. He does not consider emotional complexity, psychological trauma, or broader social causes relevant unless they can be translated into a form the institution can document and use. His role is to represent the institutional standard against which the player's theory must be tested.

Elara Vance is one of the two central suspects. She is emotionally fragile, traumatized, and affected by a severe dependency on Somnoryl-μ. She does not remember shooting Arthur Penhaligon. During the night of the murder, she was under the influence of the drug and experienced a state of altered consciousness. From her perspective, the night was a beautiful and fragmented dream. She returned home barefoot, wet from the rain, and psychologically detached from her surroundings, but she could not explain how these conditions came about. She generally attempts to speak truthfully about what she remembers, but her memory is incomplete and affected by shame. Her relationship with Kae strongly influences how she speaks. She believes he is fundamentally kind and incapable of murder, even though she knows he has a criminal past. She does not fully understand how he obtains money or Somnoryl-μ, because Kae has concealed his unemployment and criminal activities from her.

Kaelen "Kae" Ward is the second central suspect and the character most directly associated with the weapon and the criminal history surrounding the case. He has previously committed robberies and stolen cars, owns the Taurus 905 revolver, and attempted to rob Arthur before the murder. However, Kae did not shoot Arthur. His involvement is indirect, concealed, and emotionally motivated. His primary objective during interrogation is to protect Elara. He is willing to lie, redirect questions, and eventually confess to a murder he did not commit if he believes doing so will prevent Elara from going to prison. His confession is therefore not a reliable revelation of the truth. It is a protective performance designed to produce a particular institutional outcome.

This distribution prevents the investigation from being solved through a single authoritative testimony. Elara cannot explain the crime because she cannot remember it. Kae can explain parts of the context, but deliberately distorts his testimony. Turner can judge the institutional adequacy of an accusation, but does not possess the full truth. Stoker and the player must construct a theory by comparing different forms of incomplete knowledge.

### 5.3.3 Character as Constraint

The characters' established identities limit the possible actions and responses available within the narrative. Elara cannot suddenly provide a precise chronological account of the murder because her memory and psychoactive condition prevent it. Kae cannot calmly reveal every relevant fact because his central motivation is to protect Elara. Turner cannot accept every interpretation as equally valid because his role requires him to filter evidence through institutional standards.

These limitations are not only technical restrictions on dialogue. They are intrinsic constraints that make the fictional world and its characters consistent. The player's freedom is not created by allowing every character to say anything or by making every conclusion possible. It is created by allowing the player to act meaningfully within a system whose responses are coherent with the characters' motivations and circumstances.

A character's reaction can reveal information even when their words do not provide a direct answer. Elara's emotional collapse may indicate that confronting with certain evidence leads to a painful personal truth. Kae's sudden willingness to confess may reveal his protective motivation rather than confirm that he committed the murder. Turner's rejection of a theory may demonstrate the limits of institutional reasoning rather than prove that the player's interpretation is incorrect.

## 5.4 The Dramatic Structure

### 5.4.1 Three Levels of Conflict

The investigation is not a neutral search for facts. It is a negotiation between characters who each attempt to preserve something: their freedom, their relationship, their professional position, or their understanding of themselves. The central dramatic conflict operates on three interconnected levels.

The Indexical Conflict: Who killed Arthur Penhaligon? The evidence points in multiple directions. Kae owned the weapon, had a criminal history, and attempted to rob Arthur previously. Elara was physically present at the scene, was severely impaired by Somnoryl-μ, and cannot remember what happened. Some reports were created as Red Herring (a clue, argument, or piece of information that misleads, distracts, or takes people's attention away from a central or important issue), these were made to conflict with some of the interpretations, these can be filtered or be influenced by them to chase other investigation trails. The evidence reports establish connections but does not resolve the question of intention, awareness, or responsibility.

The Relational Conflict: What will Elara and Kae sacrifice to protect each other? Kae is willing to confess to a murder he did not commit, providing a plausible but false motive. Elara is unable to provide a coherent account because she genuinely cannot remember. Their relationship is built on mutual dependency, but also on concealment and protective deception. The investigation path through the interrogation forces these concealed truths into the open, and provides more personal and emotional information to complement the information obtained through the reports.

The Institutional Conflict: What constitutes sufficient proof? Captain Turner represents the bureaucratic demand for a clear, prosecutable conclusion, accepting any minimal defendable theory not really caring about the truth. Stoker and the player must transform an ambiguous human situation into a legally defensible accusation. Where the conflict between understanding and proof shapes every interaction with the institution. This accusation must be sufficiently sustained by the evidence that the player presents at the end of the investigation. However, the morality, responsibility, and justice of the outcome are not determined by the institutional verdict alone. They are left to the moment of catharsis, where the player reflects on their decisions and interactions throughout the narrative.

### 5.4.2 Dramatic Pressures

Before the player begins, the dramatic situation is already unstable. The murder has occurred, the suspects have been arrested, and the initial investigation has been abandoned. Detective Thorne, the original investigator, resigned without explanation, leaving the case file incomplete. Captain Turner has assigned Stoker to complete the investigation and produce a result.

This situation creates five dramatic pressures:

Narrative Pressure: The crime has already happened. The player cannot prevent it. They can only reconstruct it.

Temporal Pressure: The investigation period is limited. Stoker must produce a conclusion before the institutional window closes.

Professional Pressure: Stoker's career depends on the outcome. Failure means dismissal and return to the rural station.

Epistemic Pressure: The evidence is incomplete, contradictory, and open to multiple interpretations. Certainty may be impossible.

Moral Pressure: The suspects are not simple villains. They are damaged people whose actions emerged from addiction, poverty, fear, and misguided protection. The player must decide how to weigh intention, capacity, and consequence.

### 5.4.3 Dramatic Action

The dramatic action of the investigation unfolds through the player's decisions about how to engage with the case.

Evidence Examination: The player can examine physical evidence, request forensic reports, and review documents. Each piece of evidence provides information but also raises new questions.

Suspect Interrogation: The player can question Elara and Kae. Each interaction tests a hypothesis or pursues a line of inquiry. The characters respond according to their knowledge, motivations, and patience thresholds. Elara may become distressed, confused, or emotionally overwhelmed. Kae may become defensive, aggressive, or protective. Their responses provide information but also reveal the limits of their knowledge and the depth of their concealment.

Institutional Engagement: The player can interact with Captain Turner. Turner evaluates the player's theory and demands procedural justification, while also aiding the investigation requesting additional reports with a narrative and temporal cost.

Dream Sequence: The player can enter a symbolic dream sequence by combining the auto-injector with the Somnoryl-μ vial. This optional investigation mode provides symbolic and affective material that complicates the waking evidence being intentionally constructed to lead the player to wider conspiracy with no factual resolution.

### 5.4.4 Revelations

As the investigation progresses, the player can uncover some revelations that escalate the dramatic tension:

Kae's Criminal Activities: The player discovers that Kae was not working for a moving company. He lost his job, and without any other alternative, he returned to his life of robbery, while accumulating debt to his drug dealer. This revelation exposes the gap between the life Elara believes they share and the reality of Kae's secret activities.

The Robbery Pattern: The crime analysis report connects the prior attempted robbery of Arthur to a broader pattern of unsolved robberies in the Wharf Street corridor. The modus operandi is consistent with Kae's criminal behavior, but the murder does not fit the pattern.

Elara's Impairment: The toxicology report establishes that Elara was at peak psychoactive impairment during the murder window. This revelation complicates her culpability. She may have fired the weapon, but her capacity to understand and control her actions was severely compromised.

Elara's Fragmented Memory: Elara describes the night as just being in beautiful dream. She does not remember leaving the apartment, walking barefoot in the rain, or carrying the gun. Her memory loss is genuine, but it also prevents her from providing a complete account of what happened.

The Trace Evidence: The particulate analysis connects the abrasions on Elara's feet to the specific environmental conditions of the crime scene alley. This evidence places her at the scene but does not establish her mental state or intention.

Kae's Protective Confession: If the player applies sufficient pressure, Kae confesses to the murder, providing a plausible easy solution to the crime. However, his confession contains inconsistencies and convenient claims that directly contradict the reports. The player must decide whether to accept his confession or recognize it as a protective lie.

### 5.4.5 Formal Cause

The formal cause of the narrative is the complete dramatic structure designed before the player begins the investigation. It consists of more than a sequence of events. It includes the relationships between events, the characters who act within them, the motivations that make those actions probable, and the consequences that allow the investigation to develop towards a resolution.

In this prototype, the formal cause can be organized through the six elements of dramatic structure discussed by Laurel: action or plot, character, thought, language, pattern, and spectacle (Laurel, 2014). These elements form a downward causal movement in which the larger action determines how characters behave, how they think, what they say, what patterns can be identified, and how the experience is finally presented to the player.

Applied to the Oakhaven investigation:

Crime and investigation establishes the central action. Characters give that action a human and social structure. Motivations and beliefs determine why they conceal, distort, or disclose information. Language expresses these positions through testimony and confrontation. Patterns emerge from the relationships between statements, objects, reports, and physical traces. Spectacle presents these patterns through the rooms, interfaces, audiovisual elements, objects, and interaction systems of the prototype.

This formal structure does not determine exactly what every player will conclude. It determines the conditions under which different conclusions can become plausible. The formal cause establishes a field of competing possibilities rather than a single sequence of mandatory discoveries.

## 5.5 The Resolution Structure

### 5.5.1 Convergence Architecture

The narrative structure does not follow a fully linear sequence, but it also does not attempt to create an independent branch for every possible player action. Instead, it uses a convergent structure in which the player can investigate the case through different combinations of evidence, interactions, and interpretations before reaching a limited set of resolutions.

The structure has three layers:

Investigative Variation: Players choose different evidence, interactions, and routes through the case. They may focus on Kae's criminal history and possession of the firearm, or on Elara's physical condition and altered state. They may treat Kae's confession as credible, interpret it as a protective lie, or recognize that both suspects are responsible in different ways.

Interpretive Convergence: These routes are transformed into a theory about responsibility. The player must decide which evidence is reliable, which testimony is credible, and what explanation the available material supports. This is the moment when the player's epistemic agency becomes visible as a commitment.

Resolution Variation: The theory produces one of a limited number of verdicts and emotional outcomes. The institutional resolution determines how responsibility is assigned through the final accusation. The emotional resolution determines how the characters understand and experience that judgment.

At the beginning of the investigation, the player is presented with a bounded but relatively open field of possible actions. They can decide which reports to request, which objects to examine, which suspect to question, and whether to pursue symbolic information through the dream sequence. These decisions affect the order in which information is encountered and the strength of the player's developing theory.

The middle of the narrative contains the greatest degree of variation. The player's agency is not located only in the final choice. It is distributed across the process of forming a theory. The player chooses what to consider relevant, what relationships to establish between pieces of evidence, and when to stop investigating and commit to a conclusion.

As the investigation progresses, the possible routes begin to converge. The player must eventually communicate a theory to Captain Turner and identify who they consider responsible. This institutional confrontation transforms a private interpretation into an official judgment. At this point, the narrative cannot continue indefinitely through further evidence collection. The player must commit to an interpretation under conditions of incomplete knowledge.

### 5.5.2 Principal Verdict Structures

The resolution branches into a limited set of outcomes. These outcomes represent different configurations of responsibility that the narrative considers thematically and dramatically significant.

Verdict 1: Kae Convicted of Murder, Elara Released

The player concludes that Kae is the primary murderer. This verdict is supported by evidence of Kae's possession of the firearm, his prior confrontation with Arthur, his criminal history, his concealment of evidence, and potentially his own confession. Elara is released because the evidence against her is considered insufficient or because she was not believed to be the shooter.

Verdict 2: Elara Convicted of Manslaughter, Kae Convicted of Obstruction

The player concludes that Elara fired the weapon but was severely impaired by Somnoryl-μ, reducing her culpability from murder to manslaughter. Kae is convicted of obstruction for hiding the weapon and lying to the police. This verdict recognizes the distinction between physical action and conscious intention while still holding both characters responsible for their roles in the case.

Verdict 3: Elara Convicted of Manslaughter, Kae Convicted of Gun Possession

The player concludes that Elara fired the weapon while impaired but does not establish Kae's participation in the shooting. Kae is convicted only of illegal firearm possession. This verdict fragments responsibility, leaving both characters damaged without producing a complete account of the crime.

Verdict 4: Insufficient Case - Detective Stoker Dismissed

The player fails to construct a sufficiently coherent or supported accusation. Captain Turner dismisses the case and terminates Stoker's position. This verdict represents the institutional consequence of failing to transform investigative effort into a defensible conclusion.

### 5.5.3 Emotional Resolution Variants

The verdict reached at the end of the investigation does not fully determine the emotional meaning of the narrative. Two players may reach the same institutional conclusion while producing different emotional consequences for Elara and Kae. These differences emerge from the evidence presented, the timing of the confrontation, the theory developed by the player, and the way each suspect is made to confront the consequences of their actions.

Elara is most affected by evidence that reveals the extent of Kae's deception. She believed that Kae was protecting her and that, despite his past, he remained fundamentally honest with her. If the player confronts her with evidence of the firearm, the robberies, or Kae's concealed financial problems or by showing her his false confession to the crime, the investigation becomes a personal revelation. Her distress is not caused only by the possibility of imprisonment. It is also caused by discovering that the person she trusted had been constructing a false version of their life and relationship.

Kae is most affected by the consequences of his protective strategy. He is willing to confess to a murder he did not commit if he believes the confession will keep Elara free. In case the player is able to pressure Kaelen enough to force a confession out of him, and still decides to convict Elara. Kae is then forced to confront the fact that his willingness to take responsibility did not repair the situation he helped create, leaving him feeling devastated.

### 5.5.4 Constraint and Catharsis

Elara cannot simply recover a complete memory when the player asks the correct question. Kae cannot reveal the truth without risking the person he is trying to protect. Captain Turner cannot accept emotional certainty as a substitute for institutional evidence. These limitations prevent the emotional resolution from becoming a simple reward for selecting the correct dialogue option. The player must work within the characters' established positions and accept that no confrontation can undo the consequences of the past.

Catharsis in this narrative is not defined as the achievement of a perfect or morally clean conclusion. It emerges from the recognition that every resolution leaves something unresolved. A player may successfully identify Elara as the shooter, but still need to question whether punishment adequately accounts for her altered state. They may identify Kae's complicity, but still recognize that his actions were motivated by an attempt to protect someone he loved. They may produce a procedurally convincing accusation while remaining uncertain whether the institutional result represents justice.

The intended emotional effect is one of reflection rather than reassurance. The player is encouraged to reconsider the assumptions that guided their investigation. Whether possession implies guilt, whether memory loss implies deception, whether confession implies truth, and whether legal closure is equivalent to moral resolution.

## 5.6 Chapter Synthesis

This chapter established the complete designer-facing narrative model that structures the interactive investigation. It presented the fabula of Arthur Penhaligon's death, introduced the characters and their different relationships to knowledge, and explained how the player reconstructs the case through incomplete evidence, conflicting testimony, and constrained interaction.

The crime, characters, motivations, evidence relations, and possible resolutions form the formal cause of the interactive experience. The player encounters these elements through the material conditions of the prototype, such as the precinct, evidence corpus, dialogue, interfaces, audiovisual presentation, and investigative actions. Meaning emerges through the movement between these two levels.

Player agency is not based on unrestricted narrative branching. Instead, the investigation supports variation through the selection, combination, interpretation, and presentation of evidence. Different investigative paths can produce different theories about responsibility while remaining within a bounded fictional world. These theories converge into a limited set of verdict structures, which can then produce different emotional resolutions according to the confrontations and evidence that shaped them.

This narrative architecture provides the basis for the next stages of the research. The Workshop chapter examines how this formal structure was enacted through low-fidelity materials, facilitator-led role-play, evidence requests, suspect interactions, and final accusations. The Digital Prototype chapter describes how the narrative relationships and investigative possibilities were translated into an interactive system.
