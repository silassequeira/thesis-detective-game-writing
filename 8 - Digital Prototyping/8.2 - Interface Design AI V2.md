### 8.2 Interface Design

The user interface is where the prototype's epistemic principles become directly manipulable. Carrying forward the paper-and-HTML/CSS planning from Chapters 6 and 7, the interface treats evidence as a physical workspace, embeds the two-slot constraint inside conversation rather than behind menus, enforces clear modal focus across overlapping windows, and signals procedural relevance without ever confirming whether the player's overarching theory is true.

#### 8.2.1 The Interface as the Interrogation Instrument

Rather than hiding evidence selection behind a separate inventory screen, the dialogue panel places Slot A and Slot B directly inside the conversation, in a narrow sidebar beside the dialogue stream and the list of available questions, anchored at the bottom centre of the screen. Every time either slot changes, the panel re-evaluates the equipped combination and immediately rebuilds the available question buttons. For the player, interrogation becomes an iterative physical test: they arrange one or two clues in the sidebar and immediately see which lines of questioning the character is willing to entertain.

To keep slot manipulation fast during conversation, the interface supports three ways of equipping evidence without leaving dialogue:

1. **Drag-and-drop:** Cards can be dragged from the panel's quick menu onto either slot—a tilted "ghost" card follows the cursor and valid drop targets highlight—or dragged back out of a slot to unequip them.
2. **Quick-menu tabs:** Clicking an empty slot opens the quick-menu drawer beside the dialogue panel, where the player can switch between their pinned folder and the full inventory. A similar quick menu is also available during free roam.

Switching between **Inquiry** and **Confrontation** happens inside the same panel, with a single key, rather than through a separate accusation screen. Switching re-skins the mode badge, the borders of occupied slots, the detective's speech accents, and the question buttons' hover states from the detective's slate blue to a confrontation magenta, while the character's speech remains warm amber. The player feels the escalation from open questioning to confrontation visually before choosing a line.

Question buttons form a readable visual vocabulary through distinct coloured left borders:

- **Chain and standalone questions** carry the active mode's blue or magenta accent.
- **Resume options** appear in warm gold, signalling a bookmarked chain of questioning waiting to be continued.
- **Escape actions within a chain** use a muted grey, distinguishing interface actions (leaving the current chain or switching modes) from in-fiction questions.
- **Locked actions** are dimmed with a faint red edge, signalling world interactions whose required items are not yet equipped.

(Colour and opacity values are listed in Appendix X.9.)

By default, the conversation area opens collapsed, showing only the latest speech bubble, so that attention stays anchored on the current exchange; clicking the stream expands the panel to reveal prior turns.

#### 8.2.2 One Panel Grammar Across Systems

#### 8.2.3 Evidence as a Workspace for Thinking

Because Chapter 6 showed that players naturally sort, annotate, and cross-check documents between conversations, the interface treats the 61 evidence items as an active reasoning workspace rather than a passive inventory list:

- **Inventory and folders:** The inventory shows all acquired items in a card grid alongside a strip of folder tabs. Players begin with a starter folder and can create their own—up to six folders in total—rename and delete them (deleting a folder asks for confirmation and makes clear that the evidence stays in the main inventory), and cycle between them. A toggle switches the left sidebar between the two slots and the contents of the current folder, so players can drag evidence into their own case folders and pin one folder to appear in the conversation quick menu. Shortcut hints for inspecting, reading, equipping, and removing light up when the cursor is over a card and dim when it is not.
- **Inspect mode:** Opened from any card, inspect mode pairs the evidence card and quick-equip buttons (highlighted when the item is equipped) with a multi-page preview and a personal note box. Players can write a short note for each item; notes persist across saves and are never parsed or graded by any game system.
- **Read mode:** Opened from inspect mode, the inventory, or the document folders placed in rooms, read mode is a full-screen document reader with zoom from half size to four times, a double-click zoom toggle, drag-panning, a view reset, page arrows, and edge strips for cycling pages. High magnification ensures that dense forensic tables, timestamps, and medical notes remain genuinely legible rather than decorative props.
- **Conversation archive:** The archive lists past sessions grouped by character (Miller, Elara, Kae) beside a transcript of the selected session. Each detective statement is marked with the slot pair used and with small labels for the evidence shown. Mirroring the player's tabletop notes, it serves as an external memory of what has already been asked and shown.

Crucially, the same hover shortcuts work across panels: pressing the inspect or read key while hovering over any acquired, non-pending card—whether in the inventory, the quick menu, the terminal, or the free-roam slot strip—opens that view directly in place. (Key bindings for all of these panels are listed in Appendix X.8.)

#### 8.2.4 Layering, Focus, and Control

With up to nine UI panels coexisting in the precinct scene, interface management must guarantee visual order, prevent the detective from walking underneath open windows, and ensure that the Escape key behaves predictably. Each panel sits at a fixed layer in a sorting stack: the free-roam HUD at the bottom; the dialogue panel and terminal above it; then the archive and the wait panel; the inventory and pause menu; inspect mode and settings; read mode above all interactive panels; and, at the very top, a non-blocking screen fader and the saving indicator (full stack in Appendix X.7). Escape handling follows the same deliberate logic: the dialogue panel opts out of Escape entirely so that an interrogation cannot be aborted by accident, while the inventory and inspect mode first cancel any open sub-prompt—a folder rename or deletion, an unsaved note edit—before closing.

Focus and player locking are handled centrally by a shared panel base class and a panel tracker. Opening any modal panel increases a count of open modal panels and, if that panel can be closed with Escape, places it on an escape stack (the dialogue panel joins the count but not the stack). While any modal panel is open, the player character is locked, freezing movement and camera zoom until the last panel closes. A single global escape handler owns the Escape key: it closes the topmost panel, first letting that panel cancel any sub-prompt, and opens the pause menu only when no modal panel is open and the player is not in the main menu. As a defensive safeguard against soft-locks if a panel is ever disabled mid-transition, the tracker resets a stuck modal count after three seconds of active, unpaused gameplay.

#### 8.2.5 Feedback: What Is Signalled and What Is Withheld

To preserve interpretive ambiguity (Section 8.1.6) without frustrating usability, the interface draws a strict boundary between **procedural legibility** and **epistemic judgment**.

On the procedural side, the interface clearly signals item status and interactive readiness:

- **Unseen evidence:** Newly acquired cards carry a warm gold border, and the free-roam HUD's inventory icon changes until every new item has been opened in inspect or read mode.
- **Pending evidence:** Reports awaiting laboratory processing are dimmed behind a dark overlay with an hourglass and a live countdown, and cannot be dragged, equipped, or inspected.
- **Locked world actions:** Unavailable actions remain visible in Interaction mode with dimmed, red-edged styling, while actions that are ready show which item they will use.
- **Empty and terminal misses:** When nothing is equipped, dialogue shows a neutral prompt ("Equip evidence in a slot to start inquiry."), and a mismatched terminal submission shows "No matching record found for this combination." without hinting which slot, tape, or document failed.

Within conversation, the dialogue panel and the archive also record two procedural relevance markers: an out-of-range warning, indicating that the equipped evidence missed the current chain node's target and reduced the character's patience, and a slip marker, indicating that a one-shot slip event fired. In the dialogue panel, these markers are hidden while the conversation stream is collapsed—the default view—and appear only if the player explicitly expands it, whereas the archive always displays them in the session log. Even here, the distinction remains strict: the interface indicates whether a move was _procedurally relevant_ to a character's active line of questioning or triggered a state transition, never whether the player's overarching theory of the crime is right or wrong. _[Author note: in the dialogue panel, confirm whether the slip badge text (`"SLIP EVENT {id}"`) was intentionally left exposed as a procedural cue or should be noted in 8.2.8 as a debug-style string carried into the playtest build.]_

#### 8.2.6 Time and Transitions

Time pressure is communicated through three restrained interface elements rather than intrusive pop-ups:

1. **The persistent clock HUD:** Positioned top-left on a dark translucent plate, the clock shows in-game wall time (starting at 3:30 PM), the hours and minutes remaining, a state label (running, paused, warning, or expired), and a progress bar. Crossing the 12-hour warning shifts the text and bar from muted gold to bright warning amber; reaching zero turns them red. Crucially, the HUD stays hidden until the clock starts, and hides whenever a modal panel opens or Miller forcibly approaches the player—ensuring the clock is _felt_ across exploration rather than stared at during conversations. Below the clock sits a minimal strip showing the two equipped slots in greyscale, and small key badges for the main panels appear in the bottom-right corner.
2. **The wait panel:** Interacting with the Evidence Room chair or the Observation Room printer opens a modal slider for 1 to 24 whole hours. Confirming fades the screen out and back in while the clock advances, and the panel rejects the request with an inline error if the clock is stopped or has already expired—framing waiting as a deliberate, irreversible commitment.
3. **The locked-room countdown:** During the 25-second collapse sequence, a progress bar shifts from green to red and pulses rhythmically in the final ten seconds, paired with an injection prompt once the Auto Injector and Adrenaline Vial are both equipped.

#### 8.2.7 Visual Language

Across all panels, colour and typography are used functionally to distinguish speaker, mode, and epistemic status rather than for ornament. Built on a shared reference palette, screens pair deep, warm noir surfaces in menus and HUDs, and near-black charcoal in the investigative panels, with warm gold headings, muted parchment body text, and bevelled keycap badges. Semantic accents consistently encode agency and state: **slate blue** marks Detective Julian Stoker and Inquiry mode; **warm amber** marks witnesses, suspects, and Captain Miller; **magenta** marks Confrontation mode; **burnt amber-gold** flags warnings, pending timers, and unseen evidence; and **cyan** isolates computer access tapes.

Typographically, the computer terminal uses a monospaced typeface (Anonymous Pro) to give institutional database queries the character of a terminal, while the dialogue, inventory, inspect, and archive panels rely on widely spaced uppercase headers, small badges, readable body text, and large display titles, paired with custom click and grab cursors that reinforce tactile card handling. On the technical side, four core gameplay panels (the archive, dialogue panel, free-roam HUD, and computer terminal) share a scalable configuration and are rescaled to screen width from the settings menu. Because early UI Toolkit compatibility limited CSS custom properties across stylesheets, the shared palette values were copied directly into each panel's stylesheet—a pragmatic trade-off that guaranteed reliable rendering at the cost of keeping the stylesheets in sync by hand. (Exact colour values, type sizes, and panel dimensions are given in Appendix X.9.)

#### 8.2.8 Interface Limitations and Summary

Taken together, the interface translates the thesis's core mechanics into tangible interactions—turning slots into conversational instruments, folders and notes into externalized theory-building tools, and the HUD clock into felt _kairos_ pressure—while strictly withholding confirmation of whether the player's final deduction is right. Four implementation limitations remain in the evaluated build:

1. **Legacy overlays:** Proximity prompts, slip-event notifications, the locked-room countdown bar, the quotes in the ending sequence, and the ending illustration and credits roll still render with Unity's legacy `OnGUI` rather than UI Toolkit.
2. **Copied stylesheet colours:** Duplicating the palette values across twelve panel stylesheets makes global theme changes a manual task.
3. **Partial UI scaling:** Only the four scalable panels resize with the settings menu in the precinct scene; the inventory, inspect mode, and read mode stay at a fixed scale, and no panels in the main menu are scaled.
