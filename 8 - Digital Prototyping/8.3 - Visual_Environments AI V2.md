### 8.3 Visual Environments

In the prototype, the physical precinct is not a decorative backdrop; it is part of the epistemic system. Space gates access to files, distributes clues across institutional stations, stages shifting social encounters, and imposes temporal friction on every movement between leads. Answering the open threads from Chapter 7 (§7.1, §7.2.3, and §7.4.1), this section details how the tabletop floor plan was translated into a navigable 3D precinct, how doors and room-level visibility make game state legible in space, and how camera framing, lighting, and the auxiliary scenes (the dream, the ending, and the main menu) shape play. Scene and object names are listed in Appendix X.11.

#### 8.3.1 From Four Tabletop Rooms to a Navigable Precinct

The Chapter 6 tabletop workshops relied on four abstract spaces drawn on paper: the Evidence Room, Corridor, Observation Room, and Interrogation Room (Chapter 6, Figure 38). During those rehearsals, participants repeatedly tested doors and asked to inspect adjacent areas that were not yet modelled (§7.1). To satisfy that spatial curiosity while keeping solo-project scope bounded, the digital prototype preserves the four core rooms and expands the precinct floor to **six rooms**, plus the jail cells of the ending scene:

1. **Evidence Room**
2. **Corridor**
3. **Observation Room**
4. **Interrogation Room**
5. **Bathroom** — added to give the player an isolated, non-public space for administering Somnoryl-μ.
6. **Locked Room** — added as Detective Thorne's sealed case archive, housing the deeper paper trail and the physical collapse sequence.

Following the scoping principle set out in §7.1, every location exists to serve a specific narrative and epistemic function rather than to pad floor space. During pre-production, a purely 2D visual-novel presentation was rejected for three reasons: it could not easily convey the spatial scale of a precinct, it demanded unsustainable 2D character and background illustration variants as suspects and room states changed, and it removed self-directed spatial exploration. Adopting a real-time 3D **isometric-style** perspective—an elevated three-quarter view rather than a rigid, fixed classic orthographic projection—offered an economical alternative: a single modular environment could support free movement, physical sightlines, diegetic clue placement, and reusable character animations within a realistic solo development budget.

> Each navigable scene  (`Dream_Sequence`, `Ending_Sequence`, `GeometryAttempt`), was configured with to have really dark scenes, with point lights to illuminate each room, light probes to illuminate the character objects dynamically even with baked lightening. All objects don't have any textures assigned besides really specific ones like, the character objects, and some objects that need to be a little bit different to have better visibility, the interactable ones have a orange color texture to them, and the rest has the base lit Unity texture. Because of the limited time, scope and being a solo developer, the decision was to assume the prototype look and save the textures and final lightning to future improvements, focusing on the aspects that my research was validating. Even without any textures assigned, the look and feel still matches the view I was going for, with the lightning doing most of the work, the yellow lights and ambience sound are meant to explore the audiovisual look and feel of the noire aesthetic. 


> In the Ending_Sequence scene and the Geometry Attempt one, for character's that are able to walk around the precinct, including the player character, footstep sounds were added, with a script that randomizes the pitch and sound that would play for each step. The sound was gathered from the Freesound being a simple "Footsteps in concrete" sound, with variants audio samples to make each step sound different. 

> In the Dream sequence scene it was instead assigned a watery footstep sounds, to sound different from the other scenes, and give that infinite ocean feel.

##### The Precinct

**Evidence Room:** The player begins the investigation here, framed as Detective Julian Stoker's workspace. It exposes the primary investigative tools: the computer terminal, a tape container holding the four institutional access tapes, a physical evidence box containing six foundational items (among them the revolver, the fired projectile, the hoodie, and the Somnoryl-μ vials), a resting chair where the player can wait, a document folder that opens directly in read mode, and a window casting real-time blue exterior light, paired with spatial rain audio whose volume rises as Julian approaches the glass and fades as he moves away. While the Evidence Room opens freely onto the Corridor, it withholds the Locked Room until the player acquires the keys.

**Corridor:** The Corridor functions as the spatial hub connecting all five interior rooms and ending at the stairs door. It exposes no standalone forensic items; instead, it mediates movement and houses the precinct's exit. Trying the stairs door before the final confrontation has reached its verdict plays a fallback interaction telling the player the case is not yet ready, withholding the transition to the ending until the investigation reaches closure.

> The corridor has a specific office soundscape that is louder the closer the player gets to the lower not interactable door south of the Observation Room, aiming to give the idea that the precinct extends further than the floor the player is able to explore. 

**Observation Room:** Positioned beside the Interrogation Room, the Observation Room represents the station's institutional channel. It stages Captain Miller's presence and Officer Buckley, who offers guidance, alongside a second wait station (a printer) and a document folder that opens in read mode. Visually overlooking the interrogation space, it reinforces the procedural hierarchy watching over Stoker's work.

**Interrogation Room:** Rather than remaining static, the Interrogation Room's contents change across play. Its door stays shut until a suspect has been brought in. When Elara or Kae is summoned, the seating and room-presence systems coordinate their walk-in, their seated idle, and their eviction when suspects are swapped. Alongside the active suspect, the room holds a document folder, a table, chairs, and a cassette recorder.

> This scene contains a light bulb ambience sound, to give the room the background sound that matches the yellow lightning of the room, being mostly silent with only the lights being audible. 


**Bathroom:** The Bathroom is sealed and can be opened either legitimately with the keys or forced open by combining the Somnoryl-μ vials with the Auto Injector. Inside, it offers only a flushable toilet and privacy. Epistemically, however, its isolation is critical: on waking from the dream, the game checks which room the player is in. Taking the drug inside the Bathroom returns Julian safely to the investigation; taking it in any public room triggers Miller's angry sequence and forces an immediate confrontation.

**Locked Room:** Opened with the keys requested from Miller (or offering an optional action to write a note to the captain), the Locked Room acts as Thorne's neglected archive rather than a mechanical puzzle box. It holds 25 dossier and forensic records, two prior case folders, shelving, filing cabinets, and suspended dust. Crossing its threshold launches Julian's collapse and the 25-second adrenaline-injection countdown (Section 8.1.6), turning spatial discovery into embodied narrative jeopardy.

##### Doors as State Made Visible

Doors in the precinct translate the flag architecture of Section 8.1 into immediate spatial feedback. Locked doors do not open by walking into them; they open only through world-object interactions that require specific equipped evidence (such as the keys, or the vials and the injector), and those interactions set the flag the door is waiting for. Three doors—to the Locked Room, the Interrogation Room, and the Bathroom—listen for their flag and swing open smoothly when it is set; if the flag is already set when the scene starts, for instance after loading a save, the door simply starts open.

Proximity readability is handled by an outline that appears on an object when Julian comes within range of it. Once a door has opened, or a one-time interaction has been used up, its outline is permanently switched off. In this way, the golden highlight works as a state indicator in the world: an outline on a doorframe tells the player the door is still locked, while its absence confirms that the way is already open.

> In this scene specifically there's also a yellow outline configured, for when the player is closer to certain object that they are able to interact with, this outline is from a free Unity extension, this is intended to give direct visual feedback of the object the player is actually going to interact with, similar to Disco Elysium, it's not assigned to any character object.

> Another thing this outline was configured to and helps the player with, is by having the outline appear when a player is close to specific opened door that are not visible due to the isometric perspective, aiming to be easeally findable in the navigable space and to enter certain rooms, even if not visible. 

##### Seeing Only Where You Are: Room-Level Visibility

To prevent players from inspecting rooms they have not entered and to keep rendering lightweight, the game shows only one room at a time, starting in the Evidence Room. Trigger volumes in every doorway detect the player crossing a threshold and switch the visible room.

Strict single-room display, however, would cut off the view through open doorways. Each doorway trigger can therefore keep a neighbouring room visible and hide specific objects: stepping into the Interrogation Room keeps the Observation Room visible; stepping into the Bathroom keeps the Corridor visible; and crossing into the Locked Room keeps the Evidence Room visible. Unentered rooms are capped by dedicated dark covering objects inside each room. Because unentered rooms are switched off and not rendered, these dark caps cover the edges so the player sees only a dark void rather than raw geometry seams. When a doorway trigger reveals an adjacent room, it hides the blocking cover, and restores it as soon as the player leaves. On returning from the dream, the player wakes directly inside the room where the injection took place.

#### Space and Time

Because the internal clock runs in real time while it is running (one in-game hour per real hour), moving through the precinct consumes the 24-hour *kairos* window introduced in Section 8.1.6. Walking between the Evidence Room, Corridor, and Observation Room costs only seconds of game time, yet repeated backtracking adds up—while the major temporal leaps remain deliberate waits at the chair and the printer, pending laboratory delays, and the six-hour penalty on returning from the dream. Together, room gating and the running clock turn physical movement into resource management, reinforced visually by the persistent clock HUD discussed in Section 8.2.2.

#### Light, Shadow, and Atmosphere

The precinct has no directional (sun) light; it is lit entirely by small groups of local lights in each room (two to four per room), paired with a light probe group per room. In the Evidence Room, this interior warmth is contrasted by real-time blue light spilling through the exterior window. #### 8.3.5 Camera and Framing

The camera follows Julian from an elevated three-quarter angle with a slight forward offset, rather than a rigid two-axis isometric lock. It follows lazily, with a small dead zone and light smoothing, so short adjustments at a workstation feel grounded rather than jerky. Player movement is mapped onto the camera's horizontal axes, so pressing *up* always moves Julian away from the camera.

The scroll wheel zooms between a close and a wide framing, and zoom is disabled whenever a panel is open or player control is locked. During suspect dialogue and the locked-room fall sequence, the camera shifts slightly toward the conversation partner and moves in to about two-thirds of the default framing. It never zooms *out* if the player was already closer, and it restores the player's previous zoom once the interaction ends. (Camera values in Appendix X.11.)
