# 8.3 Interface Design: what to write in each section

The thread running through 8.3 is that the interface is where the design principles become something the player can touch. Each section below says what to cover, in the order I'd write it.

## 8.3.1 The interface as the interrogation instrument

- **Opening claim:** the dialogue panel is the game's core mechanic made visible, not a menu around it. Say why the two evidence slots sit inside the panel rather than in a separate screen.
- **How the question list is built:** the list of available questions is recomputed every time a slot changes. Describe what this does to the player. They arrange evidence and then see what the character is willing to be asked.
- **Three ways to fill a slot:** dragging a card in (via the drag ghost), clicking, or using the quick menu. The quick menu offers either the equipped folder or the full inventory, switched with H. Explain why you built several routes (speed during a conversation, without leaving it).
- **Confrontation as a mode change:** it restyles the whole panel with a magenta accent (#904C77), so the player feels the shift without being told. Contrast it with Inquiry's detective blue and the character's amber.
- **Question buttons as a visual vocabulary:** chain, standalone, resume, action, and locked (dimmed, red edge). Explain what each type tells the player about what they can do.
- **The conversation stream:** it is collapsed by default, with only the newest bubble visible, and expands on click. Justify this as keeping attention on the current exchange.
- **Figure:** a dialogue panel capture with both slots filled, plus a confrontation capture beside it.

## 8.3.2 One panel grammar

- **The claim:** dialogue, object interaction ("INTERACTION"), narrative choices ("NARRATIVE") and forced confrontation are four modes of one panel, not four screens.
- **Why it matters:** the player learns the grammar once. Evidence is always handled the same way, whether they are questioning a suspect, using an object or reaching a forced moment with Miller.
- **What changes per mode:** the header, the content area, whether choices appear, and whether the player can leave. Forced mode removes the exit and the toggle, and you can tie that to the Miller sequences.
- **Reuse:** the computer terminal uses the same slot component, drag handler and evidence cards. Present this as a deliberate decision, not a coincidence.
- **Figure:** the same panel in all four modes, side by side.

## 8.3.3 Evidence as a workspace

- **The claim:** the player has to hold, organise and re-read evidence, so the interface supports reasoning between conversations, not only during them.
- **Inventory:** folders, the card grid, and the new-folder, rename, delete and cycle actions. Mention the 61 evidence items as the scale this has to handle.
- **Inspect mode:** a detail view with per-item notes (288 characters, saved with the game). Notes are the one place the player records their own interpretation, and the game never evaluates them. That ties directly to the ambiguity principle.
- **Read mode:** for documents, with zoom from 0.5× to 4× (Ctrl+wheel), double-click for 2×, and page navigation. Explain why legibility of in-world documents matters for a detective game.
- **Archive (L):** session history per character, slot badges showing what was presented, and "USED" chips on evidence. Position it as the player's external memory of what they have already tried.
- **Navigation:** shortcuts that work across panels (Q/E to cycle, F to inspect, R to read), and hover plus key acting on the hovered card.
- **Figures:** inventory with folders, inspect with a note, read mode zoomed, and an Archive session. These are four captures.

## 8.3.4 Layering and focus

- **The problem:** several panels can be open, the player character must not walk around under them, and Esc must always do something sensible.
- **Layering:** the sorting-order stack, from the HUD (3) up through dialogue (5), inventory (10), inspect (20) and read (30), to the fader and save indicator at the top. Present it as a small table or diagram.
- **Modal counter:** `UIPanelTracker` locks the player while any panel is open and releases them when the last one closes.
- **Esc ownership:** `GlobalEscapeManager` is the single owner. The top closable panel closes first, the dialogue panel opts out (so Esc cannot abandon a conversation by accident), and pause toggles only when nothing else is open.
- **Safety net:** the 3-second stuck-counter auto-reset. Frame it as a defensive decision, and say honestly that it exists because a panel that fails to release the lock would soft-lock the game.
- **Figure:** a layering diagram.

## 8.3.5 Feedback: signalled and withheld

This is the section where the ambiguity principle meets the interface, so be precise about the line.

- **What the interface does signal:** a gold border on unseen evidence, pending evidence at 45% opacity with ⏳ and the time remaining, locked actions dimmed with a red edge, and the terminal's "No matching record found for this combination."
- **Procedural feedback about relevance:** "Evidence out of range — patience reduced" and the slip-event badges.
- **Where that feedback sits:** the dialogue badges are hidden while the stream is collapsed (the default), but always visible in the Archive. So the information exists for a player who goes looking.
- **What it never does:** it never says whether the player's overall theory is right, and never rates the final verdict. State the distinction plainly: the system says whether a move was procedurally relevant, not whether the interpretation is true.
- **Caveat to settle first:** the dialogue badge reads "SLIP EVENT {id}", which looks like a debug leftover. Decide whether it stays as written before you describe it as a design choice. Say it was deliberate only if it is.
- **Figure:** an expanded stream with a warning badge, next to the Archive view of the same badges.

## 8.3.6 Time in the interface

- **The HUD:** wall time starting at 3:30 PM, the countdown, a status (RUNNING, PAUSED, WARNING, EXPIRED) and a bar. Use the scene values: 24h total, a warning at 12h.
- **Restraint:** it stays hidden until the clock starts, and hides while the inventory is open or during NPC approaches. Argue that the clock is felt, not stared at.
- **The time panel:** a 1–24h slider with fades. Explain that skipping time is a decision the player makes and pays for, and that the panel is rejected if the clock isn't running or has expired.
- **Delayed evidence:** the pending cards in the inventory are the interface side of the 8.1 time argument. Cross-reference 8.1.5.
- **Figure:** the HUD in its running and warning states.

## 8.3.7 Visual language

- **Palette:** noir backgrounds (#0E0C0A to #302C20), gold text (#DCC89B), detective blue for the player, amber for characters, and warning amber (#C88C3C).
- **Meaning, not decoration:** each colour carries a role (who is speaking, what mode you're in, what needs attention). Say which role each has.
- **Implementation:** UI Toolkit, with one theme block copied into each USS file. Be honest here, because it links to the limitations below.
- **Typography and spacing:** I haven't read this in the USS yet, so confirm the fonts and sizes from the files or your own notes before writing it.
- **Figure:** a palette strip with the role of each colour.

## 8.3.8 becomes a short closing bridge
**Summarise the interface in one claim:** it makes the design principles tangible (evidence as a physical resource, the slot constraint, time pressure) while withholding any verdict on the player’s theory.
