## Chapter 8: Digital Prototyping

This chapter documents the implementation of the digital prototype that translates the conceptual, narrative, and formal specifications of Chapters 4 through 7 into a playable single-player detective game. Where Chapter 4 proposed the five core epistemic mechanics, Chapter 5 authored the Oakhaven Central case model, Chapter 6 rehearsed those interactions with printed artifacts in tabletop workshops, and Chapter 7 formalized the Petri-net state machine, Logic Chain dialogue model, and early interface layouts, the task of digital prototyping was to bind those strands into a coherent interactive build. Crucially, the prototype had to preserve interpretive agency under solo-player conditions—making evidence organization, two-slot experimentation, social friction, and time pressure immediately legible without automating deduction or confirming whether the player's theory is correct.

As a solo development effort completed within a bounded production window, every technical and visual choice reflects deliberate scoping. Rather than building an expansive city, procedural dialogue generation, or bespoke 2D visual-novel illustrations for every state, the prototype concentrates its complexity into four Unity scenes: a six-room precinct where the investigation takes place, a focused surreal dream chamber, a repurposed cell wing for the ending, and an introductory menu (scene names are listed in Appendix X.1). Combinatorial inference is bounded to pairs of evidence across two equipped slots, and narrative variation is driven by reusable state flags rather than branching scripts—allowing depth of investigative ambiguity without unmanageable authoring overhead.

The prototype was built in **Unity 6000.3 LTS (6000.3.10f1)** on three technical pillars:

- **Data assets (`ScriptableObject`s):** All 61 evidence items, dialogue chains, standalone interactions, one-shot narrative events, terminal unlock rules, world actions, and ending paths are authored as modular data assets, edited through a custom node-graph editor and audited with offline extraction tools.
    
- **UI Toolkit:** The investigative HUD, the two-slot dialogue panel, the inventory and folder workspace, inspect and read modes, the computer terminal, the conversation archive, and the pause and settings menus are built in Unity's UI Toolkit, with a few secondary overlays still drawn by Unity's legacy `OnGUI` system.
    
- **NavMesh navigation:** Character entrances, interrogation-room seating swaps, and Captain Miller's timed confrontations move characters through the 3D precinct, alongside a custom isometric-style camera and a per-room visibility system.

## 8.1 System Architecture

### 8.1.1 Architectural Stance

The digital prototype is structured around a strict separation between what the game knows (_authored data_), what the player has done (_runtime state_), and how the two communicate (_flags_). Rather than embedding narrative logic inside scene scripts or character behaviours, all case knowledge is authored as immutable data assets: the 61 evidence items; the dialogue structures (multi-step logic chains, standalone evidence interactions, and linear narrative sequences); one-shot narrative events; world-object definitions and their actions; computer-terminal unlock rules; narrative and suppression rules; and the four ending paths with their dialogue overrides. Runtime changes—acquired and pending evidence, equipped slots, remaining hours, folder contents, conversation histories, and active flags—never modify these assets; they live exclusively inside a small set of runtime managers.

To prevent coupling as new interactions and consequences were authored, narrative subsystems do not call one another directly. Instead, they read from and write to a single shared vocabulary of named flags and one-shot _slip events_, held by one component, `NarrativeStateManager`. Evidence acquisition, world triggers such as doors and the dream entrance, NPC behaviours (entering rooms, approaching the player, forcing confrontations, changing posture), the narrative director, audio, and ending resolution all operate independently: each either asks whether a flag is set or subscribes to a notification when flags change or slip events fire. An evidence pickup or dialogue exchange simply emits a flag or slip event; any world state that depends on it reacts without the emitter needing to know it exists.

Because the prototype spans four scenes—the main menu, the precinct investigation, the dream sequence, and the closing ending sequence—runtime state must survive scene changes. The managers that hold investigation state (narrative flags, evidence, folders, slots, the clock, pending evidence, ending resolution, saving, audio, and the pause and settings UI) are therefore grouped under a single persistent object that is carried across scene loads. Scene-specific infrastructure—combination evaluation, dialogue retrieval, slip-event handling, interrogation-room seating, the narrative director, sequence controllers, local UI, and each character's knowledge log—is rebuilt with each scene (full split in Appendix X.1). This allows the player to enter the dream or move to the station cells in the ending while keeping their accumulated flags, inventory, clock state, and resolved ending verdict.

4. Why this split between authored data, runtime state and flags? Did an earlier version put logic in scene scripts and break? (R)

> The split is three-way rather than two-way because authored data and runtime state differ in lifetime and ownership, not merely in kind. Authored data is the case as designed: identical across playthroughs, shared by every save, and required to remain unchanged after a run ends so that the next run encounters the same case. Runtime state is the case as played: per-save, continuously mutated, and discarded between runs. Collapsing the two would force one of two failures — either play mutates the case in order to record itself, corrupting the corpus for every subsequent run, or the case is frozen into each save, producing a different game each time it is written. The separation is what allows a single authored corpus to support many investigations without the corpus itself drifting. 
> 
> Flags, rather than direct references or event subscriptions, are the communication layer because the precinct is freely navigable: any subsystem may need any other subsystem's state at an arbitrary moment, in an order the designer cannot predict. Direct calls couple emitter to listener and force the emitter to know every downstream consequence. Event subscriptions are temporal — a listener that begins after an event has fired has missed it. Flags are declarative: "is this true?" can be asked at any time and returns the same answer regardless of when the underlying change occurred. That property is what makes order-independent investigation possible without a central scheduler deciding what to evaluate and when. Slip events exist to carry the small set of transitions that _are_ temporal — one-shot consequences that must not repeat — so that the flag layer is not asked to express logic it cannot hold. 
> 
> The split was specified before implementation rather than reached by refactoring a coupled version. The justification was anticipatory: in a tightly coupled script network a failure is located by reading across systems, whereas in a flag model it is located by inspecting which flag failed to set. That is the legibility argument of 7.2.1, applied at the level of the whole build rather than the level of a single interaction.

### 8.1.2 The Two-Slot System as a Constraint

At the centre of the prototype's interaction design is a deliberate structural ceiling: at any moment, the player can equip at most two pieces of evidence, into Slot A and Slot B, managed by `SlotManager`. Carrying forward the cardboard two-slot board tested in the Chapter 6 workshops, the slot manager prevents the same item from occupying both slots and rejects items still awaiting laboratory processing. Rather than building a general-purpose deduction engine capable of parsing arbitrary sets of clues, every combination rule in the codebase—whether a node in a dialogue chain, a standalone evidence interaction, or a terminal unlock rule—is evaluated against at most two equipped items. Rules that list more than two items use an _anchor-plus-partner_ convention: the first listed item acts as the anchor, and any one of the remaining items satisfies the partner condition. Limiting rules to pairs keeps the authoring space tractable and ensures the interface remains compact and legible during play.

Equally important is that this single two-slot primitive serves as the unified input verb across five otherwise distinct systems in the game:

1. **Suspect and Captain dialogue:** The equipped pair determines which inquiry or confrontation lines surface in conversation.
2. **World objects:** Doors and other objects in the precinct check the same slots. The locked-room door requires the keys; the bathroom door requires the Somnoryl-μ vials together with the Auto Injector.
3. **The evidence processing terminal:** Forensic requests pair an institutional access tape with a piece of evidence or a case record. Pairing the Forensics Laboratory tape with the fired projectile, for example, unlocks the Ballistics Examination Report.
4. **The locked-room emergency:** During the locked-room collapse countdown, survival depends on equipping Julian's Auto Injector together with his Adrenaline Vial.
5. **The dream sequence:** Entering the dream state requires equipping the Auto Injector together with the Somnoryl-μ vials.

Because every investigative, technical, and embodied action passes through the same slot bar, the player learns one consistent gesture—_equipping up to two objects to express an intent_—and carries that literacy across the entire precinct.

Epistemically, the two-slot constraint forces the player to hypothesise a specific relation between two items rather than clicking through an exhaustive list of owned clues. When the player equips two items together in dialogue, the system reinforces this intentionality through **hierarchy suppression** rather than additive clutter. Any item already claimed by an active logic-chain option suppresses standalone questions about that single item, and any matching interaction whose triggers are a strict subset of a broader match in the same evaluation is removed. The same rule operates inside individual chain nodes. For example, if the player equips Kae's Firearm Evidence Record alone, a standalone question about gun ownership surfaces; if they equip it alongside the Ballistics Examination Report, the paired question dominates and suppresses both single-item prompts. Showing more evidence narrows the conversation to the specific synthesis the player has proposed, rather than adding noise.

The primary trade-off of this constraint is authoring labour: because inference is not computed dynamically from semantic tags, every meaningful pair must be explicitly authored as a chain node, interaction, or unlock rule—a limitation returned to in Section 8.1.7.

### 8.1.3 From Proposal to Response: Logic Chains and Social Friction

Section 7.3 defined the dialogue model at a conceptual level. The player frames a _conversational proposal_ from up to two items and a mode. The system reads that proposal against authored _logic chains_, and suspects yield or resist according to their _patience_ and the _strength_ of the case built against them. This section describes how the build realizes that model: how a proposal is read and ranked, how a chain is authored, how characters remember and resist, and where the evaluation deliberately stops. Where the implementation departs from the Chapter 7 plan, the departure is stated.

In the evaluated build, 23 chains are authored for Elara, 30 for Kae, and 20 for Captain Miller (15 for the final confrontation and 5 for his warnings). _[Author note: counted from the chain folders, excluding the "Unused Chains" folders. Confirm that every one is registered in the chain database, and add node and question totals.]_ Against this, the 61 evidence items allow 1,830 distinct pairs, so the authored chains cover a deliberately small part of the space of possible proposals.

#### Reading a proposal

A conversation opens when the player approaches a character and chooses Inquiry or Confrontation. Inside the panel, the mode can be switched at any time, and every change to either slot triggers a fresh evaluation. Each evaluation considers only the chains authored for that character, and only those currently available. A chain can be unavailable because a suppression rule has retired it, because it waits for particular flags, because it waits until specific earlier steps have been visited, or because the player has already completed it.

What is considered also depends on where the player stands. Outside any chain, every available chain whose next step fits the equipped evidence offers its questions, so one proposal can open several lines of reasoning at once. Once the player chooses a question from a chain, they are _inside_ that chain, and every other chain is set aside until the current one ends, breaks, or is left.

For each candidate step, the equipped evidence falls into one of the three categories of evidential fit defined in 7.3.2:

- Evidence that matches the step's required items surfaces its **primary** question.
- Evidence that matches the triggers of one of the step's other questions surfaces that **ranged** question. A step can carry several ranged questions, each approaching the same argument through a different document.
- If nothing fits and the player is already inside the chain, the step surfaces its **weak question**. This is the only case in which an out-of-range proposal produces anything at all; outside a chain, evidence that fits nothing simply produces no chain question.

Alongside the chains, each character has standalone _evidence interactions_ (7.3.2): baseline reactions to an item or a pair that never cost patience. An interaction is hidden whenever a chain question already answers the same evidence. Individual interaction questions can also be hidden while a chain is active, held back until a flag is set, retired once they have been heard, or used to start a delayed processing request (Section 8.1.6).

Every surfaced option is then placed in a single ranking, from the most specific to the most generic:

1. a bookmarked chain the player can resume;
2. a chain step whose primary question requires a pair;
3. a ranged chain question that requires a pair;
4. single-item chain questions, primary before ranged;
5. narrative chains that need no evidence;
6. evidence interactions: first those with no trigger, then paired interactions, then single-item interactions;
7. the weak question, always last.

**Hierarchy suppression** reinforces the ranking. When a question that requires a pair is satisfied, it removes any other question on the same step whose matched evidence is a strict subset of that pair; the same rule applies among evidence interactions. For example, if the player equips Kae's Firearm Evidence Record alone, a standalone question about gun ownership surfaces. If they equip it alongside the Ballistics Examination Report, the paired question takes over and suppresses both single-item prompts. Showing more evidence therefore narrows the conversation to the specific synthesis the player has proposed, rather than adding noise.

#### Authoring a chain

A chain is an ordered list of steps, and each step belongs to one mode. Progress always moves to the first step that has not yet been visited. If that step belongs to the other mode, the chain cannot continue until the player switches. The panel shows this without revealing the answer: when the chain's next step is in the other mode and no question fits the equipped evidence, it offers a single action, _"Confront [character] with the evidence"_ or _"Step back and question [character]"_. As 7.3.2 anticipated, moving an argument forward can therefore require the player to change register in the middle of it.

Each step is authored from a small set of fields:

|Field|What it does|
|:--|:--|
|**Required evidence**|Surfaces the primary question. In _any_ mode, one listed item is enough. In _all_ mode, the first item is an anchor and any one of the other listed items completes the pair (the anchor-plus-partner convention of 8.1.2).|
|**Related evidence**|The wider set of items the step treats as part of its argument. A ranged question can open a chain only if its trigger is also related to the step.|
|**Questions**|One primary question and any number of ranged questions, each with its own trigger list and the same _any_ / _all_ choice.|
|**Responses**|Each question has a base response, heard the first time, and an optional variation heard on later visits. Both are split into pages.|
|**Non-advancing marker**|Lets a question be answered without moving the chain on.|
|**Slip event**|A one-shot consequence attached to a question (8.1.5).|
|**Weak question**|A question template plus two diminished responses, used when the player's evidence does not fit.|
|**Strength contribution**|How much answering the step adds to the character's strength; it defaults by mode and can be overridden per step.|

> **Table 8.X — Authoring Fields of a Chain Step.**

Three text conventions extend what a single step can express without duplicating it. First, an `{evidence}` token in a question is replaced with the names of the items that surfaced it, joined by "+". A question written as `[Show {evidence}]` therefore appears as, for example, _"[Show Elara Medical Record + Meredith Gable Witness Statement]"_, which makes the act of presenting the documents part of the detective's line. Second, a `{FLAG_…}` token keeps the question hidden until that flag is set. This lets one step reward knowledge established elsewhere with a sharper version of the same question. Third, an `[EXIT]` marker in a response ends the conversation after that line, so a character can close an exchange within the fiction.

The weak question is the most flexible of these tools. As noted in 7.3.2, it can serve three purposes:

- it can steer, phrasing a deliberately vague challenge that points towards the kind of evidence the step expects;
- it can cover a combination the author saw players attempt but chose not to reward;
- it can give in-fiction feedback that the proposal does not fit, without the interface saying so.

Finally, a chain as a whole can be restricted to one character, held back until named flags are set, or held back until particular steps of other chains have been visited. Narrative chains, which surface without any evidence, are used for exchanges the story needs regardless of the player's proposal.

#### Memory, bookmarks, and leaving a chain

Each character keeps their own record of the conversation (the _suspect memory_ of 7.3.2). When the player chooses a question that advances a chain, several things happen at once: the player enters the chain if they were not already inside it, the step is marked as visited, a bookmark records the question and the evidence that surfaced it, and the character's strength may rise. Returning to a step already heard plays its variation response. When a chain begins, the camera also moves closer to the character, a small spatial cue that a line of questioning is now under way. Slip events attached to chain questions fire as soon as the question is chosen. Those attached to standalone interactions wait until the character's final response page has been read.

A chain is left in one of four ways:

- the player completes its final step;
- two consecutive missteps break it;
- the player ends the conversation;
- with no evidence equipped, the player chooses _"Try a different line of questioning"_.

Breaking a chain is not the same as erasing it. Visited steps stay visited, so when the player later re-enters, the chain picks up at the first step not yet heard, provided the equipped evidence fits that step. The bookmark offers a shortcut back. When the player opens a conversation with empty slots and outside any chain, each unfinished chain whose bookmarked step matches the current mode is offered as _"Let's get back to [chain]. [last question]"_. Choosing it re-equips the evidence that originally surfaced the question and replays that exchange with its variation response, re-establishing context before the argument continues.

Completion is final unless the author says otherwise. When the last step of a chain is a confrontation whose question fires its slip event, the chain closes for that character. Only a reset package (8.1.4) can reopen it, and a reopened chain is replayed with variation responses.

#### Social friction: patience and strength

**Patience** is a value between zero and one held by each character. It falls only when the player chooses a weak question. For Elara and Kae, a weak question in Confrontation costs twice as much as one in Inquiry, and Miller's confrontation missteps cost more still (values in Appendix X.5). A separate counter tracks consecutive missteps. The first weak question earns the step's first diminished response; the second in a row earns the second diminished response and breaks the chain. Any question that fits resets the counter.

Because weak questions exist only inside chains, patience is spent only when the player has committed to a line of reasoning and then failed to support it. Exploring combinations outside a chain costs no patience at all. The social cost therefore attaches to arguing badly, not to looking.

When patience reaches zero, the conversation closes at once and a cooldown begins, measured in in-game hours (Section 8.1.6). While it runs, trying to talk to the character plays a short fallback scene instead of opening a conversation. When it ends, patience is fully restored, and for Miller a flag fires that lets reset packages reopen his confrontation loops. Each depletion is counted. The second depletion of a character sends Miller to warn the player, and reaching a character's limit triggers Miller's angry confrontation, after which no further cooldown starts.

**Strength** is a whole number held by each character that only ever grows. Each advancing question contributes once, ever: by default one point in Confrontation and none in Inquiry, though an author can override this per step. Resumed exchanges, non-advancing questions, and weak questions contribute nothing. Strength is not displayed in the dialogue panel. _[Author note: confirm it is not shown anywhere else.]_ It is read by the flag-driven narrative rules of 8.1.4, which can require a character's strength to reach a threshold before a scripted beat plays. A beat triggered by strength waits until the conversation has closed, and every narrative rule waits while another panel is open, so scripted interruptions never cut into an answer.

The two counters are deliberately asymmetric. Patience is local, social, and recoverable: a bad run costs time, then resets. Strength is cumulative and argumentative: pressure, once applied, is never lost. A careless player loses hours; a careful one accumulates a case.

#### A worked example: Elara and the barefoot witness

The two-step chain in which the detective confronts Elara with evidence that places her barefoot at the scene shows these mechanisms working together.

The first step is an Inquiry. Its primary question requires a pair, anchored on Meredith Gable's witness statement and completed by Elara's medical record, the foot-trace analysis, or one other related document. Its sharpest version, which ties the barefoot figure to the hoodie Elara shares with Kae, appears only once ownership of that hoodie has been established. Until then, a ranged question built on the same pair asks more plainly why she was found barefoot with cut feet. Other ranged questions open the same step from different angles, for instance through the crime-scene photo's custody record, or by raising the rain-soaked hoodie found in her hamper. One further question, asking simply whether she was barefoot when officers found her, is marked as non-advancing: Elara answers, but the chain does not move. Choosing any advancing question enters the chain, marks the step as visited, and fires a slip event that records Elara's barefoot presence as a narrative flag, which other chains and later dialogue can then read.

The second step is a Confrontation. In Inquiry mode the chain is now blocked, and the panel offers to _"Confront ELARA with the evidence"_. The step requires Elara's medical record paired with either the witness statement or the witness's 911 call. If the player equips something that fits none of the step's questions, the weak question appears: the detective shows the items and tells her to _"really look at the evidence."_ The first time, Elara answers that she does not understand where the detective is going. The second time in a row, she breaks down (_"I don't know what you want me to say!"_), the conversation ends, and the chain breaks, at a cost of a quarter of her patience each time. If instead the player presents a fitting pair, Elara admits that she took the Moon drug to escape that night. Because this step is authored to contribute three points rather than the default one, her strength rises by three, and because it is the chain's final confrontation, the chain closes.

#### Where the evaluation stops

The evaluator decides only whether a proposal is _relevant_ to a step the author has written. It never compares the proposal with a model of the truth: no representation of who committed the crime is consulted when questions are surfaced, options are ranked by specificity rather than correctness, and a weak question means "this does not support the argument in progress", not "this is false". Competing hypotheses are authored as chains in their own right; both suspects, for example, have a chain arguing that their height matches the shooter's. _[Author note: confirm this example, or choose another pair of competing chains.]_ Whether a theory is right is never settled inside a conversation. It is settled, if at all, by the ending paths of 8.1.4, which read the flags the player's chosen arguments have set.

That boundary has limits, and they should be stated plainly. The fact that a question appears at all tells the player that the author anticipated this connection. The camera move on entering a chain, the slip-event notification, and the accompanying sound and music cues all confirm that a move mattered locally. These signals are examined in Section 8.2.5. And because exploring outside a chain is free, a determined player can probe the authored space by trying pairs until something surfaces. The only cost is the real time it takes, and dragging cards rather than clicking them (7.4.2) was meant to add friction against exactly this. _[Author note: report in Chapter 9 whether playtesters probed combinations this way.]_

#### Departures from the Chapter 7 plan

Three differences between the planned model and the build are worth recording:

- **Related evidence now advances.** In 7.3.2, related evidence was to keep an argument alive without moving it forward. In the build, any chain question, primary or ranged, advances its step unless explicitly marked as non-advancing. Ranged questions therefore became alternative routes through the same step, and holding moves became an explicit authoring choice rather than the default.
- **Optional weak questions on entry.** A chain can allow its first step to show the weak question even before the player is inside it, so an author can respond to a near-miss that would otherwise produce nothing.
- **Resuming restores the evidence.** The bookmark not only remembers where a chain stopped but re-equips the items that surfaced the last question, so the player returns to the argument as they left it.

### 8.1.4 Data-Driven Evidence, Dialogue, and Progression

Because the case narrative, clue dependencies, and ending structure continued to evolve throughout development, all narrative content had to remain editable without modifying C# code. Every domain entity in the prototype is therefore authored as a data asset and indexed in a database asset for its type (Appendix X.2). To keep multi-node dialogue maintainable as the corpus grew, custom editor tooling renders logic chains as visual node graphs and structured inspector lists inside the Unity Editor. Adding a new suspect line, revising response text, or introducing an additional ending requires creating or editing assets rather than writing new gameplay logic.

The ending pipeline provides the clearest demonstration of this data-driven architecture. The ending manager (`EndingManager`) holds four ending paths, each with a priority: Kae as murderer with Elara free (100); Kae as murderer with Elara as accomplice (95); Elara as murderer with Kae as accomplice (90); and Elara as murderer with a "gun only" outcome for Kae (85). Each path defines its own flag conditions together with three dialogue sequences: Elara's reaction, Kae's reaction, and an outcome narrative. When the player reaches the stairs at the end of the investigation, the ending manager sorts the paths by descending priority and selects the first whose conditions pass. It then checks two global dialogue overrides: if an override's conditions pass against the active flags, it swaps out whichever of the three sequences it supplies. Conditional variations—such as Kae reacting differently if he was previously pressured, or convicted on a robbery angle—are thus layered onto existing endings without duplicating them.

Progression rules and the retirement of obsolete content follow the same asset-first model, separating _when_ a narrative event happens from the systems that play it:

- **Flag-driven narrative rules:** Each rule pairs flag conditions, and optionally a minimum confrontation strength with a given character, with a priority, a fire-once guard, and a narrative sequence. In the precinct scene, three authored rules watch for flag and strength changes and interrupt free roam to deliver scripted beats when their conditions are met.
- **Suppression rules:** Each lists flag conditions under which obsolete dialogue chains or interactions are disabled once the narrative has moved past them. Both chain and interaction databases filter out suppressed entries before any question is surfaced.
- **Reset packages:** Attached to NPCs, each specifies a trigger flag together with the exact chains to reactivate, flags to clear, and slip events to restore, so that Miller's confrontation and warning loops can reopen after a cooldown.

(The individual rules and packages are named in Appendix X.3.)

The principal engineering cost of a fully data-driven pipeline is the fragility of string identifiers. Because assets reference one another and C# scripts through raw text identifiers—for evidence, flags, slip events, chains, nodes, and interactions—a single spelling discrepancy between an asset field and the code causes a trigger, door, or rule to fail silently at runtime. To mitigate this risk without abandoning flexible asset authoring, three verification practices were built around the project. First, core runtime flags referenced in C# scripts were centralized as named constants rather than duplicated as inline text. Second, startup and runtime diagnostics—a validator that checks required managers on scene load, an in-game diagnostic that tests the currently equipped slots against the dialogue databases, and a runtime debug console—surface missing wiring and evaluation mismatches immediately during playtesting. Third, a suite of custom editor extractors serializes all scene references and authored asset graphs into plain-text manifests. Treating these dumps as an offline audit layer made it possible to inspect every flag, trigger, and asset link across all scenes and catch naming mismatches before playtest builds were frozen (tooling listed in Appendix X.10).

### 8.1.5 Flags: From Evidence to Narrative Consequence

Because the precinct is freely navigable and players choose which threads to pursue first, the prototype cannot rely on linear scripts or hardcoded scene sequences. Instead, it uses a global flag system to ensure that players can gather evidence in any order while the world and characters still react coherently. Every significant state change—acquiring key forensic reports, opening doors, exhausting a suspect's patience, or triggering an accusation—converges on `NarrativeStateManager`, which keeps a runtime set of active flags alongside a record of one-shot events that have already fired.

The bridge between physical clues and narrative state begins at acquisition. Whenever an item is unlocked or acquired, an evidence-to-flag bridge checks a table of authored mappings and writes the corresponding flag. In the precinct scene, eight key forensic and documentary items carry such mappings (Appendix X.3). Flags are also written by world-object actions, terminal unlock rules, completed narrative sequences, and slip events. Once set, flags propagate outward across every subsystem without direct object references:

- **Dialogue gating:** Individual questions within a chain can embed a flag token in their text. The token is stripped from the displayed prompt, and the question stays hidden unless that flag is set. Standalone interactions similarly check required and excluded flags before surfacing.
- **Physical world and NPC behaviour:** Flag-driven doors swing open the locked room, the interrogation room, and the bathroom when their flags are set. Room-presence and seating components move Buckley, Miller, Elara, and Kae in and out of rooms, and animation bindings shift seated postures—for example, switching Elara to a posture of disbelief when she reacts to the gun, or to rubbing her arm once her addiction to Moon comes to light. Miller's warnings and angry confrontations trigger when escalation flags fire.
- **Rule evaluation and endings:** The narrative director fires scripted beats when its rules' conditions are met, and the ending manager resolves the final ending purely from the active flag set.

Crucially, nothing in this architecture depends on acquisition order or direct object-to-object calls. Because subsystems either check whether a condition holds or react when it becomes true, a revelation unlocked through the computer terminal in hour 3 produces the same downstream behaviour as one unlocked in hour 14, and multi-condition rules (such as ending paths or narrative rules) simply wait until all required flags coexist.

To prevent key narrative beats from repeating indefinitely once unlocked, the prototype distinguishes persistent state (_flags_) from atomic transitions (_slip events_). Slip events represent one-shot epistemic turning points—revelations, evidence grants, or irreversible realizations. When the player selects a question that carries a slip event, the dialogue system checks whether that event has already fired for the character (unless the question explicitly allows repeats) and attaches it to the result. Rather than firing mid-sentence, the dialogue panel defers the event until the player finishes reading the character's final response page. Because `NarrativeStateManager` records each event only once, its rewards—unlocking and acquiring follow-up evidence, setting new flags, and displaying a brief notification—execute exactly once. Paired with suppression rules, which retire obsolete chains and interactions once their successor flags are active, slip events ensure that discoveries move the investigation forward rather than looping. (Only when an authored reset package explicitly restores a loop—such as resetting Miller's warning state after a cooldown—is a fired slip event reset.)

Architecturally, this flag-and-slip model is the direct software realization of the Chapter 7 Petri-net state machine. Where Chapter 7 modelled investigation flow using places, tokens, `CHECK_NODE` guards, `OUTPUT_PLACE` artifacts, and `GLOBAL_ROOM_STATE`, the Unity implementation maps those formal constructs onto runtime equivalents while expanding them where 3D space and time required additional control:

|Chapter 7 Petri-Net Construct|Chapter 8 Unity Implementation|Relationship to Design|
|:--|:--|:--|
|**Place / Token** (narrative or system condition)|A named flag in the active flag set|**Kept**, with flag clearing and reset packages added so cooldowns and suspect swaps can retract tokens.|
|**`CHECK_NODE`** (pre-condition guard)|Required evidence on a chain node, flag conditions, and flag tokens embedded in questions|**Kept**; extended with anchor-plus-partner slot matching and per-character strength thresholds.|
|**Transition Firing** (atomic state change)|Slip events|**Kept** as a one-shot gate; deferred until the final response page is read.|
|**`OUTPUT_PLACE`** (produced clue or state)|Evidence unlocks and acquisitions, and flag writes|**Kept**; augmented with delayed delivery when forensic outputs must arrive after a time delay.|
|**`GLOBAL_ROOM_STATE`** (spatial context)|Room-state tracking, flag-driven doors, and NPC room presence|**Diverged** from a single global state into reactive room-visibility triggers and flag-driven doors and seating.|

### 8.1.6 Time as a Constraint

To make investigative choices genuinely costly rather than exhaustive, the prototype binds the precinct investigation to a finite countdown managed by the internal clock (`InternalClockManager`). The clock begins once Captain Miller's opening briefing completes. Although the architecture plan originally budgeted 36 in-game hours, the time window was changed to 24 hours, with a warning at 12 hours remaining, with the time each time related action took being adjusted to fit this 24h timeline. When the remaining time drops to 12 hours, the clock raises a warning flag, prompting Miller to intercept the detective for a mid-shift status check. If the countdown reaches zero, the clock marks itself expired and sets the flags that terminate open exploration and force an immediate confrontation with Miller.

Information itself is gated by this clock through delayed evidence processing. When the player requests specialized laboratory analyses, medical records, or external reports from Miller, requests configured with a processing delay do not become usable immediately. Instead, the pending-evidence manager (`PendingEvidenceManager`) schedules the requested item against the current clock, while the item is marked as acquired so it appears visibly in the inventory. Wherever evidence is shown—cards, slots, inspect and read views—a pending item is greyed out with an hourglass and a remaining-time readout, and cannot be inspected, read, or equipped into either slot. Only when the clock passes the item's unlock point is the lock cleared, optionally firing a slip event. The player must therefore make the decision of which evidence to request, and whether to wait for them or not, or pursue other leads while waiting for results.

Time is simultaneously passive and spendable. Real-time exploration slowly consumes the 24-hour budget, with the time spent in the game matching the time in real life, so one in-game hour equals one real hour, yet players are meant to make the decision of when to wait for certain reports, with time being more of a currency where hours are deliberately spent at two rest stations (the resting chair in the Evidence Room and a station in the Observation Room), which open a wait panel. Choosing between 1 and 24 hours fades the screen to black and advances the clock immediately, through pending lab delays or suspect cooldowns—at the risk of crossing the 12-hour warning or expiring the shift altogether. Entering the optional dream state carries a similar temporal cost: on returning to the precinct, six hours are deducted from the waking investigation, forcing the player to weigh surreal exploration against procedural deadlines.

Time pressure also governs interpersonal friction. Weak or out-of-range questions during dialogue drain a character's patience, tracked in each character's knowledge log. For Elara and Kae, a misstep in confrontation costs twice as much patience as a misstep in inquiry; Miller's patience drains faster still. When patience reaches zero, the conversation closes, the character's depletion count rises, and a cooldown begins that is measured in _in-game hours_ (two hours for Elara and Kae, three for Miller). Waiting out a lockout costs shift hours, while repeatedly exhausting a suspect's patience escalates the investigation: after two depletions, Miller comes to warn the player, and reaching the depletion cap (three for Miller and Elara, four for Kae) triggers Miller's angry confrontation prematurely. (Exact values are given in Appendix X.5.)

Finally, the locked-room sequence condenses the entire game's temporal logic into a short, real-time miniature. Unlocking the locked-room door and crossing its threshold collapses Julian onto the floor and launches a 25-second countdown. With movement locked, the player must open their inventory under active time pressure, pair the Auto Injector and the Adrenaline Vial across the two slots, and press the interact key to inject before the timer expires and triggers a death ending—rehearsing in 25 seconds the same two-slot-under-_kairos_ gesture that governs the 24-hour case.

### 8.1.7 Sound Systems

#### Position in the architecture

The two audio managers—one for sound effects, one for music—belong entirely to the consumer side. Neither ever sets a flag, so audio is a pure projection of game state. It cannot alter the story, and a missing clip or an empty cue cannot block progression. The two managers use that same input in opposite ways: sound effects react to the _moment_ a flag changes, while music re-evaluates the _current state_ continuously.

#### Sound effects: edge-triggered, event-driven

**Data.** Sound effects are defined in a single database asset with two parts: a pool of variants, one of which plays whenever any slip event fires, and a list of flag rules. Each rule has a label, the flags that trigger it, a list of sound variants, and an option to avoid playing the same variant twice in a row. Each sound definition specifies its clip, volume, base pitch, pitch variance, looping, and priority.

**Runtime.** On start, the manager creates a small pool of 2D audio sources routed to the mixer's sound-effects channel, so the Settings volume slider controls them. It also inverts the database into a lookup from each flag to the rules that use it. When a flag is newly set, the manager plays one random variant for each matching rule, avoiding an immediate repeat where configured. Clearing a flag is ignored. A fired slip event plays one of the universal slip variants the same way.

**Voice management.** The manager looks for an idle source in the pool. If every source is busy, a new sound takes over a source only when its priority is strictly higher than that of the sound already playing; otherwise the new sound is dropped. Small random pitch offsets keep repeated sounds from feeling mechanical.

**No direct access.** No other system can call the sound-effects manager directly; the only way to make it play is to change narrative state. Every sound in the game is therefore explained by a flag or a slip event, and the database doubles as the sound-design specification.

**Current content.** Five flag rules—door openings (with four variants), picking up paper, picking up other items, a toilet flush, and acquiring the keys—and three slip-event variants (Appendix X.6).

**Consequence of edge triggering.** Notifications are raised only when a flag is newly set, so a flag plays its sound once per session unless something clears it. The project handles this for presence flags: interrogation-room seating and room-presence components clear their flags after each eviction or walk-in, and the save system treats suspect-presence flags as transient and strips them from saves. Loading a save restores flags without raising notifications, so no sounds replay on load.

#### Music: level-triggered, state-driven

**Data.** Each music cue asset defines an identifier, a clip, a priority, an optional time window (the cue is eligible only below a given number of hours remaining), whether the clock must be running, flags that must all be set, flags that must all be absent, looping, volume, and a force-play option. Whether a cue is eligible is a pure function of the clock and the flag set. A cue database holds the list.

**Runtime.** Two audio sources cross-fade between cues. Twice per second (in real time, independent of game time), the music manager reads the clock and the flag set and runs the following decision order:

1. A one-shot cue that has finished playing is marked _spent_.
2. The queue is refreshed. Every eligible, non-forced cue that is not spent is queued, up to a small limit; when the queue is full, the lowest-priority entry is evicted for a strictly higher one.
3. Eligible forced cues interrupt everything, and priority decides among them.
4. If the current cue is still playing and still eligible, it keeps playing. A forced cue or a non-forced one-shot is never displaced by another non-forced cue, which waits in the queue. Only a looping cue yields to a higher-priority one.
5. Otherwise the highest-priority queued (or eligible) cue starts with a short cross-fade. If nothing is eligible, the music fades to silence.

A cue that finishes or is replaced is _spent_ and stays spent until a new game begins. Each cue is therefore a story-bound moment that plays at most once per playthrough rather than a looping background track. Setting a flag simply brings the next evaluation forward to the next frame; the decision itself still runs through the regular evaluation.

**Current content.** The database contains 27 cues. Fourteen are forced and thirteen are not. All are one-shots, and all require the clock to be running. Every cue is keyed on one required flag, except a single cue ("Round Midnight") that is purely time-based. No cue uses blocking flags. Priorities fall into five bands, from lowest to highest: evidence and deduction stings; character beats such as Kae's confession, Elara's sleepwalking, or Miller's anger; altered-state and set-piece sequences such as the locked room, the adrenaline and Moon injections, and the dream; time pressure; and outcomes, meaning the endings and Miller firing the player. (Cue names and numeric bands are listed in Appendix X.6.)

#### Music and the Internal Clock

**The clock.** The internal clock holds a countdown of hours remaining. In the precinct scene it is configured with 24 total hours and a warning at 12 hours remaining (the code defaults are 36 and 18). It starts when the intro sets its start flag, raises a warning flag at the threshold, and at zero sets its configured expiry flags. Because it counts down in real seconds, one in-game hour equals one real hour. Gameplay time therefore moves mainly through deliberate skips: the wait panel, the return from the dream, and a debug time-skip tool.

**Two coupling channels.** Music depends on the clock through two routes:

- _Through flags._ The clock publishes its milestones as flags, and music consumes them like any others: the clock starting triggers its own cue, and the time warning triggers a forced, high-priority cue.
- _Through direct reads._ On every evaluation, the music manager reads how many hours remain and whether the clock is running. Requiring the clock to run keeps the score silent before the investigation begins (menu, intro) and whenever the clock is stopped. The time window follows the clock's own convention of _absolute hours remaining, not a delta_, and compares strictly below the threshold—the same convention used for pending evidence.

The only time-windowed cue, "Round Midnight", becomes eligible below 15 hours remaining. With the scene's 24-hour budget, that is about nine hours into the case, between the clock start and the warning. Because it is not forced, it never cuts a story beat, but once the current cue ends it outranks the other queued cues.

The two channels exist because they answer different questions. A flag marks a discrete event. A continuous quantity such as hours remaining needs a threshold test. The clock does that test itself for the warning and publishes the result as a flag, while "Round Midnight" does the same kind of test on the consumer side without needing a producer.

**Pause coordination.** Opening the pause menu freezes game time, pauses the clock, and fades the music out, pausing it in place without spending the cue. Because the music manager runs on real time rather than game time, its fades and evaluation keep working while game time is frozen. Resuming brings the music back. After quitting to the menu, the music stays paused until a new game or a continued game restarts it fresh.

#### Contribution to the system

- **Decoupling.** Audio is added or changed by editing data assets only. No gameplay script refers to an audio manager, apart from pause, save, and menu plumbing.
- **State feedback.** Sound effects confirm state changes the player may not otherwise notice (slip events, key pickups, doors), while the score marks story beats and outcomes.
- **Pacing.** The clock-aware cues communicate the deadline through the same flag-and-threshold machinery that drives the narrative.
- **Cheap persistence.** Because music is level-triggered, a save needs only the lists of spent and queued cues.

### 8.1.8 Making It Hold Together

To ensure that the prototype's temporal, epistemic, and narrative rules could survive across play sessions without introducing exploits or fragile scene states, the save manager (`SaveManager`) serializes the full investigation state to JSON. A save captures the narrative flags, fired slip events, and unlocked dialogue nodes; acquired, unlocked, and already-seen evidence; the player's folders; equipped slots; the clock and its warning and expiry state; the unlock points of pending evidence; interaction histories (keyed by character rather than by transient Unity object IDs); the music queue; the player's notes; consumed world actions; and each character's knowledge log and patience and cooldown state. Two deliberate exclusions protect system stability. First, suspect-presence flags are stripped on both save and load, and neither the player's position nor the active room is restored, preventing a reload from catching a NavMesh walk-in or eviction mid-transition. Second, once the player enters the ending sequence, saving is locked, so completed runs cannot be reopened or overwritten.

Studying player reasoning in Chapter 9 also required unobtrusive runtime instrumentation alongside fast debugging hooks. The playtest logger (`PlaytestLogger`), which persists from the main menu onwards, records timestamped event streams—covering flag changes, evidence acquisitions, folder reorganizations, player notes, and completed conversations—and writes a final state dump, including each character's active chain and number of consecutive out-of-range attempts, when the application exits or on demand. During development and testing, a developer panel, a runtime console, time-skip and evidence-grant shortcuts, and a control-state debugger allowed rapid state inspection and recovery without altering release gameplay logic (Appendix X.10).

Finally, four technical limitations bound the build evaluated in Chapter 9:

1. **Hand-authored combinations:** Because evaluation is deterministic rather than inferred from tags, every meaningful evidence pair must be explicitly authored as a chain node, interaction, or terminal unlock rule.
2. **Text identifiers:** Cross-system references rely on raw text identifiers for flags, evidence, and chains, mitigated through centralized constants and editor extractors rather than compile-time checks.
3. **Mixed legacy overlays:** While the primary panels use UI Toolkit, proximity prompts, slip-event notifications, the locked-room countdown, and the ending illustration and credits sequences still render through Unity's legacy `OnGUI`.
4. **Unfinished conviction route:** Although dialogue, outcome, and slip-event assets were drafted for a robbery-conviction variant, only Kae's reaction was wired in, as a global dialogue override, rather than as a standalone fifth ending path (asset names in Appendix X.3).
