![[software_architecture_overview.png]]## Chapter 8: Digital Prototyping

This chapter documents the implementation of the digital prototype that translates the conceptual, narrative, and formal specifications of Chapters 4 through 7 into a playable single-player detective game. Where Chapter 4 proposed the five core epistemic mechanics, Chapter 5 authored the Oakhaven Central case model, Chapter 6 rehearsed those interactions with printed artifacts in tabletop workshops, and Chapter 7 formalized the Petri-net state machine, Logic Chain dialogue model, and early interface layouts, the task of digital prototyping was to bind those strands into a coherent interactive build. Crucially, the prototype had to preserve interpretive agency under solo-player conditions—making evidence organization, two-slot experimentation, social friction, and time pressure immediately legible without automating deduction or confirming whether the player’s theory is correct.

As a solo development effort completed within a bounded production window, every technical and visual choice reflects deliberate scoping. Rather than building an expansive city, procedural dialogue generation, or bespoke 2D visual-novel illustrations for every state, the prototype concentrates its complexity into a six-room precinct (GeometryAttempt), a focused surreal chamber (Dream_Sequence), a repurposed cell wing (Ending_Sequence), and an introductory menu (MainMenu). Combinatorial inference is bounded to pairwise anchor-plus-partner rules across two equipped slots, and narrative variation is driven by reusable state flags rather than branching scripts—allowing depth of investigative ambiguity without unmanageable authoring overhead.

The prototype was built in **Unity 6000.3 LTS (6000.3.10f1)** using three primary technical pillars:

- **ScriptableObject data architecture:** All 61 evidence items, dialogue chains, standalone interactions, slip events, computer unlock rules, world actions, and ending paths are authored as modular assets inspected via custom Editor node-graph tooling (LogicChainGraphWindow) and verified with offline scene/asset extractors.
    
- **UI Toolkit (UXML / USS):** The investigative HUD, two-slot dialogue panel, inventory and folder workspace, inspect and read modes, computer terminal, conversation archive, and pause/settings menus are implemented in UI Toolkit (supplemented in a few secondary overlays by Unity’s legacy OnGUI).
    
- **Unity NavMesh (NavMeshSurface / NavMeshAgent):** Character entrances, interrogation room seating swaps, and Captain Miller’s timed confrontations operate spatially across the 3D precinct alongside a custom isometric-style camera and per-room visibility system.

## 8.1 System Architecture
### 8.1.1 Architectural Stance

The digital prototype is structured around a strict separation between what the game knows (*authored data*), what the player has done (*runtime state*), and how the two communicate (*flags*). Rather than embedding narrative logic inside scene scripts or character monobehaviours, all case knowledge is authored in immutable `ScriptableObject` assets: the 61 `EvidenceData` items, dialogue structures (`LogicChainData`, `DialogueNodeData`, `EvidenceInteractionData`, and `NarrativeDialogueData`), `SlipEventData`, `WorldObjectData` and `WorldObjectActionData` definitions, `ComputerUnlockRule` assets, `FlagNarrativeRule` and `NarrativeSuppressionRule` entries, and the four `EndingPathData` definitions (with two `EndingDialogueOverrideData` variants). Runtime mutations—such as acquired and pending evidence, equipped slots, remaining hours, folder contents, conversation histories, and active flags—never modify these assets; they live exclusively inside a small set of runtime managers.

To prevent coupling as new interactions and consequences were authored, narrative subsystems do not call one another directly. Instead, they read from and write to a single shared vocabulary of string-typed flags and one-shot slip events managed by `NarrativeStateManager`. Acquisition bridges (`EvidenceFlagBridge`), world triggers (`FlagDrivenDoor`, `LockedRoomEntranceTrigger`, `DreamSequenceTrigger`), NPC behaviours (`CharacterRoomPresence`, `NPCApproachPlayer`, `NPCForcedConfrontation`, `CharacterAnimationHandler`), narrative directors (`FlagNarrativeManager`, `NarrativeSuppressionManager`), audio cues (`MusicManager`, `SFXManager`), and ending resolution (`EndingManager`) all operate independently by querying `IsFlagSet` or subscribing to `OnFlagChanged` and `OnSlipEventFired`. An evidence pickup or dialogue exchange simply emits a flag or slip event; any world state that depends on it reacts without the emitter needing to know it exists.

Because the prototype spans multiple Unity scenes—`MainMenu`, the primary precinct investigation (`GeometryAttempt`), the surreal `Dream_Sequence`, and the closing `Ending_Sequence`—runtime state must survive scene unloads. Persistent managers are therefore grouped under `GameManagers_Persistent` (anchored by `PersistentRoot`), which carries `NarrativeStateManager`, `EvidenceStateManager`, `EvidenceFolderManager`, `SlotManager`, `InternalClockManager`, `PendingEvidenceManager`, `GameStateManager`, `EndingManager`, `NarrativeSuppressionManager`, `SaveManager`, audio managers, and pause/settings UI across `SceneManager.LoadScene` transitions. By contrast, `GameManagers_Scene` holds scene-scoped infrastructure (`SceneLocalBootstrap`, `CombinationEvaluator`, `EvidenceIDRetriever`, `SlipEventHandler`, `IRSeatManager`, `FlagNarrativeManager`, and sequence controllers) alongside local UI and character `CharacterKnowledgeLog` components. This split allows the player to enter the dream state or transition to the station cells in `Ending_Sequence` while preserving their accumulated flags, inventory, clock state, and resolved `EndingPathData` verdict.

### 8.1.2 The Two-Slot System as a Constraint

At the centre of the prototype’s interaction design is a deliberate structural ceiling: at any moment, the player can equip at most two pieces of evidence into `SlotA` and `SlotB` (managed by `SlotManager`). Carrying forward the cardboard two-slot board tested in the Chapter 6 workshops, `SlotManager` prevents the same item from occupying both slots simultaneously (`IsSameEvidence`) and rejects items that are still pending laboratory processing (`PendingEvidenceManager.IsPending`). Rather than building a general-purpose deduction engine capable of parsing arbitrary sets of clues, every combination rule in the codebase—whether in `DialogueNodeData.TryGetRequiredMatch`, `EvidenceInteractionData.TryGetTriggerMatch`, or `ComputerUnlockRule.Matches`—is evaluated against at most two equipped IDs. Multi-item rules use an *anchor-plus-partner* convention: the first required ID acts as the anchor, and any one of the remaining IDs in the rule’s list satisfies the partner condition. Limiting rules to pairwise combinations keeps the authoring space tractable and ensures the interface remains compact and legible during play.

Because every investigative, technical, and embodied action passes through the same slot bar, the player learns one consistent gesture—*equipping up to two objects to express an intent*—and carries that literacy across the entire precinct.

Epistemically, the two-slot constraint forces the player to hypothesise a specific relation between two items rather than clicking through an exhaustive list of owned clues. When the player equips two items together in dialogue, the system reinforces this intentionality through **hierarchy suppression** rather than additive clutter. In `CombinationEvaluator`, any evidence ID already claimed by an active `LogicChainData` option suppresses standalone `EvidenceInteractionData` entries on that single item (`coveredByChain`), and `SuppressSubsetInteractions` removes any matching interaction whose matched trigger set is a strict subset of a broader match in the same evaluation. The same logic operates inside individual chain nodes via `LogicChainDatabase.ApplyHierarchySuppression`. For example, if the player equips Kae’s Firearm Evidence Record (`E1R`) alone, a standalone single-item question about gun ownership surfaces (`KAE_E1R`); if they equip `E1R` alongside the Ballistics Examination Report (`E2R`), the pairwise interaction (`KAE_E1R_E2R`) dominates and suppresses both individual `E1R` and `E2R` prompts. Showing more evidence narrows the conversation to the specific synthesis the player has proposed, rather than adding noise.

The primary trade-off of this constraint is authoring labour: because inference is not computed dynamically from semantic tags, every meaningful pair must be explicitly authored as a node, interaction, or unlock rule—a limitation returned to in Section 8.1.7.

### 8.1.3 Data-Driven Evidence, Dialogue, and Progression

Because the case narrative, clue dependencies, and ending structure continued to evolve throughout development, all narrative content had to remain editable without modifying C# code. Every domain entity in the prototype is therefore authored as a Unity `ScriptableObject` asset and indexed in database assets: 61 `EvidenceData` items, `LogicChainData` and `DialogueNodeData` sequences (`LogicChainDatabase`), standalone and pairwise `EvidenceInteractionData` entries (`EvidenceInteractionDatabase`), `SlipEventData` consequences (`SlipEventDatabase`), `NarrativeDialogueData` scripts, `WorldObjectData` and `WorldObjectActionData` definitions, `ComputerUnlockRule` entries (`ComputerUnlockDatabase`), and `EndingPathData` assets. To keep multi-node dialogue maintainable as the corpus grew, custom editor tooling—including `LogicChainGraphWindow` (`DialogueNodeView`, `EvidenceNodeView`), `LogicChainDataEditor`, and `DetectiveQuestionDrawer`—renders chains as visual node graphs and structured inspector lists inside the Unity Editor. Adding a new suspect line, revising response text, or introducing an additional ending requires creating or editing assets rather than writing new gameplay logic.

The ending pipeline provides the clearest demonstration of this data-driven architecture. `EndingManager` holds a list of four `EndingPathData` assets (`01_ENDING_KAE_MURDER_ELARA_FREE` at priority 100, `02_ENDING_KAE_MURDER_ELARA_ACCOMPLICE` at priority 95, `03_ENDING_ELARA_MURDER_KAE_ACCOMPLICE` at priority 90, and `04_ENDING_ELARA_MURDER_KAE_GUN_ONLY` at priority 85) alongside two `EndingDialogueOverrideData` assets (`KAE_REACTION_ELARA_SHOOTER_KAE_PRESSURED` and `KAE_REACTION_KAE_SHOOTER_ROBBERY_CONVICTION`). Each `EndingPathData` asset defines its own `FlagGroup` and `FlagCondition` predicates together with three `NarrativeDialogueData` references: `elaraReactionDialogue`, `kaeReactionDialogue`, and `outcomeNarrative`. When the player reaches the stairs at the end of the investigation, `EndingManager.EvaluateAndResolve()` sorts the paths in descending priority order and selects the first asset whose flag conditions pass. It then evaluates `globalDialogueOverrides`: if an override’s `ConditionsPass` check succeeds against the active flags, its non-null `elaraReactionOverride`, `kaeReactionOverride`, or `outcomeOverride` swaps out the corresponding sequence on the resolved path. Conditional variations—such as Kae reacting differently if he was previously pressured or convicted on a robbery angle—are thus layered onto existing endings without duplicating entire `EndingPathData` assets.

Progression rules and state retirement follow the same asset-first model, separating *when* a narrative event happens from the systems that play it:

- **Flag-driven narrative rules (`FlagNarrativeRule`):** Monitored by `FlagNarrativeManager`, each rule asset pairs flag conditions and an optional confrontation-strength threshold (`CharacterKnowledgeLog.GetStrength`) with a priority, a `fireOnce` guard, and a `NarrativeDialogueData` sequence. In `GeometryAttempt`, three authored rules (`FlagNarrativeRule_E14R_ACQUIRED`, `FlagNarrativeRule_CONFESSION`, and `FlagNarrativeRule_OBTANING_F1_EVENT_TIME`) watch for `OnFlagChanged` and `OnStrengthChanged` events, automatically interrupting free roam to deliver scripted beats when their thresholds are met.
- **Suppression rules (`NarrativeSuppressionRule`):** Indexed in `NarrativeSuppressionDatabase` and queried by `NarrativeSuppressionManager`, suppression assets list flag predicates that disable obsolete `LogicChainData` chains or `EvidenceInteractionData` prompts once the narrative has moved past them (for example, `ELARA_GUN_REACTION_CONFRONTATION`, `KAE_GUN_OWNER`, `MILLER_KEYS_AVAILABLE`, and `ENDING_SEQUENCE_STARTED`). Both `LogicChainDatabase` and `EvidenceInteractionDatabase` filter out suppressed entries before any question is surfaced.
- **Reset packages (`ChainResetPackage`):** Attached to NPCs via `ChainResetListener` (such as `ChainResetPackageMillerConfrontation` and `ChainResetPackageWarningDone`), these assets specify a `triggerFlag` along with the exact `chainsToReactivate`, `flagsToClear`, and `slipEventsToRestore` needed to reopen Miller’s confrontation or warning loops after a cooldown expires.

The principal engineering cost of a fully data-driven pipeline is string-ID fragility. Because assets reference one another and C# scripts through raw string identifiers (`evidenceID`, `flagID`, `slipEventID`, `chainID`, `nodeID`, and `interactionID`), a single spelling discrepancy between an asset field and code causes a trigger, door, or rule to fail silently at runtime. To mitigate this risk without abandoning flexible asset authoring, three verification practices were built around the project. First, core runtime flags referenced across C# scripts were centralized as constants in `GameFlags` rather than duplicated as inline literals. Second, startup and runtime diagnostics—`GameBootstrapValidator` (which verifies required managers on scene load), `PrototypeDiagnostic` (`[F1]`, which tests currently equipped slots against `LogicChainDatabase` and `EvidenceInteractionDatabase`), and `RuntimeDebugConsole` (`[F3]`)—surface missing wiring and evaluation mismatches immediately during playtesting. Third, a suite of custom editor extractors (`Assets/Editor/Extractors/`), led by `SceneContextExporter` alongside `DialogueExtractor`, `EvidenceInteractionExtractor`, `SlipEventExtractor`, `SuppressionRuleExtractor`, `NarrativeFlagRuleExtractor`, and `WorldObjectExtractor`, serializes all scene references and authored asset graphs into plain-text manifests. Treating these dumps as an offline audit layer made it possible to inspect every flag, trigger, and asset link across all scenes and catch naming mismatches before playtest builds were frozen.

### 8.1.4 Flags: From Evidence to Narrative Consequence

Because the precinct is freely navigable and players choose which threads to pursue first, the prototype cannot rely on linear scripts or hardcoded scene sequences. Instead, it uses a global flag system to ensure that players can gather evidence in any order while the world and characters still react coherently. Every significant state change—acquiring key forensic reports, opening doors, exhausting a suspect’s patience, or triggering an accusation—converges on `NarrativeStateManager`, which maintains a runtime `HashSet<string>` of active flags (`activeFlags`) alongside a record of one-shot events (`firedEvents`).

The bridge between physical clues and narrative state begins at acquisition. Whenever `EvidenceStateManager` unlocks or acquires an item, `EvidenceFlagBridge` checks a table of authored `EvidenceFlagMapping` entries and writes the corresponding flag into `NarrativeStateManager`. Eight critical forensic and documentary items carry direct mappings in `GeometryAttempt`: `E1R` (`FLAG_E1R_ACQUIRED`), `E2R` (`FLAG_E2R_ACQUIRED`), `E3R` (`FLAG_E3R_ACQUIRED`), `E4-TRACE` (`FLAG_E4-TRACE_ACQUIRED`), `E9R` (`FLAG_E9R_ACQUIRED`), `F1` (`FLAG_F1_ACQUIRED`), `L1` (`FLAG_L1_ACQUIRED`), and `N1` (`FLAG_N1_ACQUIRED`). Additional flags are written by `WorldObjectActionData` (`onSuccessSetFlag`), `ComputerUnlockRule` (`narrativeEventID`), `NarrativeDialogueData` (`onCompleteSetFlag`), and `SlipEventHandler`. Once set, flags propagate outward across every subsystem without direct object references:

- **Dialogue gating:** Within `LogicChainDatabase`, individual `DetectiveQuestion` templates can embed inline `{FLAG_...}` tokens. `ExtractFlagFromText` strips the tag from the displayed prompt and suppresses the question unless `NarrativeStateManager.IsFlagSet` returns true. Standalone `EvidenceInteractionData` assets similarly evaluate `IsFlagConditionMet()` against required and excluded flags before surfacing.

Crucially, nothing in this architecture depends on acquisition order or direct object-to-object calls. Because subsystems query state predicates (`IsFlagSet`) or react to `OnFlagChanged` and `OnFlagSet` whenever conditions become true, a revelation unlocked through the computer terminal in hour 3 produces the same downstream behaviour as one unlocked in hour 14, and multi-condition rules (such as `EndingPathData` or `FlagNarrativeRule`) simply wait until all required flags coexist.

To prevent key narrative beats from repeating indefinitely once unlocked, the prototype distinguishes persistent state (*flags*) from atomic transitions (*slip events*). Defined in `SlipEventData` assets and indexed in `SlipEventDatabase`, slip events represent one-shot epistemic turning points—revelations, evidence grants, or irreversible realizations. When a `DetectiveQuestion` with an assigned `slipEvent` is selected, `EvidenceIDRetriever` checks whether that event has already fired for the character (`_firedSlipEvents`, unless `allowRepeatSlipEvent` is enabled) and attaches the event ID to the `RetrievalResult`. Rather than firing mid-sentence, `DialogueUI.TryFireDeferredSlipEvent()` waits until the player finishes reading the character’s final response page before calling `NarrativeStateManager.FireSlipEvent`. Because `NarrativeStateManager.firedEvents.Add` returns true only once, `SlipEventHandler` executes the event’s rewards—unlocking and acquiring follow-up evidence, setting new flags, and displaying a brief notification toast—exactly once. Paired with `NarrativeSuppressionManager`, which retires obsolete chains and interactions once their successor flags are active, slip events ensure that discoveries move the investigation forward rather than looping. (Only when an authored `ChainResetPackage` explicitly restores a loop—such as resetting Miller’s warning state after a cooldown—does `ChainResetListener` call `UnfireSlipEvent`.)

Architecturally, this flag-and-slip model is the direct software realization of the Chapter 7 Petri-net state machine. Where Chapter 7 modelled investigation flow using places, tokens, `CHECK_NODE` guards, `OUTPUT_PLACE` artifacts, and `GLOBAL_ROOM_STATE`, the Unity implementation maps those formal constructs onto runtime C# equivalents while expanding them where 3D space and time required additional control:

| Chapter 7 Petri-Net Construct | Chapter 8 Unity Implementation | Relationship to Design |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Place / Token** (narrative or system condition) | String entry in `NarrativeStateManager.activeFlags` | **Kept**, with `ClearFlag` and `ChainResetPackage` added so cooldowns and suspect swaps can retract tokens. |
| **`CHECK_NODE`** (pre-condition guard) | `requiredEvidenceIDs`, `FlagCondition`, and inline `{FLAG_...}` question tags | **Kept**; extended with pairwise anchor-plus-partner slot matching and `CharacterKnowledgeLog` strength thresholds. |
| **Transition Firing** (atomic state change) | `SlipEventData` processed by `EvidenceIDRetriever`, `DialogueUI`, and `SlipEventHandler` | **Kept** as a one-shot gate (`firedEvents.Add`); deferred in `DialogueUI` until the final response page is read. |
| **`OUTPUT_PLACE`** (produced clue or state) | `EvidenceStateManager` unlocks/acquisitions and `SetFlag` calls | **Kept**; augmented with `PendingEvidenceManager` when forensic outputs must arrive after a time delay. |
| **`GLOBAL_ROOM_STATE`** (spatial context) | `GameStateManager` (`RoomState`), `FlagDrivenDoor`, and `CharacterRoomPresence` | **Diverged** from a single global state into reactive room-visibility triggers and flag-driven doors/NPC seating. |

### 8.1.5 Time as a Constraint

To make investigative choices genuinely costly rather than exhaustive, the prototype binds the precinct investigation to a finite countdown managed by `InternalClockManager`. Once `IntroSequenceController` completes Captain Miller’s opening briefing (`MILLER_BRIEF_01`) and sets `FLAG_INTERNAL_CLOCK_STARTS`, the clock begins counting down. Although the architecture plan originally budgeted 36 in-game hours, playtest tuning in the `GeometryAttempt` build tightened the window to 24 hours (`totalHours = 24`), with a midpoint warning threshold at 12 hours remaining (`warningAtHoursRemaining = 12`). When the remaining time drops to 12 hours, `InternalClockManager` fires its warning flag (`FLAG_TIME_WARNING`), prompting Miller to intercept the detective for a mid-shift status check (`MILLER_TIME_WARNING`). If the countdown reaches zero, the clock marks itself expired and writes four flags (`FLAG_MILLER_ANGRY_SEQUENCE`, `FLAG_CASE_NOT_READY`, `FLAG_MILLER_WARNING_DONE`, and `FLAG_MILLER_WARNING_RESTORE`), terminating open exploration and forcing an immediate confrontation with Miller.

### 8.1.7 Sound Systems

#### Position in the architecture

The game keeps its narrative state in `NarrativeStateManager`: a set of string flags, a set of fired slip events and a set of unlocked dialogue nodes. Any system that wants to change the story writes a flag with `SetFlag`. The manager then announces the change through events (the instance event `OnFlagSet`, the static event `OnFlagChanged(flag, isSet)` and, for slip events, the static `OnEventFired`) and requests an autosave.

Many systems produce flags: dialogue pages and choices (`DialogueUI`), evidence acquisition (`EvidenceFlagBridge`), pickups (`PickupInteractable.flagOnPickup`), slip events (`SlipEventHandler`), character behaviour (`PatienceCooldownHandler`, `CharacterRoomPresence`), scripted sequences (intro, locked room, dream) and the `InternalClockManager` itself. Other systems consume them, for example `FlagNarrativeManager`, `FlagDrivenDoor`, `CharacterAnimationHandler` and `EndingOutcomeController`.

`SFXManager` and `MusicManager` belong to the consumer side. Neither one ever calls `SetFlag`, so audio is a pure projection of game state. It cannot alter the story, and a missing clip or an empty cue cannot block progression. The two managers use that same input in opposite ways: sound effects react to the *moment* a flag changes, while music re-evaluates the *current state* continuously.

#### SFXManager: edge-triggered, event-driven

**Data.** `SFXDatabase` is a ScriptableObject with two parts. `universalSlipEventVariants` is a list of sounds, one of which plays whenever any slip event fires. `flagEntries` is a list of `FlagSFXEntry` rules, each with a label, a list of trigger flags, a list of variants and an `avoidImmediateRepeat` option. An `SFXDefinition` describes one sound: clip, volume, base pitch, pitch variance, loop and priority.

**Runtime.** On `Awake` the manager creates a pool of 2D `AudioSource` components (default `poolSize` of 8) routed to the mixer's SFX group, so the Settings slider controls them. It also inverts the database into a dictionary from flag ID to the entries that use it, trimming whitespace from the authored strings. On `OnFlagChanged(flag, true)` it looks the flag up and plays one random variant per matching entry, avoiding an immediate repeat when configured. Clearing a flag is ignored. A fired slip event plays one of the universal slip variants the same way.

**Voice management.** `GetAvailableSource` scans the pool round-robin for an idle source. If every source is busy, a new sound steals a source only when its priority is strictly higher than the playing sound's (plus `priorityStealThreshold`, currently 0). Otherwise the sound is dropped. Small random pitch offsets keep repeated sounds from feeling mechanical.

**No direct API.** All of `SFXManager`'s methods are private. The only way to make it play is to change narrative state. Every sound in the game is therefore explained by a flag or a slip event, and the database doubles as the sound-design specification.

**Current content.** Five flag entries and three slip-event variants:

| Entry | Trigger flags | Variants |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening Doors | `FLAG_LOCKEDDOOR_OPEN`, `FLAG_SUSPECT_IN_IR`, `FLAG_BR_DOOR_OPEN` | 4 |
| Pick Up Paper | `FLAG_PICKUP_PAPER` | 1 |
| Pick Up | `FLAG_PICKUP` | 1 |
| Toilet Flush | `FLAG_TOILET_FLUSH` | 1 |
| Keys | `FLAG_KEYS_ACQUIRED` | 1 |

**Consequence of edge triggering.** `SetFlag` raises events only when a flag is newly added. A flag therefore plays its sound once per session unless something clears it. The project handles this for presence flags: `IRSeatManager` clears its `repeatableSeatFlags` after each eviction, `CharacterRoomPresence` clears its walked-in flag, and `SaveManager` treats the suspect-presence flags as transient and strips them from saves. Loading a save restores flags through `LoadState`, which raises no events, so no sounds replay on load.

#### MusicManager: level-triggered, state-driven

**Data.** Each `MusicCueData` asset defines a cue: ID, clip, priority, an optional time window (`activeWhenHoursRemainingBelow`), `requireClockRunning`, `requiredFlags` (all must be set), `blockedByFlags` (all must be absent), `loop`, `volume` and `forcePlay`. The method `IsEligible(hoursRemaining, clockRunning, isFlagSet)` is a pure function of those inputs. `MusicCueDatabase` holds the list and an ID lookup.

**Runtime.** Two `AudioSource` components cross-fade between cues. Every 0.5 s of unscaled time, `EvaluateAndTransition` reads the clock and the flag set and runs the following decision order:

1. A one-shot cue that has finished playing is marked *spent*.
2. The queue is refreshed. Every selectable, non-forced cue that is not spent is queued (maximum 8; when full, the lowest-priority entry is evicted for a strictly higher one).
3. Eligible `forcePlay` cues interrupt everything, and priority decides among them.
4. If the current cue is still playing and still eligible, it keeps playing. A forced cue or a non-forced one-shot is never displaced by another non-forced cue, which waits in the queue. Only a looping cue yields to a higher-priority one.
5. Otherwise the highest-priority queued (or eligible) cue starts with a 2 s fade-out and 2 s fade-in. If nothing is eligible, the music fades to silence.

A cue that finishes or is replaced is *spent* and stays spent until `ResetSpent()` is called at New Game. Each cue is therefore a story-bound moment that plays at most once per playthrough rather than a looping background track. When a flag is set, `OnFlagSet` only zeroes the evaluation timer so the change is picked up at the next frame. The evaluation itself still runs from the polling path.

#### MusicManager and the Internal Clock

**The clock.** `InternalClockManager` holds a countdown of `HoursRemaining`. In the scene it is configured with 24 total hours and a warning at 12 hours remaining (the code defaults are 36 and 18). It starts when `FLAG_INTERNAL_CLOCK_STARTS` is set and sets `FLAG_TIME_WARNING` at the threshold. At zero it sets the configured expired flags, which in the scene are `FLAG_MILLER_ANGRY_SEQUENCE`, `FLAG_CASE_NOT_READY`, `FLAG_MILLER_WARNING_DONE` and `FLAG_MILLER_WARNING_RESTORE`. Because it subtracts `Time.deltaTime / 3600`, one in-game hour equals one real hour. Gameplay time therefore moves mainly through `SkipHours`, called by `TimeAdvanceController` (the "wait" panel), by the dream return (`SceneReturnHandler`) and by the debug time-skip tool.

**Two coupling channels.** Music depends on the clock through both routes:

- *Through flags.* The clock publishes milestones as flags, and music consumes them like any others. `FLAG_INTERNAL_CLOCK_STARTS` triggers `CUE_INTERNAL_CLOCK_STARTS`. `FLAG_TIME_WARNING` triggers `CUE_TIME_WARNING` (priority 40, forced).
- *Through direct reads.* `MusicManager` reads `HoursRemaining` and `IsRunning` on every evaluation and passes them to `IsEligible`. `requireClockRunning` keeps the cue system silent before the clock starts (menu, intro) and whenever the clock is not running. The time window follows the clock's own convention of *absolute hours remaining, not a delta*, and compares strictly below the threshold. `PendingEvidenceManager` uses the same absolute-hours convention.

The only time-windowed cue is `CUE_ROUND_MIDNIGHT` (below 15 hours, priority 30, not forced). With the scene's 24-hour budget, it becomes available about nine hours into the case, between the clock start and the warning. Being non-forced, it never cuts a story beat, but once the current cue ends it outranks other queued cues.

The two channels exist because they answer different questions. A flag marks a discrete event. A continuous quantity such as hours remaining needs a threshold test. The clock does that test itself for the warning and publishes the result as a flag, while `CUE_ROUND_MIDNIGHT` does the same kind of test on the consumer side without needing a producer.

**Pause coordination.** Opening the pause menu sets `Time.timeScale` to 0, pauses the clock (a depth counter) and calls `MusicManager.Pause()`. That call fades the music out and pauses it in place without spending the cue. `MusicManager` runs on unscaled time so that its fades and evaluation keep working under a zero time scale. `Resume()` brings it back. On Quit to Menu the music stays paused until New Game or Continue calls `ResumeFresh()`.

#### Contribution to the system

- **Decoupling.** Audio is added or changed by editing ScriptableObjects only. No gameplay script references an audio manager apart from pause, save and menu plumbing.
- **State feedback.** Sound effects confirm state changes the player may not otherwise notice (slip events, key pickups, doors), while the score marks story beats and outcomes.
- **Pacing.** The clock-aware cues communicate the deadline through the same flag-and-threshold machinery that drives the narrative.
- **Cheap persistence.** The level-triggered music design means a save needs only the spent and queued cue lists.

### 8.1.8 Making It Hold Together

To ensure that the prototype’s temporal, epistemic, and narrative rules could survive across play sessions without introducing exploits or fragile scene states, `SaveManager` serializes the full investigation state into JSON (`SaveData`). A save snapshot captures `NarrativeStateManager` flags, fired slip events, and unlocked nodes; `EvidenceStateManager` acquired, unlocked, and seen IDs; player-organized folders (`EvidenceFolderManager`); equipped slots (`SlotManager`); `InternalClockManager` hours and warning/expiry flags; `PendingEvidenceManager` absolute unlock thresholds; `EvidenceIDRetriever` interaction and non-advancing play histories (normalized by `characterID` rather than transient Unity instance IDs); `MusicManager` cue queues; `InspectModeController` notes; consumed `WorldObjectActionData` IDs; and each character’s `CharacterKnowledgeLog` and `PatienceCooldownHandler` state. Two deliberate exclusions protect system stability. First, suspect presence flags are stripped on both save and load (`IsSuspectPresenceFlag`), and neither the player’s coordinates nor the active room is restored, preventing reloads from catching NavMesh walk-in or eviction coroutines mid-transition. Second, once the player enters `Ending_Sequence`, `StairsDoorHandler` invokes `SaveManager.LockSavesAfterEnding()`, preventing completed runs from being reopened or overwritten.

Studying player reasoning in Chapter 9 also required unobtrusive runtime instrumentation alongside fast debugging hooks. `PlaytestLogger` (persisted from `MainMenu`) records timestamped `.jsonl` event streams—covering flag changes, evidence acquisitions, folder reorganizations, player notes, and completed conversations—and writes a `_final.json` state dump (including per-character `activeChainID` and `consecutiveOutOfRange` telemetry) on application exit or `[F10]`. During development and testing, `DevToolsPanel` (`[F2]`), `RuntimeDebugConsole` (`[F3]`), `DebugTimeSkip` (`[F5]`–`[F8]`), `DebugEvidenceGranter` (`[F1]`/`[F11]`), and `ControlStateDebugger` allowed rapid state inspection and recovery without altering release gameplay logic.

Finally, four technical limitations bound the build evaluated in Chapter 9:

1. **Hand-authored combinations:** Because evaluation is deterministic rather than inferred from tags, every meaningful evidence pair must be explicitly authored in `LogicChainData`, `EvidenceInteractionData`, or `ComputerUnlockRule` assets.
2. **String-typed identifiers:** Cross-system references rely on raw string IDs (`flagID`, `evidenceID`, `chainID`), mitigated through `GameFlags` constants and editor extractors rather than compile-time checks.
3. **Mixed `OnGUI` overlays:** While primary panels use UI Toolkit, proximity prompts, `SlipEventHandler` toasts, `LockedRoomSequenceController`’s 25-second countdown, and `EndingOutcomeController`’s illustration and credits sequences still render through Unity’s legacy `OnGUI`.
4. **Unfinished conviction route:** Although dialogue, override, and slip assets were drafted for a robbery-conviction variant (`KAE_REACTION_ENDING_KAE_MURDER_ROBBERY_CONVICTION`, `OUTCOME_ENDING_KAE_MURDER_ROBBERY_CONVICTION`, `SLIP_STEP2_VERDICT_KAE_MOTIVE_ROBBERY_CONVICTION`), only `KAE_REACTION_KAE_SHOOTER_ROBBERY_CONVICTION` was wired as a global override rather than a standalone fifth `EndingPathData` branch.
