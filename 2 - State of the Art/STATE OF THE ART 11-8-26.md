2. State of the Art

## 2.1 The Origins of Drama

The human impulse to participate in storytelling activities, peripherally as a passive viewer or as a performer, has existed for millennia, from ancient myths and folk tales, games and rituals, singing and dancing, to Greek theater, to experimental plays in the 20th century. The evolution of forms of participation is a notable achievement in cultural history. This chapter intends to trace cultural and philosophical roots for ergodic literature and contemporary interactive videogame narratives.

The origins of dramatic performance can be traced back to ancient Greece, where theater had a critical role in the social, religious, and philosophical life of its citizens, which served as a foundation for all of the works where participation from the audience is one of the most crucial components.

The ancient Greek theater was born out of a ritual that came from the festivals of Dionysos, where the entire community came together to perform those said rituals, consisting of choral songs and dances through which they reaffirmed their shared values, fears, and ethical dilemmas (Laurel, 2014). The theater started in the 6th century BCE, in one of Dionysos's festivals that took place annually in Athens, where Thespis made the bold innovation of speaking lines as a specific character engaging in dialogue with the chorus leader. This revolutionized how people celebrated and honored Dionysos by opening the door to early Greek theater, where they communicated philosophical and religious ideas and provided a collective experience of emotion. This evolved to become a participatory and sacred event, where the "Chorus" served as intermediary between the characters and the audience, having the important role of guiding the emotional journey throughout the play (Laurel, 2014). But it was not about suspense or surprise, since they knew the stories being performed; it was more about re-experiencing known events through emotional and moral reflection, leaving the participation to be the catharsis or purification of emotion through empathic understanding (Laurel, 2014). In this way, the audience had an interactive engagement, not because they could change the narrative, but because they co-created the meaning derived from it due to their empathy, judgment, and reflection on each dramatic piece.

Being both a sacred and participatory event allowed those who attended to see a tragedy, where the audience saw actors enact struggles of the gods and mortals, allowing them to be confronted by questions related to justice, pride, fate, and human responsibility. This concept of audience participation, still very limited, not actually having any impact on the narrative or events, remained unchanged in periods like the Roman Empire and medieval times and was still primarily religious and doctrinal scripts (Cepek, 2014). Interestingly, some plays such as those in The Chester Mystery Cycle (14th century) explored domestic comedy and social archetypes and had indirect audience engagement (Ciobanu, 2016). In the Flood play, for example, Noah's wife openly addresses spectators and appeals to the shared domestic experience, at times complaining about men and directing her attention toward women in the audience (Ciobanu, 2016). This form of engagement didn't change the narrative, but encouraged social and emotional participation through recognition and proximity, not leaning into more experimental or interactive forms of participation.

This long-standing convention of passive observation shifted dramatically during the Renaissance era, where we saw the rise of humanism, global exploration, and the questioning of established norms. During this time, in theater, a new form of play began in Italy that relied on stock characters and improvisational situations, which allowed the actors to string together conveniently memorized lines and an array of pre-arranged comedy bits called "lazzi" (Schmitt, 2004). This, mixed with improvisation, allowed for a dynamic framework that, depending on the audience's reaction and interest in the play, would adapt the characters and events to craft a more interesting and engaging piece (Schmitt, 2004). This type of interaction with the audience, and how each actor can dynamically shape the narrative, is a progression from just indirect engagement in linear plays and could even be a conceptual structure for interactive narratives in the future.

Modern Theater

In modern times, 19th and–20th centuries, theater became more experimental with works from Stanislavski, Brecht, Artaud, and Grotowski that broke conventions and structures defined over the centuries. But there was one, Augusto Boal, with the work Theatre of the Oppressed, that revolutionized the formula in terms of participation. Born in Brazil in 1931 and influenced by the theories of Brecht, Stanislavski and Paulo Freire’s Pedagogy of the Oppressed, he went around touring with his plays in poor Brazilian neighborhoods (Sajnani et al., 2020). His plays ended with asking the audience to rise above oppression, and instead of just talking to the audience, he felt that he should be listening and empowering them. He encouraged audiences to talk back to the action and to also suggest new actions for the characters, and there's the example of a female audience member that couldn't communicate her idea to the actor, so she went on stage to perform it herself, creating the concept of "spect-actor" (Sajnani et al., 2020). This method became known as Forum Theater, and each play started with a short scene centered on a social problem, either sexism, racial discrimination, etc. After this scene finishes, it starts again, but this time spect-actors are invited to interrupt the proceeding with their own actions (Sajnani et al., 2020). There is also a "Joker" that monitors the performance and makes sure that each spect-actor is able to complete their action, also asking the audience to evaluate the usefulness of each proposed solution to the social problem they were discussing (Sajnani et al., 2020).

This origin and evolution establish how people gained interest in creating and interacting with works that provide a sense of participation, where their actions are meaningful and make them feel more immersed within the narrative. Just as the Greek chorus invited emotion and co-creation of meaning, and Boal's spect-actors stepped onto the stage and reshaped the narrative, we can design technology that invites the player/reader to become an active participant within the narrative through text. Brenda Laurel proposed these advances could serve as a foundation to design how a computer can create opportunities for agency in shaping narratives through interactive computational audiovisual representations.

Learning from Theater

In Computers as Theatre, Laurel directly addresses this evolution, proposing a shift in how we conceive human-computer interaction (HCI), where instead of looking at the computer as a static tool with an interface for controlling it, it should be seen instead as a medium where theater becomes a fundamental model for exploring and designing interactions (Laurel, 2014). It becomes a new digital theater where new engaging digital experiences and narratives can be explored, utilizing computers as tools for creating participatory and convincing narrative experiences, just as traditional theater does.

Laurel's work also provides a framework of structural vocabulary for understanding how this participation works, and how it could easily be adapted to what developers do with a computer by bridging drama with human-computer interaction (Laurel, 2014).

Laurel draws from Aristotelian Drama the four causes to reinterpret the role of humans and computation in interactive media:

This would be applied to human-computer interactions with the designer's vision of the completed interactive experience, with representations of action with agents (human, computer-based or hybrid) (Mateas & Stern, 2004).

Aristotle also provides a structural framework for understanding how meaning is constructed and perceived in narrative experiences, describing drama in terms of six hierarchical elements: action (plot), character, thought, language (diction), pattern, and enactment (spectacle), being related to formal cause (the author's plan) and material cause (the audience's perception) (Laurel, 2014).

In the process of sensemaking (Fig. 1), we can find two directions, tracing formal and material causes, downwards and upwards, respectively:

Formal cause proceeds downward: plot determines character, character determines thought, thought determines language, language determines pattern, and pattern determines spectacle (Laurel, 2014).

Material cause proceeds upward: the audience perceives spectacle, infers pattern, interprets language and action, reconstructs thought, understands character, and finally comprehends plot and theme (Laurel, 2014).

Interactive drama introduces a new causal situation where the player functions as a character who can choose actions, leading to becoming a partial source of the formal causation (Laurel, 2014). The player's intentions guide action from language through spectacle. Upwards, this agency is constrained, limited by material resources and media that allow certain actions while excluding others; downwards, it is also constrained by the authorial causes operating at the level of the plot (Laurel, 2014).

Within this dramatic framework, the concept of dramatic agency is important when understanding participation in interactive narratives. Dramatic agency refers to the experience of taking action within a narrative and perceiving that action to be meaningful, motivated, intelligible and consequential to the plot (Laurel, 2014). From the player or reader's perspective, it's important to understand the formal causation from the level of the plot to character, so that they understand what to do and why they should take certain actions within the story world at all. This motivation and need for action is conveyed through dramatic probability, and by understanding why certain actions are dramatically probable, the reader or player then understands what actions are worth considering (Mateas & Stern, 2004).

A player can also be understood as more than a reader; we can also understand its agency as the efficient cause manifested through skills, tools, and techniques, applied in the act of play, therefore driving the narrative under the formal and material conditions.

These concepts become the groundwork for the emergence of ergodic literature (explained in the next section) and interactive digital narratives, in which participation becomes not only emotional or interpretative but also procedural, requiring the reader or player's active intervention to determine how the story unfolds.

## 2.2 Ergodic literature

The concept of "Ergodic Literature" was coined in 1997 with the book Cybertext: Perspectives on Ergodic Literature from the writer Espen J. Aarseth (Aarseth, 1997), and defines it as a form of textuality in which "nontrivial effort is required to allow the reader to traverse the text", and marks a shift from passive consumption of narrative to an active, procedural engagement in its unfolding, being that the reader must perform choices, manipulation and interpretations that materially determine the textual outcome of the reading. In a way, the reader becomes a user, needing to have both interpretive and operational competencies (Aarseth, 1997).

It's a form of reading that predates the computer, having examples of texts such as The I Ching, with commentaries compiled between the 5th and 2nd centuries BCE, whose combinatorial algorithm generates different textual configurations through user intervention, and standing in the topological metaphor of the labyrinth, where unicursal and multicursal paths model distinct reading logics, connecting with the theater traditions of ancient Greek culture, which distributed agency among chorus, actors, and audience (Aarseth, 1997).

The historical trajectory of this type of reading leads to the experimental prints of Apollinaire's Calligrammes (1918), which requires the reader to physically turn strips of text to combine lines from 10 different sonnets, generating a vast number of unique poems; Saporta's Composition no. 1 (1962), that consists of a box of loose sheets that must be physically shuffled and assembled by the reader to create a one-time narrative (Aarseth, 1997). These examples of reading , like the "Choose Your Own Adventure" and others such as Choice-Based / Parser-Based within the digital computation medium, helped define this alternative form of literature.

### 2.2.1 Choose Your Own Adventure

Building on Aarseth's notion of ergodic traversal (Aarseth, 1997), the Choose Your Own Adventure (CYOA) 1979 series represented the first large-scale popularization of interactive storytelling in print form. Edward Packard stated that the idea for the books came from bedtime stories he told his daughters. He would ask them what they wanted the main character to do next, and then weave a narrative around their choice, leading to the forking path structure and releasing his first book, Sugarcane Island, in 1976 (Jenkins, 2014). This specific form of ergodic literature invites readers to assume a protagonist's role and periodically make decisions that redirect the story's path. Each choice leads to a different numbered section, sometimes converging, sometimes diverging toward multiple endings.

While the branching structure may appear simple, its implications were profound. Keenan Jenkins (2014) observed in Choose Your Own Adventure: Interactive Narratives and Attitude Change (Jenkins, 2014) that these stories transform the reader from a passive spectator into a participant who feels responsible for the outcomes they encounter. Jenkins' experiments showed that interactivity produces distinct psychological effects, such as identification, transportation, and responsibility that reshape how readers engage with narrative (Jenkins, 2014). This deepens how readers make decisions "as themselves", projecting personal values into the protagonist's role, but also making the reading immersive not by being "interactive", but also through active choice and emotional investment that generates reflection through moral consequences in that fictional world (Jenkins, 2014).

Illustrative Case: Trial of Champions

Let's use the example of the book of this genre "Trial of Champions (Fighting Fantasy #21)", of 1986. This is a Fighting Fantasy type of book, where the reader plays as either a slave or gladiator, captured and forced to compete in a lethal arena, not for fame, but for the master's entertainment.

The reader is both the author and actor of this fictional world narrative, and before the first paragraph of the adventure even begins, the reader is asked to roll dice to determine three key attributes: Skill, Stamina, and Luck. These numbers will define the identity for the protagonist, being a mechanical reflection of strength, resilience, and chance.

From this moment on, the reader begins their adventure, and every action, victory or defeat will be influenced by the stats they have rolled in the beginning of the book, and the narrative conclusion of the adventure will also be determined by the choices the reader makes early on, either the paths that he takes or the equipment that he takes during the journey. These matter in the success of certain events and battles; all of these events make for a transportation, where the reader is cognitively and emotionally absorbed, due to these mechanisms that mimic the unpredictability of a real experience (Jenkins, 2014). This formula is meant for re-readability, because if the character dies due to poor judgment or unlucky rolls, it's meant for the reader to start again and re-roll stats, rethink tactics and path decisions, making him both responsible for past failures, but motivated to explore different outcomes in order to reach the end of that adventure.

This example of reading merges both narrative interactivity and procedural interactivity, layering the engagement with the narrative where the audience becomes both a reader and a player within the story and simulation. This makes up for a living narrative that deepens identification and responsibility and sustains emotional immersion long after the final dice roll.

This form of ergodic reading, with its interactive participatory systems, helps bridging the digital computational form with interactive fiction, which reinforces the idea that meaning in interactive narrative arises through participation, not mere observation (Aarseth, 1997).

### 2.2.2 Interactive Fiction (Parser-Based)

Building on the example of ergodic structures and the "Choose Your Own Adventure" style of books, its experience is expanded within a computational medium, allowing for the mathematical aspect related to stats and rolls as mentioned before, as well as going through the book for the branching choices to be completely automatic and allowing for a more fluid form of interaction with these fictional narratives, being divided into two examples of interaction, being parser-based or choice-based (Montfort, 2003).

The parser-based form of interaction in these computational fictional narratives consists of allowing the player to write their way through the story by typing commands in natural language, such as "go north," "open the door," or "talk to guard," establishing a new literary paradigm in which the reader's words become performative acts within a fictional simulated world (Montfort, 2003).

This form of interactive fiction is a "text-accepting, text-generating computer program" (Montfort, 2003) built around two essential components: the world model and the parser. The world model represents the simulated environment, its characters and its physical or logical rules, while the parser serves as the interpretative interface, translating the player's textual input into actions understood within that simulated world. Together, these two components transform reading into a procedural dialogue between human intention and machine structure, where meaning emerges through the interplay of interpretation, constraint and discovery.

The reader is neither passive reader nor omnipotent author, but a participant who navigates and co-creates narrative meaning through linguistic trial and error. The player must understand not only the fictional world, but also the system's language, grammar, and logic in order to proceed, in which the commands that succeed or fail generate a feedback loop of learning and adaptation (Aarseth, 1997).

Illustrative Case: Colossal Cave Adventure

For this example of fictional narrative, we'll use the "Colossal Cave Adventure" (often just called Adventure), created around 1975-1976 by Will Crowther, which is considered the first parser-based interactive fiction game and the ancestor for all of the text adventure and narrative-driven style of games (Montfort, 2003).

Figure 2 - On the left ADV on PDP-10 computer; on the right Map of Colossal Cavern

This game is considered as a "textual machine", a dynamic system that produces different outputs based on the user input (Fig. 2), and being that the game is set in a maze of interconnected textual rooms modeled after Kentucky's Mammoth Cave system (Fig. 2), each room has a short description and the player navigates through that environment by typing movement commands corresponding to compass directions or contextual cues (Montfort, 2003). Since the player does not have any visual cues of the places and layout of the cave, the player builds through imagination how it could be, same as if they were reading an ergodic book, as well as having to either memorize or draw the spatial layout as well as the geography of the places he goes through in order to traverse the cave system (Aarseth, 1997). Another part of the gameplay is interacting with objects and creatures in order to collect treasures and bring them back to a safe location. The interaction with these game elements is another puzzle that the player needs to solve, because the player needs to write the correct commands in order to interact with each one.

The "combat" is limited to just a few encounters and has the same complexity as interacting with an object. For example, there are the dwarves that appear randomly, and the player can use the command ("THROW AXE") or other object, but its success depends on chance. The other encounters are scripted within the narrative of the game, being the encounter with the dragon by killing it with its bare hands, and the Troll on a bridge, which demands the player to sacrifice an item flagged as valuable in order to proceed in the direction that the troll is blocking (Montfort, 2003). The navigation of the game world and its various challenges and items are meaningful and procedural because the player can die due to some decisions or just by misunderstanding of the game's system logic, and if the player dies either by falling to his death, getting killed by the dragon, troll or any other encounter, he loses all of the progress but creates a feedback loop that transforms trial and error into comprehension of the game system (Aarseth, 1997).

Illustrative Case: Multi-User Dungeon (MUD)

The parser system of Colossal Cave Adventure evolves directly into the Multi-User Dungeon (MUD); it extends that structure into a networked, persistent and collaborative environment (Aarseth, 1997). MUDs represent the socialization of ergodic literature. The first one was called MUD1, developed by Roy Trubshaw and Richard Bartle at the University of Essex from 1978 to 1980 (Aarseth, 1997). This collaborative system concept was heavily inspired by Gary Gygax's popular role-playing board game, Dungeons and Dragons (1974) (Aarseth, 1997). This tabletop game consisted of a cooperative fantasy storytelling game, where one of the players is the Dungeon Master and his function is to create the adventure, describe the world, control and voice the monsters and NPCs the other players will encounter. The remaining players collectively roleplay as their characters and perform their actions in the situation the DM put them in, where they might have to roll different dice that determine their outcome, described also by the DM. The system created for the "Colossal Cave Adventure" and the ambition of creating a virtual system that could in a way resemble the collective experience of DnD led them to create a new form of interactive fiction, where the reader becomes the co-author of the experience, the text becomes persistent and collective, and the narrative becomes a performance rather than a closed one-player experience (Aarseth, 1997).

"In the MUD, the user is not alone in the labyrinth; others are there as well, simultaneously weaving their own paths through the same text" (Aarseth, 1997).

MUDs are textual worlds with procedural systems, where the software interprets the user commands, updates the world state to all of the users inside, and communicates the outcome as new text. But this system made two key innovations: 1) simultaneity, where multiple players inhabit, change, and interact with that world simultaneously, and 2) its innovation with that system's persistence, where even if players log off their session, the world continues to exist and change with the other players inside (Aarseth, 1997). This form of participation is similar to the Dungeons and Dragons experience, because agency is divided between the player that explores, interacts, and communicates, the wizard (builder/Admin) that creates rooms, objects and rules for that player, and the system that mediates the commands and enforces logic (Aarseth, 1997).

This shift moves from narrative as structure to narrative as an event, where reading becomes a collaborative performance. Much like the participatory dynamics of Greek theater, where the chorus mediated emotional and moral engagement, or Augusto Boal’s spect-actors actively reshaped a play’s outcome, ergodic literature and interactive fiction distribute agency to the reader or player. Choices, actions and procedural interactions allow participants to co-create meaning, extending theater’s principle of shared narrative authorship into textual and computational media.

### 2.2.3 Interactive Fiction (Choice-Based)

Another variant of digital ergodic literature that didn't get the same attention during that period was the choice-based type of ergodic literature, which Espen refers to as hypertext, branching narratives and configurative texts (Aarseth, 1997). This genre of interactions starts in the 1980s with books such as Michael Joyce's Afternoon: A Story (1987) that is cited as the "first hypertext novel", where the story consists of a man named Peter who suspects that his ex-wife and son may have been in a fatal car accident, but that story is told in a fragmented, ambiguous, and nonlinear way, with different readings producing different sequences of events and meanings (Aarseth, 1997). This novel consists of 500 lexia (nodes) connected through hypertext links, where certain words, phrases, or blank areas are intended to be clicked on. This progresses the narrative and understanding of some of its meaning, with no definitive ‘correct’ way of going through it, where the reader must make constant decisions not knowing the consequent text that will be revealed. Afternoon is often mistaken for a "Choose Your Own Adventure" type of book, but approached the nodes and outcomes of that traversal in a different, more experimental way (Aarseth, 1997), such as… This type of literature would be a foundational inspiration for more recent frameworks created in the 2000s, such as ChoiceScript and other similar ones that take the branching hypertext novels to a more accessible and focused community, where anyone can create their own novels within this genre (Sarian, 2023).

Interactive fiction, during the time parser-based systems started to become famous, also branches into a new visual form of interactive fiction, when computers start to display graphics and new developers start to explore it with video games as a medium.

### 2.2.4 Case Study: Marc Blank’s Deadline

Figure 3 - Left: Front Cover (Atari 8-bit) Right: Front Cover (IBM PC)

Deadline is a parser-based interactive fiction game published in 1982 by Infocom (Fig. 3), written and designed by Marc Blank (one of the developers of the Zork text adventures) (Karhulahti, 2012). Built on the Z-machine, it consisted of a very limited engine at that time that allowed for the games to be easily distributed for different computers (Fernández-Vara, 2009). This limitation led to the physical package containing "feelies" (physical artifacts and documents that serve as tangible extensions of the game world) (Fig. 5), which convey information about the story world and allowed the game to also have a tactile aspect, helping the player to know more about the backstory, the crime, setting up possible suspects and scenery while freeing up space on the disk (Karhulahti, 2012). The game was released to the early home microcomputers (Apple II, TRS-80, Atari 8-bit, Commodore 64, IBM PC, and later ports).

Narrative Base

Historically, Marc Blank made the shift in Infocom from the interactive fiction adventure theme of treasure hunting (with monsters and creatures), to a grounded detective mystery setting with a lot more narrative decisions (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012). In Deadline, NPCs had their own routines and moved around the house, and certain events only happened in certain time frames, whether the player was there or not (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012). The game is significantly hard, requiring multiple playthroughs to understand and be at those time events, while also understanding and finding out all of the necessary clues, while not accusing the wrong person, in a very limited time to complete the story (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012).

Figure 4 - Map of Mr. Robner’s Mansion

Marc Blank's Deadline drama is confined within a mansion (Fig. 4) with 50 possible locations (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012). The mystery consists of finding out what happened to the wealthy businessman Marshall Robner that was found dead on the floor of his library, with a strict and punishing time limit of 12 in-game hours where, if the detective has not arrested the correct person, the Chief Inspector Klutz takes him off the case (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012).

It's recommended for the player to read the feelies and instructions manual that came with the game (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012). This helped establish and make the player feel more immersed when interacting with the game, with an "official" police report, interviews, official memos, lab report, as well as a corpus delicti (concrete evidence of the crime), a photo of the crime scene and physical evidence (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012).

The plot is complex for its time, and a clear jump in the interactive fiction genre from previous Infocom titles (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012). The plot requires the detective to find certain clues around the mansion, with a certain freedom from which one he starts with, although some evidence remains useless unless first submitted for analysis (Sergeant Duffy analyzes and reports the results), while other clues, like answering the phone and hearing the conversation, require the detective to be there in that time frame, or being at a certain location where two NPCs are going to be and taking certain actions at a certain time (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012). All of this makes Deadline a chronotope (narrative framework where time and space are intrinsically linked) game (Aarseth, 1997).

The plot is also structured in a way that clues reveal what really happened to Mr. Robner, but also reveal the motif, and the detective also uncovers certain lies that were told during the interviews, so the plot unfolds, probability narrows down to certain NPCs, that when confronted with certain clues, move to a certain location, and this leads be the climax, where the detective confronts the two NPCs that are involved in the case, and the way he deals with that certain situation or not results in different endings that result in different cathartic moments (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012).

The endings are necessary to the plot, and the game being chronotope leads to the player either being frustrated with the ending or not (Aarseth, 1997). The player can always arrest people based on his deductions, but the player can also interpret certain clues and characters wrongly, and the player needs to find all of the important clues and analyze them in a certain order, making the best ending really hard without a walkthrough (Infocom, n.d.). Even if the player finds all of the clues, if he does not make the arrests at a certain time, it will lead to one of the characters getting killed, and the player also needs to find the evidence to that crime as well, all within a strict 12 in-game hours (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012).

Player’s Role

The player’s role within the game is rewarded if he has a detective spirit within him (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012). The player, after reading the feelies and knowing about the backstory that led to the investigation, boots up the game and starts playing. For Deadline, the amount of commands recognized by the parser was greatly increased compared to previous titles like Zork, and even if the player reads the instructions manual, the frustration that comes with typing a command that the parser does not understand, although it's relevant to the plot and it would be possible considering the possible commands in the parser, leading the system to be an obstacle and not an assistant when interacting with the narrative (Fernández-Vara, 2009)(Aarseth, 1997).

Moving around each place is straightforward (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012). When the player is in a certain location, the parser lets the player know the possible directions in which the player can go, with commands like "Go North," "Go South," "Go Southeast," etc. But Deadline promises a lot more with its system. Espen Aarseth talks about Deadline in his book Cybertext but mocks, negatively, its parser system for the shallow responses from the NPCs and the parser itself in some situations (Aarseth, 1997).

There's a certain part of the game where the player must talk to the gardener, "Mr. McNabb" (Infocom, n.d.). The parser says that he's mad about something, and the player can greet him and ask "What is wrong," in which the gardener responds, "I'm sure you've read the interviews." (Infocom, n.d.) Later, while he's doing his work in a really specific point, he, without a logical reason, actually opens up to why he's upset, where he talks about someone leaving holes where he has roses, and you ask him "Show me the roses" or "Show me the holes," in which he responds, "What holes?" (Infocom, n.d.), and until you figure out the exact command that triggers the specific event, where you go to investigate those holes, you are left frustrated by the lack of recognition from the parser (Fernández-Vara, 2009). This frustration happens throughout the game, with a lot of interactions with objects and NPCs that leave you frustrated (Aarseth, 1997). This also leads the player to be unsure if certain interactions are even possible to begin with, making the game's difficulty feel unforgiving, not due to the complexity of the case but due to the system where it plays out (Aarseth, 1997).

Main Player’s Actions (and their effect on the narrative)

The main player’s actions that the player can perform that influence the narrative are related to how many clues they are able to gather throughout the gameplay and what decisions they perform that trigger certain events (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012). These decisions might require a lot of experimentation and multiple playthroughs (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012).

The actions that affect the narrative are related to the clues they need to uncover in order to arrest the correct suspects, with some of them being time-specific (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012). The player will need to listen to a call being made at 9 AM (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012). The parser does a good job alerting the player that the phone is ringing during that time (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012). The player can answer the phone by inputting the command "answer the call" (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012). This will result in the player hearing something suspicious, but he will have to wait for several minutes and listen to the phone again (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012). Another important time-specific action the player needs to go after consists of hearing why the gardener Mr. McNabb is upset, which leads the player to dig through a hole and find a missing handle of the teacup and send it to be analyzed by Sergeant Duffy (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012)(Infocom, n.d.).

The player, after uncovering some clues, can later confront certain characters, most importantly George Robner, with the calendar that says "Marshall Robner completed his will on July 8", which will make George act really suspicious with this information (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012)(Infocom, n.d.). The player will then have to follow him around until he goes to a hidden room inside the library, in which the player will need to be hidden in the balcony, or else he will be spooked and not go inside that hidden room (Infocom, n.d.). Confronting the character before a certain time will also lead him to not get an important clue("New Will") (Infocom, n.d.).

The hardest and most important clue that the player needs to get consists of the "Lab Report", which will come without any meaningful information unless the player uncovers all of the clues before in the correct order (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012). After uncovering all of them, the player will then "ANALYZE fragment for LoBlo", which will result in getting the method of murder, in which he will confront and accuse certain characters with (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012)(Infocom, n.d.). There is a really important clue that influences the ending as well, which is the "ticket stub", that the player can confront because Mr. Baxter lied about his alibi, but getting this evidence is unforgiving because he will need to follow Ms. Dunbar, where she drops that same ticket (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012)(Infocom, n.d.).

All of this leads to the multiple endings that result in how much evidence the player collected and whether they make the arrest at a specific time or not (Infocom, n.d.). Having the ticket and making the arrest when Mr. Baxter and Ms. Dunbar are meeting at the shed is crucial to solving the case correctly (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012)(Infocom, n.d.). Not having this clue and arresting them will result in insufficient evidence, because the player needs to establish motive, means and opportunity with all of his clues (Infocom, n.d.). If the player does not make the arrest at that specific time, it will lead to Baxter killing Dunbar and requiring the player to also prove that murder as well (Infocom, n.d.). Arresting the wrong character will also result in other endings, and failing to perform all of these actions within the time limit will also result in the player failing the game (The Digital Antiquarian, 2012).

Lessons for Design (why does it matter? what they did well and how)

Despite many flaws, either due to more technical aspects or narrative ones, Deadline still achieves something really great when it comes to the genre of interactive fiction.

The game establishes time as being a primary mechanic, which leads to temporal agency. All of the player's actions are within the time limit and make every decision, either spending time examining clues, moving around and confronting characters, have a sense of urgency and matter heavily to the game's conclusion, punishing wrongful use of the in-game time. This is important because the players need to keep track of evidence and the information that they get and make rational and considerate decisions that get them to finish the game with the proper ending.

This design logic can be observed in later detective RPGs, like Disco Elysium or Pentiment, that also have this mechanic incorporated in their gameplay. Wasting time or making wrong decisions that will lead to wasting time either results in missing out on important content or leads to influencing the ending in meaningful ways, making the principle that time scarcity creates meaningful choice become axiomatic in detective game design.

The second major design innovation was the implementation of NPC autonomy. NPCs moving around and acting in certain ways during the 12 hours the game takes place not only makes the NPCs feel more alive within that fictional game world, as well as making the game have a sense of emergent discovery incorporated with it, where the player needs to learn and adapt to the system, not the other way around. The player will need to look around for certain NPCs that perform different actions during a certain time, as well as certain characters only appearing in certain hours of the day, with important narrative decisions requiring the player to follow NPCs around, who perform those actions, whether the player is there to interact with them or not. This also weighs on the player's decision, because some of them lead to NPCs moving into certain locations, and if the player is not there, it will influence the resolution as well as obtaining certain clues.

This design decision might be more complicated to implement nowadays with games having graphics, but certain genres were greatly influenced by this, such as immersive sims, in which the player's decisions were systemically adapted because of the game system.

The game's flaws being related to the technology it was made on (being parser-based) also makes the need for graphics and UI to be essential when making these kinds of games. Only being a text adventure leads to the player making wrong decisions, not because they were wrong, but because the game limits them to only interact in a certain way, as previously mentioned, where not seeing the clues and only imagining them can also lead to a lot of confusion and lead to the player interpreting them wrongly, with the game feeling punishing and hard.

## 2.3 Narrative Videogames and the Reconfiguration of Player

## Agency

This chapter argues that the transition from parser-based to graphical adventure games did not eliminate ergodic agency, but redistributed it through visual interfaces, menus, and embodied avatars, reshaping how players perform narrative roles.

The ergodic nature of adventure games in the home PC market was very present aside from a few RPG games that went in a different direction. However, the introduction of graphical interfaces and mouse-driven interaction fundamentally changed how agency, immersion and direct engagement was experienced, only now through new affordances and signifiers.

In this context, affordances refer to the perceived possibilities for action made available by the interface, while signifiers are the visual or textual cues that communicate those possibilities to the player (Norman, 2013).

Janet Murray in Hamlet on the Holodeck redefines videogames, not as technical toys or ergodic literature, but as an expressive medium shifting the focus to the narrative affordances unique to digital environments having four essential properties (Murray, 1997):

1. Procedural, being the computer's ability to execute rule-based behaviors and model behaviors and relationships.
2. Participatory, with the ability to respond to the user's input, inviting the interactor to become a co-creator of the narrative experience, as seen before with interactive fiction.
3. Spatial, the power to represent navigable space, creating the feeling of presence within a story world, up until now described in text, and now being rendered through graphics.
4. Encyclopedic, having more advanced hardware to store and retrieve information, making computers more capable of allowing the creation of narratives with a lot more scope and detail.

And these properties, as Murray describes, give rise to core pleasures in digital narratives, with them being (Murray, 1997):

- Immersion, which consists of the sensation of being transported to and surrounded by a convincing, different reality, being an active creation of belief where the interactor's mind works to reinforce the reality of the experience.
- Agency as the satisfying power to take meaningful action within the virtual world and see the results of those actions, not just choice, but the feeling of enacted intention, created when the computer's systems and player's action are in total alignment.
- Transformation, with the pleasure derived from the medium's ability to represent multiple versions of a story, identities and objects.

### 2.3.1 Origins of the Graphic Adventure Interface

This evolution first started in the 1980's, with the game Mystery House (1980) being the first graphical text adventure (Montfort, 2003). Created by Roberta Williams and published by On-Line Systems, it consisted of the player and six other NPCs that are invited to a mysterious Victorian mansion. It retained the interactive fiction parser-based commands for interaction, but diminished the linguistic richness, now limited to two-word commands for interaction, making the "guess-the-verb" problem of the parser system more noticeable and frustrating.

Figure 6 - Screenshots Mystery house apple II

The novelty came from having visual immediacy featuring really simple graphics only consisting of black-and-white lines (Fig. 6), reducing the cognitive work previously required to construct mental models from just purely textual description, for a primitive representation of rooms, doors, furniture and interactable objects (Fernández-Vara, 2009). The visuals consisted of non-functional signifiers that allowed for very limited spatial awareness, which failed to help on how to actually interact with the world, with sparse text responses that provided no understanding of why a certain action failed.

The graphical novelty of its system was a remarkable achievement shifting the genre to graphical text adventure, but while text adventures during that period had better language recognition and provided better descriptions, choices and dramatic agency, *Mystery House* instead relied more on spectacle compromising affordances and having a better interactive narrative, while reducing some of ergodic "work" such as spatial mapping, object identification and imaginative construction through visual instead of text.

### 2.3.2 Animation and the Shift to Direct Manipulation

The King's Quest (1984), developed by American software company Sierra Entertainment, revolutionized the genre with a new engine AGI (Adventure Game Interpreter) being a transitional model for later graphical adventures (Tiippana, 2017).

Figure 7 - Screenshots King’s Quest DOS

The engine reduced the nontrivial effort characteristics of interactive fiction by having direct manipulation of the animated player character in real time using the keyboard arrow keys or a joystick. While most of the interactions were still parser-based, some of them were afforded by selecting them from a menu or by inputting them directly from the keyboard, with the novelty in graphical text adventure of having graphical animated representation of those actions.

The "guess-the-verb" problem was still present; even with the navigation becoming more clear and having the game world and characters being visible, the acceptable verbs were still invisible for most of the interactions. Now instead of having a visible message area where the game messages would be displayed, the graphics took up most of the screen (Fig. 7) and displayed a pop-up window as a response to the player's parser input (Tiippana, 2017).

This change made it so players spent more time immersed in the fiction by walking and observing even if the player still had to guess the correct commands; this made the experience more fluid. King's Quest reduced even more some of the ergodic aspects, such as object visibility and action feedback interpretation, and moved away from the "blind" navigation to a kinetic form of traversal.

### 2.3.3 Menu-Driven Affordances for Dramatic Agency

Portopia Serial Murder Case designed by Yuji Horii, first released on the NEC PC-6001, was a murder mystery where the player searches for clues, explores different areas, interacts with characters and solves item-based puzzles (Kretzschmar & Raffel, 2019). Originally the interactions were still parser-based, and in 1985, the game was ported to the Family Computer (Famicom), and for this port to be possible, being a parser-based text adventure, Yuji and his team created a menu system that allowed the user to easily input commands by just selecting them from a menu (Fig. 8). Portopia marks a direct shift from ergodic interactive fiction, reframing completely the linguistic aspect of typing and guessing verb conjugations, along with the frustration and breaks related to parser interactions, sustaining more the player in the fictional world.

Figure 8 - Screenshots Portopia Renzoku Satsujin Jiken Famicom (English Translation)

Portopia's menu system created explicit action diegetic affordances, with each menu option functioning as signifiers, where the system model is directly connected to the player's mental model where they are the boss giving orders to command an assistant called Yasu to perform tasks and actions, instead of addressing the computer (Kretzschmar & Raffel, 2019). Other affordances allowed were visual through point-and-click interaction where the player searches for hidden clues through a magnifying glass in certain locations, or during interrogations where the player points to the character they want to "hit".

Portopia Serial Murder Case had other novelty features besides the menu approach for inputting commands. The story consisted of a police investigation on an alleged suicide and had a lot of depth when it came to dialogue and narrative progression. The game allowed for open exploration, allowing the player to travel between multiple locations freely, with the possibility of questioning or arresting different NPCs, learning about their motives and background, gathering clues, some of them being hidden, all of this at their own pace and order.

The possible decisions the player can make have a direct impact on the story, with accusing the wrong person leading to a dead end, sometimes having to select the command "hit" to find hidden walls, asking about different objects to different NPCs, all of this influencing the multiple endings (Siliconera, 2023). The game's climax represents the ultimate expression of dramatic agency, in the game being that there's no game-over screen, which requires the player to make a deduction and force a set of menu commands in order to reveal the true killer with a twist that deepens the logic for the choice of adapting the game to a menu-based system.

As narrative videogames evolved beyond text-based interaction, concepts such as agency, immersion and affordances became increasingly tied to interface design. Rather than expressing intent through language alone, players interacted through visible signifiers and embodied actions, reshaping how narrative participation was experienced.

### 2.3.4 Orchestrating Narrative through Character Agency

All of the previously discussed games influenced the creation of Maniac Mansion (1987), developed by Lucasfilm Games, which became a foundational work for point-and-click adventure games through the introduction of the SCUMM (Script Creation Utility for Maniac Mansion) engine (Tiippana, 2017). Rather than relying on free-form text input, SCUMM formalized player interaction through a visible, menu-driven interface (Fig. 9), marking a decisive shift in how ergodic participation was structured and performed.

Figure 9 - Screenshots Maniac Mansion Apple II

Maniac Mansion is a game where the player controls different characters inside a mansion, each with different abilities, and this choice meaningfully alters how the story unfolds. Some puzzles are only solvable by particular characters, which encourages multiple playthroughs and gives weight to your selection.

In Maniac Mansion, players control a group of characters trapped inside a mansion, each possessing distinct abilities that meaningfully affect how puzzles can be solved. The initial selection of characters already constitutes a narrative commitment, where certain challenges are only solvable by specific characters, encouraging multiple playthroughs and giving weight to the player’s choice. Character death or incapacitation is not merely a mechanical setback but a narrative event that alters the story's trajectory. These outcomes reinforce the idea that player decisions matter within the fictional world, even when they lead to absurd or tragic results.

This design is supported by branching narrative outcomes, where decisions about character survival, object use and puzzle resolution influence which ending the player reaches. While the plot remains largely scripted, the path through it is shaped by player action, producing a constrained but tangible form of dramatic agency.

The game also popularized the use of cutscenes, which function as scripted narrative interludes triggered automatically by the system. These moments temporarily suspend player control to establish pacing, convey dramatic information, or reframe the narrative situation before returning agency to the player. In this sense, cutscenes operate as theatrical devices, structuring the rhythm of play while preserving the illusion of continuous participation.

The engine presented a similar layout to Sierra's AGI (ex: King's Quest), with the game world being depicted on the top half of the screen, with the bottom half listing the verbs expanding on Famicom's Portopia Serial Murder Case, not making the player guess or look outside of the game for the exact word they want to use in order to perform a certain action.

From an interface perspective, SCUMM presents a layout with the game world displayed, where interactions and animations are represented in real-time in the upper portion of the screen and a list of verbs similar to Portopia occupying the lower section that were interactive either with a mouse or using a keyboard (Tiippana, 2017). Maniac Mansion significantly reduces the frustration associated with parser-based systems by making possible actions explicit. Now agency lies in visual narrative context and is determined by the player's interpretation. Clicking a door with the "Use" verb meant "open door," not "examine door" or "push door." Since the parser was replaced by a list of verbs, the system instead understood the player's intent from the combination of verb + target.

Clicking on the environment is typically interpreted as "Walk to" and invalid actions no longer result in abstract error messages. Instead, the player character responds diegetically, refusing or commenting on the action, maintaining immersion within the fictional world. While this approach reduces the linguistic and interpretive labor characteristic of early interactive fiction, it instead replaces them with visual and spatial affordances, where the linguistic and spatial ergodic effort is no longer present, only maintaining the cognitive effort through puzzles that are now mouse-driven.

Maniac Mansion represents a pivotal moment in the history of narrative videogames, shifting from interactive fiction text adventure games to the point-and-click adventures. It already shows attempts to reconcile player agency with dramatic coherence in videogames, inciting (even in a still limited way) an approach to the concept of transformation, where characters have different capabilities, varied outcomes within a fixed dramatic frame and the mansion essentially functioning as a modular narrative system but not fully mutable story. These systems were later explored in more narrative depth in the 1990s and early 2000s, completely shaping videogames as interactive narratives.

## 2.4 Storytelling Foundations in Narrative Videogames

The 1990s and early 2000s are characterized by a "Cambrian explosion in narrative innovation" (Holmes, 2012), as technology and computational capabilities significantly improved during this time. The 1990s laid the foundation for the mass adoption of 3D technology, and with the adoption of CD-ROMs for videogames, they provided enough storage space for games to store more content and with better graphics, in addition to better quality sounds, voice-acting and cinematic cutscenes. With this technological evolution, games started to deliver more complex narratives while integrating playful and engaging mechanics, leading to different videogame genres that explored storytelling in different more expressive ways.

### 2.4.1 The Narratology vs Ludology Debate

Academically, the late 90s and early 2000s saw the birth of ludology, opening a discussion in the game studies field between ludology and narratology, and whether games should be studied based on their mechanics, interactivity and their rules or by analysing its narrative, since videogames started to develop more complex narratives where people could analyse them like they would do with a movie or a book.

The main argument presented by Gonzalo Frasca (2003) in the "Introduction to Ludology" (Frasca, 2009) is that, for games to be studied through a ludological perspective, studying them through a narrative perspective is not only inaccurate but limits the understanding of the medium and the ability to create even more compelling games. He also argues that narrative is a form of structuring representation, and that videogames are not primarily based on representation, but instead on an alternative semiotic structure known as simulation (Frasca, 2009).

Since fundamentally the way a game creates meaning is tied to the simulation aspects of digital games, referring to their rule-based systems defined by dynamic, generative simulation that offer rhetorical possibilities where unlike narratives that are fixed representation sequences, games require the interactor's participation in order to be defined as such (Frasca, 2009).

This position was a reaction to sloppy scholarship, being the result of how academics constantly treated games as if they were passive texts applying theories of literary criticism, failing to acknowledge the unique mechanical, competitive and creative dimensions of the medium (Aarseth, 2012).

The narratology response to the ludology critiques was that games can and should be studied as narrative media, and that narrative elements are essential components of videogames, being that videogames are ludo-narratological constructs, containing both ludic components and narrative ones.

This is where videogames differ from traditional media, and why Brenda Laurel proposed that mental shift to how we develop digital computer experiences (Laurel, 2014), with a new ludonarrative view of videogames making more sense when analyzing them through an academic form. These interactive narratives in videogames, unlike movies or books, allow the user to transform from a passive observer into an active participant, and both the ludology and narratology aspects of a videogame should work together in order to create a holistic experience, where player interaction feels connected to the narrative and vice versa. We can tie this to the Neo-Aristotelian theory, where agency is maximized when there's a balance between formal constraints, with plot requirements from the author and material constraints where affordances for action are provided by the virtual world (Laurel, 2014).

This framework shifted the question from "Are games narratives?" to "How can we design interactive experiences that create dramatic engagement through rule-based systems that offer meaningful agency?" (Laurel, 2014). This expands more meaningful explorations, as it acknowledges both dimensions without privileging one.

### 2.4.2 Game Design as Narrative Architecture

Henry Jenkins, in 2004, proposes the idea of game designers as narrative architects (Jenkins, 2004), connecting to Brenda Laurel's computer as theater, designing a stage, worlds and sculpt spaces and creating preconditions for an immersive narrative experience in four ways (Jenkins, 2004):

1. Embedded Narrative - The "pre-scripted" story content created by the author of the game that exists prior to gameplay (Jenkins, 2004). Examples mentioned before, more present in parser-based IFs, include scenarios where the author has already created the descriptions of items and places and the dialogue trees, where in more recent games they take form of logs and cutscenes, where ultimately the player's objective is to discover or assemble this narrative similar to a detective.
2. Emergent Narratives - That consists of the narrative that arises from the user's interaction with the system and its mechanics (Jenkins, 2004). More present in simulation games and immersive sims, where the narrative that the player gets depends on its interaction. For example, in an immersive sim like Deus Ex, the game provides certain tools that allow players to explore its game design in its own way, like moving through a level without getting noticed, or engaging in combat with enemies being just some of the ways the player interacts with the system.
3. Evocative Spaces - Where the environment itself tells the story (Jenkins, 2004), including level design, architecture and object placement that don't directly explain the story to the player (like in embedded narratives), but instead rely on the player's knowledge of the world to understand in what way it's conveying that story.
4. Enacting Stories - consisting of the player directly engaging and performing actions that move the character's story and narrative arc (Jenkins, 2004), in which the player is not passively observing a character's journey: they are performing it, similar to theater, where the distinction between the actor and the character becomes blurred and the player's actions are the character's actions, creating a direct connection to the narrative.

Jenkins' concept of game designers being narrative architects, creating virtual spaces, systems and possibilities where narratives can emerge (Jenkins, 2004), serves to acknowledge both the uniqueness of games and the importance of narratives.

Game genres that emerged or got expanded in the 1990s and early 2000s explore environmental storytelling in different ways.

Immersive sims, with games such as Ultima Underworld, System Shock and Thief, explored new forms of storytelling by shifting from rigid, scripted events to a systemic design, through consistent world rules with examples like physics, light or AI behavior, which allowed for dynamic interaction and create a reactive playground. This incited an emergent narrative from the player's action in the game world, where instead of binary choices, the player instead solves problems through enacting the story, where the player is synced with the fictional character allowing intent either from tools, or from gameplay choices that create action, resulting in visible consequences that affect the fictional world, and thus specially provides agency.

Western RPGs focused more on adapting the tabletop games like Dungeons and Dragons to a digital experience, with games like Fallout (1997) or Planescape: Torment (1999), mixed a systemic approach, with stats and rules that shape the gameplay, with branching decisions that make the game feel consequential and are often attached to moral alignment, which influences the ending. While this genre contains a lot of embedded narrative through quests, side quests and fictional world with a lot of lore, it also explores an enacted story, either through dramatic agency or through roleplaying, allowing for a lot of character transformation or seeing the consequences of moral actions taken throughout the story, which result in different endings and outcomes.

Japanese Visual Novels, like YU-NO: A Girl Who Chants Love at the Bound of this World (1996), took heavy inspiration from the foundation that Portopia Serial Murder Case created, relying its agency on a branching narrative, with the player selecting from different pre-scripted choices.In some visual novels, the butterfly effect can be noticed, with choices that were made initially having consequences later on and leading to a completely different story path, with this genre being an example of embedded narrative that allows for pre-scripted dramatic agency, even if most of these games don't have spatial or kinetic affordances.

Adventure games in the 90s leaned to have linear puzzle narratives, with games such as The Secret of Monkey Island, where the player is mostly rewarded through their logical and problem-solving techniques, with agency lying on the ability to unlock the next narrative beat by solving a puzzle. Few examples in the late 90s went in a different direction exploring more emergent and enacted narratives, especially within the mystery genre. The Last Express is an example, where players need to be in certain locations at certain times, creating emergencies within the plot, and events happen whether the player is present or not, with the player's actions leading to certain resolutions within the story.

### 2.4.3 Interaction and Narrative

These definitions serve to question a problem that, unlike Interactive Fiction that is primarily text-based, where every player interaction is limited by its text medium, videogames offer much more possibilities that are not explored when it comes to having agency within the narrative itself. During the 90s and 2000s, there were narrative systems that attempted to implement player agency in meaningful ways to shape the narrative.

Janet Murray, in Hamlet on the Holodeck: The Future of Narrative in Cyberspace (Murray, 1997), elaborates further, explaining dramatic agency is what you feel when you take an action because you care about what is happening in the story, and the system responds in a way that both acknowledges your choice and makes dramatic sense, and rewards previous anticipations appropriately. While most videogames attempt to have dramatic agency, most often narrative systems only present an illusion of choice, either through 1) branching narrative systems that are still "pre-scripted", which predict and limit player interactions but still try to deliver a coherent story, and 2) emergent narratives that offer a narrative through user's interaction with the system and its mechanics, but still feel superficial when it comes to having meaningful stories, where dialogue and interactions with NPCs are never fully explored (Murray, 1997).

Illustrative Case: Façade

Figure 10 - Screenshots Façade

In 2005, Mateas and Stern directly questioned the issue: can a system simultaneously provide players with meaningful agency, while maintaining the dramatic coherence that characterizes compelling narratives? (Mateas & Stern, 2004). Creating the interactive drama Façade (2005)(Fig. 10), the game is similar to parser-based interactive fictions, allowing the player to input anything with the keyboard to interact with two AI-driven characters that react to your inputs in real time (Mateas & Stern, 2004).

Façade places the player in the role of a long-time friend visiting a married couple, Trip and Grace, at their apartment where, within minutes, it becomes clear that their marriage is having issues (Mateas & Stern, 2004). The game abstracts human relationships through social games that overlap in real time: 1) the affinity game, determining whose side the player appears to take, 2) the hot-button game, triggering narrative topics of escalating sensitivity, and 3) the therapy game, tracking each character's self-realization about their problems, where the player's decisions directly affect the outcome for each one of these states, and influence the player to achieve one of the many endings (Mateas & Stern, 2004).

The game also contains a drama manager where the system maintains the whole story tension and dramatic coherence while allowing players to have dramatic agency over it (Mateas & Stern, 2004). Divided in short dramatic units called ‘beats’, each one is written with preconditions and effects on story variables, such as tension and character state, continuously evaluating the current narrative situation and selecting beats that best advance the overall Aristotelian tension arc, moving the experience from introduction to climax and resolution (Mateas & Stern, 2004). Lastly, there is the joint dialog behaviors (JDBs), which coordinate the specific lines of dialogue and physical actions between Grace and Trip to ensure they act in sync. This exchange, alongside the discourse management layer, transforms the player's input into discourse acts that translate keywords into the "intent" behind the player's words, allowing more system recognition and providing local agency that enables the player to see immediate, clear reactions to their interactions. Lastly, the two characters also perform autonomous behavior like walking, gesturing and interacting with objects in the scene (Mateas & Stern, 2004).

Mateas and Stern's project was important to the interactive narratives field, because it proved through Façade that narrative and agency could coexist (Mateas & Stern, 2004), exploring both local agency that consists of giving immediate feedback (for example, if the player types "Nice photo", a character immediately looks at the photo and smiles), but also global agency, meaning that actions significantly change the plot in the long-term, providing resolution to the player's decision in determining whether the character's marriage survives, ends or if you get kicked out, proving dramatic agency over just watching one (Mateas & Stern, 2004).

Illustrative Case: Prom Week

Figure 10 - Screenshots Prom Week

Later in 2013, another game project called Prom Week (Fig. 10) (McCoy et al., 2013) created by students and professors at the Center for Games and Playable Media at U.C. Santa Cruz, which included Michael Mateas and Noah Wardrip-Fruin, revolved around the social lives of eighteen high-school characters, with the central gameplay revolving around completing a set of given social goals during the week before prom. This example also doesn't rely on branching trees, using instead an underlying Artificial Intelligence system called Comme il Faut (CiF), which models social interactions based on over 5,000 sociocultural considerations, serving as the foundation ruleset of "Social common sense" within the functional world (McCoy et al., 2013).

Instead of controlling a single character, the player acts as an external observer who manipulates the social landscape by choosing multi-character interaction called "social exchanges" for the character to initiate (McCoy et al., 2013). Each action immediately affects the internal social state, defined by relationships, statuses and traits, which in turn are going to influence the actions available to the character (McCoy et al., 2013).

The creation of Prom Week is to push the long-debated question of the compatibility of agency and narrative, by making the gameplay the story itself (McCoy et al., 2013), with the core idea being that the conflicts inherent in the narrative structure can be addressed by treating the story as a dynamic social simulation that is meant to be interacted with. The AI system models the complex and emotional world, allowing the game system to present and reason over crucial narrative dimensions, creating a social space for emergent gameplay and solutions that the original designers did not necessarily pre-script (McCoy et al., 2013). This choice leads to an unfolding of the narrative that is unique through player choice. By structuring the narrative around emergent social interaction, the game avoids the complexity and work of having to build exponential amounts of branching dialogue (McCoy et al., 2013).

Ultimately, Prom Week is an example of the hybrid approach, directly addressing the conflict between ludology and narratology, by proving evidence that rule-systems, behaviors and agency can be directly combined with the narrative (McCoy et al., 2013), demonstrating that interactive narratives can be both deeply responsive to player choice and personally meaningful.

Developments of the 1990s and early 2000s, as well as research made during that time, marked a decisive shift in how developers, researchers and players approach narratives in videogames, proving that it is possible to integrate the ludology aspect of games so that they are not distinct from the narrative, but can actually serve as a way to enhance it and become a core narrative force.Unlike any other narrative medium, game narratives can be distinct and meaningful through player choice, interactions that, like theater, enable videogame design to deliver introspection and explore ideas through participation (Laurel, 2014). All of this is only achievable, however, if the designers manage to create experiences that perfectly hide the illusion of agency in their projects.

### 2.4.4 Case Study: Westwood Studio’s Blade Runner

Blade Runner (Fig. 11) is a point-and-click adventure game released on November 14, 1997 for Microsoft Windows, developed by Westwood Studios and published by Virgin Interactive. Louis J. Castle was the executive producer, art director and technical director for the project, and the movie Blade Runner, from Ridley Scott in 1982, deeply influenced the project, with the plot happening in parallel to the movie's plot, with a lot of actors cast for the movie doing the voice lines for their characters in the game, and the original visual artist for the movie Syd Mead also being part of the project as a visual consultant (Blade Runner, n.d.). The soundtrack was composed by Frank Klepacki, who recreated Vangelis's iconic film score entirely by ear, since Westwood had rights to use the music but no access to the original master recordings (MobyGames, n.d.). The game has an Enhanced Edition that was released in 2022, which brought the game to modern platforms, such as the PlayStation 4, Nintendo Switch and Xbox One (Blade Runner, n.d.).

Figure 11 - Blade Runner Box Cover & Screenshot

Beyond its production background, the Blade Runner game stands as an early and influential example of how constrained systems, simple variables, and carefully authored uncertainty can produce a strong sense of dramatic agency (Crogan, 2002). Rather than offering full narrative control, the game constructs the feeling of agency through detective mechanics, moral ambiguity and contextual variation. Player choice matters less in terms of altering the story’s structure and more in how it reshapes meaning, responsibility and self-interpretation within a tightly authored world (Crogan, 2002).

Figure 12 - Blade Runner movie Image & Movie Poster

Narrative Base

Blade Runner is a good example of transmedia storytelling, where instead of making the game follow the same exact story as the movie, the game builds on the world created from the original movie, with locations recreated for the game.Instead of adapting and using the original protagonist from the movie, Rick Deckard (Fig. 12), they go with a different protagonist rookie blade runner named Ray McCoy (Tosca, 2000). The game also has a deep intertextual relationship with Philip K. Dick's novel, Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? (1968), expanding on themes such as Humanity vs. Android/Replicant, Empathy and Identity, while also incorporating specific elements like animal extinction, "Kipple" and others from the novel (Fernández-Vara, 2016).

The game's narrative being parallel to the movie allowed the creators to directly incorporate some of the scenes, locations and characters from the movie (Fig. 13), like JF Sebastian's toy-filled apartment, Tyrell Corporation, and Ray McCoy's apartment that is similar to Deckard's, as well as many other locations, strengthened the narrative by creating an interactable version of the locations already seen in the movie, while also allowing the player to immerse themselves in the fictional world without replaying exactly the narrative from the movie (Tosca, 2000).

Figure 13 - Cutscene Screenshots

Westwood Studio's Blade Runner, like the movie and the novel, explores primarily the conflict between replicants and humans, where the philosophical question of "what it means to be human" drives both the narrative as well as the emotional investment with its characters, with the game's agency and multilinear narrative allowing the player's decisions to directly influence McCoy's identity and the progression into one of the 12 different endings (IGN, 2010). Endings are influenced by the system's random variables, determined at the beginning of each playthrough, dictating whether certain non-playing characters are replicants (MobyGames, n.d.).

The randomization of characters as replicants, not only affects the possible endings, but also change the meaning that each player gets from certain characters, potentially leading to situations where the player empathizes and emotionally connects with a character for being a replicant in a playthrough, and in another it is a human leaning into the idea of emergent narrative meaning across multiple playthroughs (Crogan, 2002).

The game's endings and themes take a lot of inspiration from film noir conventions that, unlike traditional adventure games where it's clear whether certain characters are good or evil, or if certain endings are desirable or not, the game embraces the theme of fatalism from the noir genre and extends its meaning through interaction. In this scenario, the player's decisions through the game, either enforcing the current system through his duty, symbolically represented in the end by the dog origami, or sympathizing with criminal replicants that are persecuted for wanting to have more dignity in their lives, represented with the tiger origami (IGN, 2010), brings a reflexive technology concept, where our actions with McCoy in the game reflect how the player sees the world, mirroring the actions directly to the player (Crogan, 2002).

Player Role

The player's role throughout the game, unlike traditional point-and-click adventures that have the player solving explicit puzzles and combining inventory items in order to progress through the story, is translated into puzzles as the narrative itself through detective work, making the player analyze clues from the Knowledge Integration Assistant, uncovering suspects through pictures with ESPER Photo Analysis System, questioning suspects by asking them from a list of questions or by performing Voight-Kampff Test and by going after and possibly killing possible replicants with the detective's gun (Blade Runner, n.d.).

The player can investigate and choose between each unlocked location from the map, with some of them triggering scripted events that influence the narrative directly (Blade Runner, n.d.). Within each location, NPCs move around the world and have some basic routines and different places where the player may find them. Moving around certain locations can also be influenced by the story chapter, leading to a change and urgency to the player's actions, being that some of them become inaccessible at a certain point (Blade Runner, n.d.).

As a point-and-click adventure, the moving around consists of the player clicking where the player wants their character to move, clicking on objects in order to collect them, and clicking on NPCs in order to talk with them. These gameplay features are pretty standard within the game genre, but there are new things that the player can do related to the Blade Runner universe (MandaloreGaming, 2018).

There are a variety of McCoy's personalities that the player is able to choose from either be polite, surly, normal and erratic, which don't really have any impact on the possible endings (they are more a performance to how the player wants McCoy to react). As such, the only meaningful dynamic is the player choice, which allows the player to choose before talking from a menu of dialogue choices and decisions, with only this option allowing the player's decision to actually influence the endings (IGN, 2010).

The player choice is tracked, not in how McCoy speaks during a conversation, but with actions the player deliberately chooses to do, being 1) what replicants and people the player chooses to kill or spare throughout the game (Fig. 14), 2) being able to take out his gun at any point, 3) which romance path he takes, 4) being able to pursue Lucy, Dektora or Crystal,

5. whether the player uploads evidence into the mainframe or not, which allows the player to get additional clues from other blade runners, and lastly 6) when the player chooses to perform the Voight-Kampff test, which is a test directly from the movie and novel that allows the player to choose between aggressive or light questions in order to determine if an NPC is a replicant or not (AdventureGameFan8, 2011).

Main Player Actions (and their effect on the narrative)

The main player actions that directly influence the narrative in meaningful ways are only directly influenced by who the player chooses to romance and, more importantly, who the player kills throughout the game (IGN, 2010).

Figure 14 - Gameplay Screenshots

As previously mentioned, the player can make decisions that influence his loyal path (dog origami) or sympathizer (tiger origami). This starts out from the beginning of the game until chapter 4: if the player doesn't start killing a lot of human NPCs, and does not kill any of the police members in the sewers, the player is able to redeem himself from his accusation during that act, but also requires him to clear out his name by getting the files from the twins "Luther & Lance", which sets up the player to the loyal path, where only two endings are possible from it, one where the player kills all of the replicants with Crystal, or another where she dies in a certain section of the game (AdventureGameFan8, 2011). In case the player kills many humans, cops or the character Guzza, or he doesn't have the clues necessary in order to be cleared from his accusation, McCoy then becomes a fugitive, which leads to the path of sympathizer or rogue. From that point, the player then has multiple choices within this branching decision, depending on 1) who he chooses to romance, 2) whether his romantic choice is a replicant or not, 3) if the player finds the multiple DNA information files or not, and 4) what replicants did the player kill until that point; depending on them, the player is then locked to multiple endings that either leave him to flee in a car or in a moonbus (IGN, 2010).

The fugitive ending is defined by his romances and whether he chooses to flee by car or flee in a moonbus, and if the player has all of the DNA files, which influences the ending's dialogue to be more hopeful. If the player was able to romance either Lucy or Dektora, the running away with a car ending becomes possible, even if the player kills or lets Lucy or Dektora get killed by Crystal, which leads him to be alone in the final cutscene (IGN, 2010).

The game's true agency comes in that last act, because even with the option of getting away using the car, the player can always go to the moonbus, located in a vast junkyard that they refer to as "kipple", which consists of a vast endless trash wasteland where trash appears to multiply each day. Once encountering the moonbus, the player can get the endings related to his romantic partner, or the one with only Clovis, and all of the replicants that the player spared are all going to be there. However, the game offers more agency in this situation, being that the player can always draw his weapon in order to kill replicants, and inside or outside of the moonbus he can betray his sympathizer path by killing all of the replicants at that point, leading to the loyal (dog origami) ending (IGN, 2010).

Lessons for Design (why does it matter? what they did well and how)

The lessons for design are related to what this game accomplished from a narrative and visual standpoint that influenced what branching story-driven videogames later in the 2000s had become. Still in 1997, this game accomplished a form of dynamic narrative that wasn't really explored at that time, with meaningful narrative agency that later games lacked (Tosca, 2000).

Westwood Studio's Blade Runner marked a shift from static puzzle games in the point-and-click adventures towards a more meaningful interactive narrative experience with a lot of replayability, proving that some randomized elements allowed for multiple meaningful playthroughs of the same game, with different interpretations and emotional connections with certain characters (Crogan, 2002).

The game is also a great example of the noir genre, which serves the field of interactive narratives as well, proving the new modern perspective that ludology and narratology are symbiotic (Tosca, 2000), with game mechanics and rules, such as 1) having the power to choose who to kill, 2) the randomized selection of what NPCs are replicants, as well as 3) the player agency and interactivity being able to change drastically in many points of the story, having meaningful consequences in the narrative and possible resolutions of the game through its interaction and interpretations of the story with certain clues and decisions.. All of these elements serve the narrative in meaningful ways, also serving the idea that videogames are capable of telling an interactive story in ways that TV, films or books are not (Crogan, 2002).

What the game also did well is be a great transmedia example of the original Blade Runner, not copying the original plot, but expanding the worldbuilding while respecting the novel, movie and fans of that fictional world, telling a story that approaches the same conflicting theme of what it means to be a human through a new interactive approach, while also respecting and creating a beautiful atmosphere that expands some of the original concepts (such as the Voight-Kampff test machine and many others) (Blade Runner, n.d.).

## 2.5 Constructing the Illusion of Agency in Videogames

Over the 2000s and 2010s, the videogame industry marketed games with agency and meaningful autonomy, but academic discussion surrounding interactive narratives tendentiously explored how that perceived agency is fundamentally illusory (Sweeting, 2013).

### 2.5.1 How Games Pretend to Give You Control

The illusion of agency stems from the way that all digital interactions and experiences are primarily programmed and designed for the audience, so naturally, when the player acts, it causes the game's system to react in a specific, pre-coded way, and all of the player's choices are inherently limited by the existing system's logic and conditions (Stang, 2013). This fundamental constraint means that even games explicitly marketed as choice-driven experiences are limited by the designed system, meaning that it can only respond to inputs that the system was designed to recognize, and those responses execute along anticipated programmed paths. In reality, a well-designed interactive experience is aware of this limitation, but is created in an illusory way with the system responding instantly, obeying their commands, and consistently responding the same way to their inputs, creating compelling decisions and choices that make the player feel that their actions are consequential and engaging with the overall system and narrative (Stang, 2013).

Brenda Laurel discusses constraints in Computers as Theatre, by presenting them not merely as technical limitations, but as a core dramatic tool (Laurel, 2014). Constraints are fundamental to creativity, considering that it's humanly impossible to imagine any life, even a fantasy or fictional one, without any limitation. Paradoxically, constraints help creativity by offering resistance that allows designers and users to have most imaginative power, being that any system that allows individuals to do anything that they want and desire will most likely not produce pleasant experiences, due to the possibility of paralysis of imagination or a feeling of powerlessness when faced with limitless possibilities.

Constraints are also essential for containing the action within the mimetic world (the fictional reality). Having well-established and believable constraints leads to having dramatic probability that is key to creating action, which forms the connective tissue of the plot and establishes certain causes that make certain effects probable. Laurel then distinguishes between different constraints that are perceived by the user and how they affect their engagement within their world (Laurel, 2014):

1. Explicit constraints are the ones that are openly stated to the user, such as command languages, menu options or instructions, and are less destructive to the overall engagement, being that they are presented before the action begins.
2. Implicit constraints are inferred from the behavior of the system and don't need to be explained, such as seeing a fire and assuming that it burns and it is going to affect the player character. This is ideal, because it allows the player to predict the rules without having to look for a manual or see the instructions again.

Intrinsic (Ideal) are constraints that are inherent and belong to the mimetic context. These implicitly define what people are likely to think of doing, rather than limiting physical possibilities, and are established by having probable contexts, such as limiting the player to only carry a certain amount of items because their character is human and only has two hands. They rely on the interactor's skill to recognize context-based limits automatically.

Extrinsic are limitations related to the context of the person as an interactor, not the fictional world. They consist of the game's software and physical reality in which the player is currently in, and because they don't exist within the fictional story or the game's internal logic, they have the power to remind the player that they are interacting with a machine, destroying the illusion that the designer intended to create (Laurel, 2014).

The characters can serve as a contextual constraint system, where the character's predetermined internal traits and predispositions restrict the potential actions that corresponding agents can perform. As such, the characters, being directly tied to the formal cause, are constrained in order to maintain dramatic probability and narrative coherence.

The best constraints are those that are implicit and intrinsic to the mimetic context because they keep the player in a state of perceived immersion, being the least destructive to the engagement that requires sustained attention and emotional involvement in the activity, as well as the state of flow, which is defined as the peak desirable experience of profound enjoyment and concentration with the challenge of a certain task (Laurel, 2014).

Similar to Greek theater, the player engages in meaning-making within an authored world. Alec Charles suggests the term "faux-scriptible texts," which give users the impression of meaning, power, and active participation while simultaneously "sublimating and diluting" their desire for genuine agency, so ultimately designers have to embrace this illusion in order to provide the feeling of agency and create meaningful, captivating choices (Sweeting, 2013)(Dechering & Bakkes, 2018). As a designer, it's only about what the audience perceives that really matters: the feeling of choice, the moment of deliberation, the weight of your decision, your anticipation of what might happen, results in genuine engagement, with the brain considering that choice to be meaningful. Even later, when analyzing the possible choices, it turns out that most of them lead to the same outcome (Sweeting, 2013).

### 2.5.2 Noticing the illusion

Critics and game designers identified a specific trope that captures how the illusion of choice becomes noticeable in a negative way, making them feel like the game took away their dramatic agency. They call it "But Thou Must," referring to Dragon Quest I (1986), where the princess asks the player to escort her back to the castle, presenting the player with two choices, either "Yes" or "No." If the player chooses "No," the princess says "But thou must" where the same dialogue options appear, and the princess will continue to respond the same way unless the player makes the "right choice" (Sweeting, 2013).

A more subtle approach is when player choice suffers from "consequence invariance", where the player is given a choice to pick an option and, after picking one, the player perceives their choice had no consequential value, leading the player to feel like their choice was meaningless to the overall plot (Sweeting, 2013)(Dechering & Bakkes, 2018). Regarding the matter of choice, in videogames like The Walking Dead, developers made an essential story beat, where they don't allow the player to skip the pre-designed outcome. In The Walking Dead Season 1 (2012), there's a scene where the player is given the choice to eat a certain meat or not. Even if the player refuses to eat and yells at everyone to stop, in both scenarios the others have already eaten and there's no way to change this outcome at all, but the story moves to the next scene where everything escalates, because the conflict was inevitable (Sweeting, 2013)(Dechering & Bakkes, 2018).

Another variation is when the player is presented with multiple options that lack genuine distinctions in their functional outcomes, most common in some modern RPGs where each option is just a different "flavor" of the same outcome. In Fallout 4, the dialogue options appear in a "Dialogue Diamond," in some situations offering four variations that lead to the same consequential outcome with different meaningless dialogue. In the beginning of the game, the game makes sure that there's no other way besides filling out the form, while providing options like "1. Why bother"; "2. Waste of time"; "3. I said no"; "4. You're right." While this design choice can save development resources, it leads to the player feeling frustrated and breaks the illusion, leading them to think that their choices ultimately don't matter (Alborov, 2015).

### 2.5.3 Constraints as the solution

C. Thi Nguyen (Nguyen, 2020) approaches games as fundamentally an art form of agency, emphasizing the crucial role constraints play in defining the player experience, drawing on Bernard Suits' philosophy: constraints are characterized not as mere limitations, but as voluntary attempts to overcome unnecessary obstacles. These constraints introduce specified inefficiencies into the pursuit of a straightforward objective, known as the pre-lusory goal.

Constraints are essential because they actively help constitute the lusory goal, meaning the desired objective is only valued and achieved when accomplished within the specified rules. The artistic medium itself is defined by the three primary elements sculpted by the designer:

1. the goals, 2)the rules (constraints) and 3) the environment, which together form this complete system of limitations by dictating the parameters of action, with constraints organizing interaction into crystallized and framed agential modes (Nguyen, 2020).

Nguyen argues that this strict adherence to constraints, while seemingly rigid in the short term, is instrumental in creating the player's long-term autonomy and flexibility. These restrictions constitute new and meaningful actions, enriching the overall range of options available to the player by enforcing this short-term rigidity, in which the system forces the player out of habitual patterns of thought and action, thereby promoting agential fluidity as the ability to practice and adopt different modes of agency (Nguyen, 2020).

Building on Joseph R. Worthen's concept of Narrative Superposition, designers who aim for meaningful and emotional dramatic agency must negotiate the tension between expansive player agency and a cohesive, resource-constrained narrative structure (Worthen, 2019). In this view, it is more important that the complexity of player input generate a subjective feeling of unique creation and autonomy, than that it yields a high degree of objectively different predetermined narrative outcomes (Worthen, 2019).

Good constraints guide the player to make dramatically satisfying choices and don't affect the mimetic world established (Laurel, 2014). Rules should enhance the player experience, either by having explicit constraints, where the system explicitly tells the player what the player can and cannot do, or implicit ones learned through experimentation and observation that help provide context to the player of how the system works; but, most importantly, through intrinsic constraints, the designers can build limitations that not only enhance the narrative experience, but can make their fictional world feel more believable to the player.

The idea is to embrace the constraints in order to maintain a controlled story while hiding in clever ways the limitation of player options. Instead of branching the narrative, which can lead to expensive and time-consuming developments, designers implement a structure where players' choices lead to varied paths that ultimately converge at key scripted events, climaxes and endings (Worthen, 2019). The player's perception of dramatic agency is maximized when they experience immense variance in the middle acts that result in meaningful resolutions, even if the beginning and ending don't offer a lot of choice. This structure is created in order to have a cogent narrative, leading the story to feel compelling, clear and persuasive, with different premises that support the conclusions without the need for varied main paths that lead to very different endings, which are processes that are very ambitious (Alborov, 2015).

By maximizing emotional and subjective engagement, the game can fight extrinsic limitations within the game (Laurel, 2014). Choices that provide deep, personalized, and emotional weight feel much better than complex soulless systems. Designers can create narratives and character identities that explore complex world themes and allow player agency to explore their subjective themes in a playful, detached way. By allowing players to violate social norms, taboos or ethical boundaries, the player can experience self-reflection and do a moral investigation through their actions in the story (Kappler & Mellqvist, 2022).

Ultimately, creating good constraints is fundamental and, by creating necessary obstacles, designers can create better experiences. While some genres are seen as examples of these objectives, like immersive sims or roleplaying games, another genre that shows its narrative potential without complex gameplay mechanics or systems is the mystery genre, that is built around the obstacles created by the designers in order to hide the truth from the player (Alborov, 2015).

### 2.5.4 Case Study: ZA/UM’s Disco Elysium

Figure 15 - Disco Elysium Cover & Screenshot

Disco Elysium is an isometric computer role-playing game released on October 15, 2019 (Fig. 15), for Microsoft Windows. The game was developed and published by ZA/UM, a cultural association turned game studio based in Estonia and later in the UK. Robert Kurvitz served as the lead designer and writer, creating the detailed world and narrative, while Aleksander Rostov acted as the art director, establishing the game's distinct painterly aesthetic. The soundtrack was composed by the band British Sea Power (now Sea Power), whose melancholic, atmospheric rock contributes heavily to the game's mood. The project was deeply influenced by the team's background in tabletop role-playing games, specifically a homebrew setting they had been developing for over a decade. The game received a definitive version, Disco Elysium: The Final Cut, released in March 2021, which introduced full voice acting for all characters (over a million words), new "Political Vision Quests" and brought the game to modern platforms including PlayStation 4/5, Xbox One/Series X/S, and Nintendo Switch.

Narrative Base

Disco Elysium is a prime example of "fantastic realism", a genre blend that grounds supernatural or weird elements within a gritty, sociopolitical reality. Unlike traditional fantasy RPGs that rely on elves and magic, the game instead builds a world that mirrors post-Soviet history but diverges into the surreal. The narrative takes place in the district of Martinaise within the city of Revachol, a former capital of the world now living under the shadow of a failed communist revolution and foreign occupation. The game's lore is dense with history, political theory and the concept of "The Pale," a connective tissue of reality that defies the laws of physics and mathematics, where history and the human mind can become "over-radiated" by the past, suspending the properties of the physical world.

The game's narrative structure operates on the principle of narrative superposition mentioned before (Alborov, 2015), where the game's limitations are embedded so deeply within the game's fiction that they almost become invisible. The plot begins with a classic noir "whodunit" (a mystery story centered on the puzzle of discovering who committed a crime) hook, with a protagonist waking up with severe amnesia in a trashed hostel room, tasked with solving why a man was hanged behind the Whirling-in-Rags cafeteria (Fig. 16). The investigation then expands to "a staggering number of diverging narrative possibilities" (Alborov, 2015) and yet, these possibilities are fundamentally predetermined expressions of a sense of narrative superposition that includes every potential narrative (Alborov, 2015).

Figure 16 - Screenshot of the hanged man

The expansion is structured in three distinct story layers: the corpse investigation, the political conflict in Martinaise, and Harrier discovering his identity after the amnesia. All these layers expand and collapse into each other, with the game integrating political philosophy as a core mechanic, where political views like communism, fascism, capitalism and centrism are integrated on all layers, with the detective's identity being shaped by these different views that can influence both dialogue, role checks and the ending dialogue.

Unlike traditional branching narratives where the ending is the only goal, Disco Elysium focuses on the texture of the journey. The player investigates the murder while simultaneously navigating a strike between the Dockworkers' Union and the Wild Pines corporation, dealing with a cryptozoologist's search, or engaging in a chaotic attempt to set up a nightclub in a cursed church. These subplots strengthen the narrative by allowing the player to immerse themselves in the mundane and the metaphysical simultaneously, creating a "material network" where seemingly small choices define the reality of the protagonist.

The narrative base, although being thematically a detective game, goes in a different direction by making the central mystery impossible to solve until the game's final moments (Alborov, 2015), essentially tricking the player into thinking they are making their own path and progressing through the investigation, but being contained by the narrative into following a pre-written path. Essentially, the mystery functions as the theme for the narrative, not its main objective and mechanical foundation. The investigation provides structure for the detective's personal mystery, with this being the narrative's true focus for essentially reconstructing Harry psychologically, while the killer serves as a narrative gear to justify exploring the fictional world.

Player Role

Contrary to traditional RPG design, Harry Du Bois is not a blank slate for player projection, but a complex character with a concrete past and a limited range of characteristically possible behavior (Alborov, 2015), with the player role not being to create his character, but instead uncover him by making choices that lead his character to redeem itself or to continue his internal collapse. Those choices create the protagonist's fractured psyche, which consists of 24 distinct skills (Fig. 17) that determine both his abilities and thoughts throughout the game (Sweeting, 2013). Skills consist of a mechanic that allows the player to customize the detective for each playthrough, affecting the protagonist's ability to perceive, interpret and interact within the fictional world.

Figure 17 - Screenshot of the Skills

The player navigates Martinaise in a standard point-and-click fashion, highlighting objects and talking to NPCs, but depending on the character's position and actions, passive checks are triggered automatically if a certain skill level requirement is met. These provide lore, background, or give a deeper understanding of a situation, not being required to progress through the plot, but adding a lot of content and role-playing flavor to the experience, with anti-passive checks happening when skills are too low and providing a response that comments on that failure. For example, there's a situation where the player is talking to Joyce Messier, a highly educated and wealthy negotiator, and the player wants to sound like they understand the complex political landscape. If the player has a very low Rhetoric skill, an anti-passive response appears saying: "She's using big words to make you look like an idiot. She thinks she's better than you. You should tell her that 'Money is just paper, lady!' That'll show her you've got a handle on the socio-economics of the situation." Besides the passive checks, there are also active checks, deliberately chosen by the player, where white checks can be retried and red ones cannot. These checks work similar to the tabletop game Dungeons and Dragons, by having the system roll two six-sided dice against a set difficulty. The result is calculated as skill level + modifiers + dice roll, where an equal or higher result wins. Failing these checks leads to an obstacle to the main goal, and where the developers excelled with their game design, because failing these checks usually results in the player having to go on a different route with more meaningful content (Stang, 2013).

The game also has the Thought Cabinet mechanic that allows the player to "internalize" ideas, ranging from political ideologies like "Mazovian Socio-Economics" to personal obsessions like "The Suicide of Kras Mazov." Internalizing these thoughts takes in-game time and applies temporary stat debuffs but, once completed, they permanently alter the protagonist's stats and unlock new dialogue options.

There are a variety of "Copotypes" the player can embody, from Sorry Cop, Superstar Cop, Apocalypse Cop, or Boring Cop, which don't just change flavor text, but alter how the world reacts to Harry (Sweeting, 2013). The game tracks the player's political alignment (Communist, Fascist, Ultraliberal, Moralist) through dialogue choices, eventually unlocking specific "Political Vision Quests" in the Final Cut version (Sweeting, 2013). These quests are not just side missions but deep dives into the absurd and tragic nature of the chosen ideology, allowing the player to perform their role to its logical, and often farcical, conclusion.

Main Player Actions (and their effect on the narrative)

While Disco Elysium emphasizes cumulative micro-decisions over explicit branching points (Sweeting, 2013), several critical moments create substantial narrative divergence and determine ending configurations.

Skill checks function as the primary gating mechanism for narrative access, operating in two distinct forms with fundamentally different narrative implications (Stang, 2013): 1) White Checks, which can be retried if the player levels up the corresponding skill or uncovers new information (modifiers) and 2) Red Checks, which can only be attempted once and affect the narrative the most. The red skill checks are also influenced by previous decisions, meaning that, depending on certain actions and information, it increases the chance of a certain check to succeed.

Crucially, the game weaponizes failure as a narrative tool (Alborov, 2015). Failing a White Check leads to an obstacle, where the player needs to unlock them and try again with better stats. Failing a Red Check doesn't stop progress but branches the story in a new, often more interesting direction (Stang, 2013). For example, there's an event where the player is tasked with bringing the body down from the tree. The player can attempt a really hard skill check and shoot the body down, but failing this check is actually a much better result narratively, leading to the player having to figure out a way to bring the body down and opening the Dockworker's Strike subplot, which helps the player understand the conflict that is happening in the game-world much better. By minimizing the penalty for failure and rewarding non-optimal outcomes, the system encourages experimentation, validating the player's impulse to embrace the chaos and creativity, while also enhancing agency towards self-expression rather than making the player reload a saved state in order to stick with the most "optimal" path.

While most of the Red Checks are meant to drive the player towards a more interesting path, some of the later checks in the game have dramatic changes to the plot, determining whether certain characters live, die or get injured. A certain later event called The Mercenary Tribunal is seen as the climax of the game, being a confrontation with a lot of red checks and decisions that lead characters to get killed, while also branching the narrative in a significant way by either leaving the protagonist to finish the game alone, with Cuno, or with his partner Kim.

Figure 18 - Screenshot of the player talking to Kim

The agency in Disco Elysium lies in the "micro-choices": the specific lines of dialogue chosen, the clothes worn (which modify stats) and the drugs consumed (Sweeting, 2013). These choices accumulate to define Harry's relationship with his partner, Kim Kitsuragi (Fig. 18). Earning Kim's respect is often cited by players as the game's true "good ending," achievable only through consistent, competent and empathetic actions, regardless of the specific political path taken. The game tracks these subtle variables, leading to different final interactions with the precinct and the city itself.

Lessons for Design

Disco Elysium's most profound contribution to interactive narrative design lies in its resolution of the fundamental paradox between narrative coherence and player agency through "narrative superposition" (Alborov, 2015). Rather than creating genuinely divergent storylines, the game generates the illusion of agency disproportionate to actual agency (Alborov, 2015) by using hard restraints, where the murder mystery remains unsolvable until act three and only having six endings, and soft restraints, which serve to create the desire in players to have the same ending on a first playthrough. This framework demonstrates that interactive narratives can feel highly variable, while ensuring every player receives a narratively satisfying, coherent experience. The key insight is in clearly distinguishing between what players control (character identity, relationships, worldview) and what they don't (central narrative arc, story resolution), communicating these boundaries explicitly rather than pretending to offer unlimited choice.

The game reconceives player agency itself, moving beyond the "more choice equals better experience" fallacy that dominates interactive narrative theory (Laurel, 2014). Writer Justin Keenan articulated ZA/UM's philosophy: players have limited agency over "the material circumstances of the world" but "profound control over what that world represents to you". This bounded agency approach recognizes that agency operates across multiple dimensions: functional, narrative, configurative, world and identity (Stang, 2013), with players valuing different dimensions based on individual preferences. By excelling at identity agency (expressing Harry's ideology and personality) and world agency (environmental responsiveness through micro-reactivity) while carefully limiting narrative agency (plot outcomes), Disco Elysium creates the feeling of meaningful choice without requiring exponentially branching storylines. Research confirms this psychological reality: perceived agency matters more than afforded agency (Sweeting, 2013), and "agency is better understood as a commitment to meaning instead of a desire to act freely" (Sweeting, 2013).

Disco Elysium's mastery of what the industry controversially calls "illusion of choice" demonstrates that this technique, when executed with sophistication, constitutes legitimate design rather than deception (Sweeting, 2013). The game's micro-reactivity system tracks thousands of minor player decisions, such as shaving mutton chops, selecting call signs, expressing political views, all reflected in subsequent dialogue (Sweeting, 2013), creating the psychological sense that every choice matters even when they don't alter central plot outcomes. This approach acknowledges fundamental human psychology: the value of choice lies not in divergent outcomes, but in the process of deciding and the emotional investment created when characters remember and respond to player actions [5]. The game combines few genuinely consequential choices (political vision quests, companion fates) with masterful illusions (aesthetic dialogue that exists purely for character expression, or thought internalization that affects perception rather than outcomes), allocating resources strategically to create perceived agency matching player engagement needs.

Disco Elysium proves that interactive narratives can achieve both the narrative depth of great literature and the agency satisfaction of great games, but also exposes the limitations of perceived agency when it restricts the player from figuring out the truth by himself. Although the game is framed through the conventions of detective fiction, it systematically removes deduction as a meaningful mechanical action. Clues are accessed through skill checks rather than analytical reasoning, and the central mystery remains structurally unsolvable until the narrative allows its resolution (Alborov, 2015). The result is an experience that excels at identity formation and interpretive freedom, yet fails to operationalize the core function of the mystery genre: the transformation of player ignorance into knowledge through active cognition.

## 2.6 Mystery as the Architecture of Agency

While previous chapters have explored the evolution of ergodic literature and the construction of agency, this chapter posits that the mystery genre is not merely a thematic category, but a structural necessity for the highest forms of interactive narrative. The mystery genre is fundamentally epistemic, being driven by the desire to know and uncover the truth. Unlike ontic narratives, which focus on the transformation of the world or character, epistemic narratives focus on the transformation of the player's knowledge (Ryan, 2013).

This structural alignment makes mystery particularly suited for games. As scholars such as Robert Palmour have stated, the detective story has arguably "always belonged" to video games (Palmour, 2021). In traditional media, such as literature, film and television, the audience is restricted to the role of Dr. Watson, as a passive observer marveling at the genius of a Sherlock Holmes who performs the actual cognitive labor. While in interactive narratives, the player becomes Holmes, with the medium fulfilling the genre's promise by shifting the "thrill of analysis" and deduction work from a passive audience to one that actually requires a form of active participation.

The mystery narrative is composed of two distinct, temporal layers, a duality first identified by Tzvetan Todorov and adapted for game studies (Fernández-Vara, 2018)[37]:

- The Story of the Crime: The events that occurred before the game began (the fabula). This is a fixed, immutable past containing the truth of "whodunit".
- The Story of the Investigation: The events occurring in the present (the syuzhet), where the player traverses space and time to reconstruct the Story of the Crime.

To understand how mystery games actualise this epistemic shift, it is essential to examine the specific cognitive operations that drive investigation. First formalised by logician Charles Peirce and later identified as central to the Holmesian method by scholars such Umberto Eco and Thomas Sebeok in the book The Sign of Three: Dupin, Holmes, Peirce Advances in Semiotics(Eco & Sebeok, 1983), they identify three forms of reasoning that structures the process of reconstructing a hidden reality from fragmentary evidence and information:

1. Deduction is reasoning from the rule (a general principle or law) and a specific Case (an instance fitting that rule) to infer the result, aiming to demonstrate that something must be true. Example — Rule: All the beans from this bag are white. Case: These beans are from this bag. Result: These beans are white.
2. Induction is reasoning from a Case and the Result observed in that case to infer the general Rule, learning by experience, looking at what happened in specific instances and using those results to guess a rule that applies to everything. Example — Case: These beans are from this bag. Result: These beans are white. Rule: All the beans from this bag are white.
3. Abduction is distinguished as the only logical operation that introduces a new idea, being the reasoning from the Rule and the observed Result to propose a plausible Case, suggesting only that something may be true (the "Aha!" moment when a detective forms an explanatory hypothesis from a clue). Example — Rule: All the beans from this bag are white. Result: These beans are white. Case: These beans are from this bag (Eco & Sebeok, 1983).

In detective games, these three reasoning forms are done in a cognitive loop, where Abduction proposes stories, Deduction tests them against traces and Induction refines expectations, all of this turning into a core cognitive mechanic. In standard "Adventure Detective Games" (e.g., L.A. Noire or Ace Attorney), the player merely triggers the next beat of the Investigation story by only performing some of this cognitive work, where most of the cases require just pushing a button to watch the detective solve the crime (Larsen & Schoenau-Fog, 2020). However, in "Puzzlebox Detective Games", the player actively authors the story of the investigation, in which the game does not tell the player what they found; instead, the player has to inspect indexical traces and construct the narrative themselves.

### 2.6.1 Indexicality and the Environment as Narrative

A critical mechanism for this translation of agency is Indexical Storytelling, which provides a theoretical framework for understanding how detective games encode narrative in their environments. Building on Charles Peirce's semiotics, Fernández-Vara defines indices as signs that have a direct physical connection to the events they represent, for example, a bullet hole implying a shot or a bloodstain implying a struggle (Fernández-Vara, 2011).

Indices have a causal relationship to their origins being remnants, traces, or consequences of events — "An index has a relation to the event, often is the consequence of it, which points to something that happened or is going on, inviting the player to reconstruct what happened" (Fernández-Vara, 2011). This reconstruction, which consists of moving from trace to event, is the core cognitive activity of detective work. Similar to what Tzvetan Todorov established, indexical storytelling operates on two temporal planes, having scripted indices designed to establish previous events that occurred before the player's arrival, and the indices created by the player's own actions, such as notes taken, connections made, theories that are formed and discarded, making the whole investigation from the player part of the narrative itself.

What makes indexical storytelling suited for detective games is the inherent ambiguity that comes with the investigation. A bloodstain is an index of violence, but raises many questions such as: “Whose blood is it from? When was it spilled? Was it murder, self-defense, or an accident?”. This leads to the same physical traces having multiple competing narratives, where the player’s challenge is to gather enough indices to narrow all possible interpretations into one coherent narrative.

In mystery narratives, indexical storytelling is different from general environmental storytelling by requiring active interpretation. The player's movement through space is not just traversal, but an act of reading. This transforms the "nontrivial effort" of ergodic literature into investigative labor. The player's agency is expressed through Narrative Systems that distinguish between "discovering" information (finding a clue) and "digesting" it (understanding its implication) (Rittenhouse et al., 2023).

### 2.6.2 Contemporary Innovations in Mystery Mechanics

Since the 2010s, there has been an explosion of innovative detective games that operationalize epistemic agency in novel ways. These titles demonstrate how constraint, fragmentation and systematic deduction can create deeply engaging narrative experiences, moving away from adventure detective games that make the player an observant, to systems that require the player’s active participation and reasoning in order to progress through the story.

Figure 19 - Screenshots Obra Dinn

1. The Spatial Puzzlebox: Return of the Obra Dinn Obra Dinn (Fig. 19) represents the pinnacle of "gating" information through spatial exploration. The player's agency is constrained but potent; they must actively deduce identities and causes of death to "lock in" truths in a notebook. The notebook functions as a "cognitive artifact", externalizing the player's deductive process and turning the mental act of solving into a tangible game mechanic (Rittenhouse et al., 2023).

Figure 20 - Her Story & Telling Lies

2. The Database Narrative: Her Story & Telling Lies The titles of both these games (Fig. 20) strip away the physical world entirely, presenting the mystery as a pure Information Game. The main mechanic is a search query: the "gate" is not a locked door, but the player's own vocabulary as they won’t be able to find a video clip about "murder" unless they think to search for it. Agency here is linguistic and epistemic: the narrative structure is non-linear and exists wholly within the player's reconstruction of fragmented database entries (Rittenhouse et al., 2023).

Figure 21 - Outer Wilds Screenshots

3. The Conceptual Key: Outer Wilds Outer Wilds (Fig. 21) employs conceptual gating by transforming the solar system into a "living" information game, where the primary obstacle is the player's own ignorance of the world's physical and historical laws. Here, the "key" to the finale is not an item found in a chest, but a set of logical inferences pieced together from alien fragments, making the player's mental breakthrough the only true requirement for progression.

Figure 22 - Shadows of doubt & Mind Map gameplay

4. The Simulated City: Shadows of Doubt Shadows of Doubt (Fig. 22) represents the shift from curated mysteries to procedural epistemic agency. Unlike the hand-crafted "gates" of the previous titles, the game utilizes a systemic simulation, where every citizen has a routine, a workplace and a social network. The player's agency is expressed through Information Management within a living sandbox; they must navigate a deluge of "simulated traces", without a designer-led trail of breadcrumbs. Here, the "Information Game" becomes a matter of triangulation: the player must cross-reference disparate data points generated by the game's engine to isolate a suspect, making the investigation an emergent struggle against the noise of a fully simulated urban environment.

Through indexical storytelling, mystery becomes an ideal framework for interactive narratives allowing for dramatic agency, being that, in a well-designed mystery, the mechanics of interaction (searching, logging, deducing) are indistinguishable from the narrative act of solving the crime, achieving ludonarrative harmony through its design.

### 2.6.3 The Mechanics of Contradiction: Weighing Truth Against Testimony

While the "Spatial Puzzlebox" and "Database Narrative" offer broad structures for epistemic agency, a more specific and aggressive form of cognitive engagement is found in what can be termed "Contradiction Style" gameplay. This mechanic, most famously codified by the Ace Attorney series and the Danganronpa franchise, has recently been formalized as 'Turnabout mechanics' in computational narrative research (Yuan et al., 2025), shifts the player's role from a passive collector of clues to an active auditor of reality. In this framework, the core gameplay loop acts as a rigorous test of the player's internal "Story of the Investigation," forcing them to weaponize their knowledge to dismantle falsehoods.

The Architecture of the Turnabout

The defining characteristic of "Contradiction Style" gameplay is the Cross-Examination or Non-Stop Debate. Unlike traditional adventure games, where an item is used to unlock a door, an item here is used to "unlock" the truth hidden within a spoken statement. As defined in recent deductive reasoning benchmarks, the player is presented with a testimony (a subjective account of the "Story of the Crime") and must identify a specific logical inconsistency by presenting a piece of evidence that disproves it (Yuan et al., 2025).

For example, a witness might claim, "I saw the victim die at 1:00 PM", but the player holds an autopsy report stating the time of death was 4:00 PM. The gameplay interaction is not merely selecting a dialogue option, but it's the act of colliding the objective fact (the autopsy report) against the subjective lie (the testimony) to trigger a narrative breakthrough. This creates a "short-term game loop" where discovery feeds deduction, which in turn feeds further discovery.

Epistemic Weight and the Semantic Inventory

This mechanic fundamentally transforms the ontological status of "items" in the player's inventory. In a typical adventure game, an inventory object is a functional tool, such as a key that fits a lock or a crowbar that opens a crate. In Contradiction Style games, items become semantic keys, not defined by their physical utility, but by their informational content and meaning.

The "weight" of an item in these games is derived from its ability to alter the narrative landscape. A "rusty knife" is no longer just a weapon: it is the physical proof that contradicts a killer's claim that the murder weapon was brand new. This aligns with the "Burden of Proof" concept discussed by Jon Ingold (Ingold, 2019), where the player must constantly "prove" their mental model to the game system. The inventory screen effectively becomes a database of "frozen truths" that the player must manually cross-reference with the fluid (and often deceptive) narrative unfolding in real-time. This turns the act of sorting through an inventory into a high-stakes cognitive activity, where the player must understand not just what an object is, but what it implies in the context of the current conversation.

Validating Epistemic Agency

The importance of this style to epistemic storytelling lies in its demand for precise player understanding. Ingold (Ingold, 2019) notes that detective games often rely on a "combination lock" design, where the player knows the answer but must find the specific input to express it. Contradiction gameplay refines this by making the "input" a test of logic rather than trial-and-error. The player cannot progress simply by clicking through dialogue; they must possess the correct epistemic key (the evidence) and apply it to the correct narrative lock (the contradiction) .

This structure ensures that the "Story of the Investigation" and the "Story of the Crime" synchronize perfectly. The game refuses to advance until the player demonstrates they have successfully reconstructed the specific beat of the past that invalidates the present lie. By forcing the player to spot the "Turnabout", there is the decisive moment where the flow of the argument completely reverses based on a single fact. These games achieve a unique form of ludonarrative harmony, where the mechanics of objection and the narrative act of truth-seeking are identical.

If the "Contradiction" mechanic relies on the precision of logic, the "Dream" mechanic relies on the precision of interpretation. While previous forms of mystery focus on the Story of the Crime (a causal sequence of physical events), dream-based narratives shift the epistemic object to the Story of the Wound (an affective sequence of psychic events). In these spaces, the mystery is not "who did it", but "what is being repressed?".

### 2.6.4 Case Study: Capcom’s Ace Attorney Investigations - Miles

### Edgeworth

Figure 23 - Ace Attorney Cover & Screenshot

Ace Attorney Investigations: Miles Edgeworth (Fig. 23) (known in Japan as Gyakuten Kenji) is a single-player adventure game released on May 28 of 2009, in Japan and in February of 2010 internationally for the Nintendo DS. The game was developed and published by Capcom, with Takeshi Yamazaki serving as the director and Motohide Eshiro as the producer. Unlike the main Ace Attorney titles created by Shu Takumi, this spin-off marked a shift in directorial vision, focusing on the popular rival character Miles Edgeworth. The game retains the distinct anime-style visual novel aesthetic of its predecessors, but transitions gameplay from first-person static screens to a third-person isometric perspective, allowing for direct spatial exploration. The soundtrack, composed by Noriyuki Iwadare and Yasumasa Kitagawa, creates a more urgent, investigative atmosphere distinct from the courtroom drama of the main series. The game has seen subsequent releases on iOS/Android (2017) and remains a cult favorite for its narrative interconnectedness and experimental mechanics.

Narrative Base

Ace Attorney Investigations represents a structural shift from the "Whodunit" courtroom drama of the main series to an inverted mystery and conspiracy thriller. While the core Ace Attorney games function as procedural dramas focused on the defense of a specific client, Investigations operates as a "hardboiled" procedural, where the narrative drive is not legal defense, but the proactive pursuit of an objective truth. The plot follows high-ranking prosecutor Miles Edgeworth, a character defined by his rigid adherence to logic and truth, as he becomes entangled in a sprawling international smuggling ring.

The narrative is divided into five episodes that, unlike the episodic nature of previous titles, are tightly interconnected chronologically and thematically. The story begins with a murder in Edgeworth's own office, and spirals outward to involve a legendary thief known as the "Yatagarasu" and a transnational smuggling syndicate. This structure allows the game to explore themes of systemic corruption and the limitations of the law, forcing Edgeworth to confront legal paradoxes where the "law" and the "truth" are at odds.

The narrative base is notable for its spatial storytelling. By moving the action out of the courtroom and into specific locations, such as an airplane in flight, a kidnapper’s theme park hideout or an embassy split between two nations, the game grounds its mystery in the physical logic of these spaces. The "locked room" mysteries are not abstract puzzles described by witnesses: they are physical environments the player must navigate (Fig. 24), reinforcing the narrative theme that the truth is hidden in the tangible details of the world.

Player Role

The player’s role undergoes a significant transformation from the main series. Instead of playing as the underdog defense attorney Phoenix Wright, who relies on bluffing and intuition to turn the tables, the player assumes the role of the "Logical Dominator" Miles Edgeworth. This shift fundamentally alters the power dynamic of the gameplay, where the player is no longer just reacting to accusations: the player is the authority figure driving the investigation.

Figure 24 - Screenshot of the open exploration in Miles Edgeworth’s Office

Edgeworth acts as a Rationalist Archetype, viewing the world as a chaotic system that must be ordered through logic. The player’s agency is expressed through this worldview, collecting facts instead of just collecting item. The game visualizes Edgeworth’s internal thought process as a mechanic, placing the player in the role of a cognitive architect who must construct a solid theory from fragmented data.

This role is further defined by the isometric perspective. The player controls Edgeworth directly, walking him through crime scenes. This change from a static "point-and-click" view to an avatar-based navigation reinforces the player’s role as an active investigator, physically present in the narrative space, rather than a disembodied observer.

Main Player Actions (and their effect on the narrative)

The gameplay of Ace Attorney Investigations introduces specific mechanics that operationalize the act of Deduction (described in Section 2.6), effectively exploring the cognitive labor of detective work.

Figure 25 - Logic Mechanic

The most distinct action is the "Logic" mechanic (Fig. 25). Unlike traditional adventure games where an inventory holds physical items (keys, guns), Edgeworth possesses an inventory of abstract thoughts (e.g., "The gun was fired twice," "The window was locked"). The player must enter the Logic menu and manually "connect" two disparate thoughts. If the connection is valid, a new piece of information or evidence is generated (e.g., connecting "Locked Window" + "No key found" = "The killer is still inside"). This action directly affects narrative progression by forcing the player to digest information rather than just collect it.

Figure 26 - Contradiction Mechanic

The player can also navigate the 3D space to find contradictions between the physical scene and the evidence file. The player points a cursor at a specific object in the environment (e.g., a clock showing the wrong time) and "presents" a conflicting piece of evidence (Fig. 26).

This action transforms the environment from a background into an antagonist that is hiding the truth. Successfully exposing these contradictions triggers narrative breakthroughs, often dismantling a suspect's alibi in real-time.

Introduced via the sidekick character Kay Faraday, this mechanic allows the player to generate a holographic simulation of the crime scene at a previous point in time. The player explores this "virtual past" and can toggle between the "Before" and "After" states. This action allows the player to investigate the Story of the Crime (Fabula) directly, interacting with the past as if it were a physical space. It serves as a narrative device to visualize theories and test hypotheses without breaking the game's timeline.

The game adapts the "Cross-Examination" mechanic of the courtroom into "Rebuttals" that happen in the field. Edgeworth argues with witnesses or rivals in real-time, pressing statements for more information and presenting evidence to expose lies. This frames dialogue as a form of logical combat, where the player's action is to dismantle the opponent's argument piece by piece.

Lessons for Design

Ace Attorney Investigations offers critical lessons for the design of immersive mystery narratives, particularly in how it bridges the gap between exploration and deduction.

What the game did remarkably well was creating a mechanic that explores the internal thought process of a detective through its Logic System. In many mystery games, the protagonist automatically makes the deduction once a clue is found, robbing the player of the "Aha!" moment. By forcing the player to manually connect "Thought A" and "Thought B," the game ensures that the player is cognitively engaged with the narrative. This validates the design principle that true agency in a mystery comes from understanding the information, not just finding it.

The game effectively demonstrates Indexical Storytelling in a 3D space: by allowing the player to confront the environment directly (Deduced mechanic), the game teaches that the setting itself is a text to be read. The reward for the player is not just narrative progression, but the satisfaction of noticing what other characters missed. This creates a deep sense of engagement, as the player feels their own observational skills are being rewarded, rather than their ability to follow a quest marker.

The Little Thief mechanic provides a powerful blueprint for how to handle reconstruction in mystery games. Instead of passive flashbacks, it creates an interactive "virtual past" that the player can explore. This is a direct lesson for designing systems involving dream sequences or psychological exploration, showing how subjective the past states can be rendered as navigable game spaces.

Finally, the game succeeds in making the player feel empowered through intelligence. The "Rebuttal" sequences, accompanied by dynamic music and visual flourishes (the "Objection!" bubbles or the finger-pointing animations, for example), frame intellectual labor as high-stakes action. This proves that a mystery game does not need physical combat to be exciting: the combat of ideas, where the player wields evidence like a weapon to "break" a suspect's lies, creates a compelling and distinct form of dramatic agency.

### 2.6.5 Dreamscapes as Investigative Environments

This shift from contradiction style gameplay to dreams requires a new form of epistemic agency, the Dream Epistemic Storytelling. Here, the environment is no longer a container for indexical traces (bloodstains, fingerprints), but a repository of symbolic traces (recurring motifs, impossible geometries, shifting atmospheres). As scholars like Frank Bosman have argued (Bosman, 2014), dream sequences in games are rarely passive cinematic interludes, but "lucid" interactive devices where the player retains agency to navigate a constructed alternative reality. In this framework, exploration becomes the investigation. The player must treat the dreamscape as a "psychic map", navigating it to reconstruct the dreamer's hidden interior self, just as a detective navigates a city to reconstruct a murder.

Figure 27 - Yume Nikki Dream Screenshots

The purest example of this form is Yume Nikki (Dream Diary), a title that strips away all traditional detective conventions such as dialogue, combat or an explicit plot, leaving only the act of navigation (Bommarito, 2014). The protagonist, Madotsuki, refuses to leave her apartment in the waking world, but in her dreams, she traverses vast, surreal environments (Fig. 27) populated by grotesque figures. Exploration serves as decoding where the player collects "Effects" (transformations like a knife, a towel, or a traffic light), which function not as power-ups, but as identity fragments or symbolic keys (Bommarito, 2014).

It is also Socially Epistemic, because the game provides no answers: the "investigation" spills out of the software into the real world. Players collaborate to decode the symbolism of the "Toriningen" or the "White Desert," effectively turning the community into a collective detective agency. As researchers Bommarito and Dunlap (Bommarito, 2014) note, this creates an "empathy dreamscape," where the struggle to understand the game's opaque logic mirrors the struggle to understand the isolated mind of a hikikomori (shut-in).

Where Yume Nikki offers silence, titles influenced by David Lynch's Twin Peaks offer "unreliable" noise. In narratives of trauma, the subconscious obscures the truth to protect the subject. As Moyra Donnelly argues regarding Twin Peaks: Fire Walk With Me, the dreamscape blurs the "inner and outer worlds" (Donnelly, 2015), creating a space where time is dilated and identity is fragmented to represent the "fractured psyche" of the survivor.

In Alan Wake, the "Dark Place" manifests the protagonist's fears into physical threats. The investigation involves collecting manuscript pages, literal "Story of the Investigation" written by the character himself, which the player must use to survive the "Story of the Crime" that is rewriting reality in real-time. The investigation follows "dream logic" rather than forensic logic. A clue might be a sudden shift in lighting or a recurring symbol that signals the intrusion of a repressed memory. The player's agency lies in recognizing these patterns, an act of abductive reasoning where the "rule" is the character's emotional state(Eco & Sebeok, 1983).

Figure 28 - Max Payne Prologue Chapter

Finally, dream investigation can take the form of forced reenactment. In Max Payne (Fig.28), the drug-induced nightmare levels force the player to navigate a distorted version of the protagonist's home, following trails of blood and hearing the screams of his murdered family. Unlike the "fun" of a power fantasy, these sections strip the player of agency (slowing movement, distorting audio) to force them to inhabit the character's helplessness. The "mystery" here is not what happened ( as Max knows his family is dead), but the experience of that loss. The gameplay forces the player to perform the "cognitive labor" of grief, walking the labyrinth of guilt that the character cannot escape.

Ultimately, Dream Epistemic Storytelling offers a unique evolution of the mystery genre by internalizing the investigative process. It suggests that the 'thrill of analysis' can extend beyond logic into the fluid ambiguity of the human psyche. By encouraging players to treat the environment as a symbolic text, these games reposition the detective as a translator of subconscious experiences rather than just solving logic puzzles.

## 2.7 Summary

Across its many forms, such as indexical environments, contradiction-based dialogue, conceptual gating, procedural simulation and dreamlike symbolic spaces, the mystery genre reveals itself not as a narrative theme, but as an architecture of agency structured around epistemic labor. In each case, player action is meaningful not because it alters the world directly, but because it transforms uncertainty into understanding through acts of interpretation, inference and synthesis. Whether the player is reconstructing a crime from physical traces, dismantling a false testimony through contradiction, or navigating a dreamscape to decode psychological wounds, agency emerges from the same core operation: the authorship of knowledge.

This analysis exposes a critical fault line in contemporary interactive narrative design. While many games adopt the aesthetics of mystery, only a few actually commit to the epistemic work. When deduction is automated, truths are gated by arbitrary progression, or contradictions are resolved by the system rather than the player, with mystery collapsing into spectacle. The result is a perceived agency that feels expressive, but remains epistemically hollow.

To resolve this tension, mystery must be treated not as a storytelling wrapper, but as a systemic commitment. The mechanics of interaction must align with the cognitive acts they purport to represent, ensuring that discovery, reasoning and validation are performed by the player rather than narrated to them. This requires a design approach that privileges information architecture, semantic systems and interpretive play over traditional branching structures or outcome-based choice.

The following chapter therefore shifts from theoretical analysis to methodological application. It outlines a design research framework aimed at operationalizing epistemic agency through interactive systems of investigation, contradiction and interpretation. By translating these principles into a concrete design proposal, this project seeks to test how mystery can function not merely as narrative content, but as the structural foundation through which player agency is produced.
