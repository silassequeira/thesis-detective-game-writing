# 5. Early Design Rehearsals

The workshop did not rehearse every system proposed in Chapter 4. It concentrated on the two-slot evidence interaction, evidence-led interrogation, the constrained distribution of case information, and an early narrative use of the dream–awake loop. The broader Trust system was removed during scope reduction, while manual case construction was developed later in the digital prototype and is therefore addressed separately.

## 5.1 Protocol

The early design rehearsals consisted of low-fidelity, facilitator-led role-playing sessions designed to explore the proposed Two-Slot Epistemic Interaction System and the narrative experience it was intended to support. The sessions examined whether participants could interpret case evidence, construct provisional theories, and communicate investigative intentions by presenting one or two relevant evidence objects during questioning and confrontation. A further objective was to observe whether participants could use the dream–awake investigative loop as part of their construction and revision of a case theory.

The rehearsal did not aim to test a finished digital interface or to establish the final effectiveness of the proposed game systems. Instead, it functioned as an early narrative and interaction rehearsal. It allowed the evidence corpus, dialogue logic, dream sequence, player guidance, and final-accusation structure to be tested before their translation into a digital prototype. The workshop therefore focused on the player’s reasoning process: how participants understood evidence, what theories they formed, how they attempted to test those theories through dialogue, and how they responded when their assumptions were challenged.

Each session began with a briefing. Participants received a short description of the fictional case, the role of the detective, and the objective of preparing a sufficiently supported accusation for presentation to the Captain. They were introduced to the Two-Slot Epistemic Interaction System through a tutorial sheet and a verbal explanation. This explanation established that evidence should not be treated only as material to collect. Instead, participants were encouraged to select evidence that related to a question, suspicion, contradiction, or confrontation they wished to pursue.

Participants were initially given a limited set of starter reports and printed images. Additional reports were accessed gradually through the workshop’s simulated police evidence system. Rather than providing the full evidence corpus at the beginning of the session, documents were divided into different channels and made available in response to participant requests. This decision was intended to avoid overwhelming participants with information while encouraging them to make requests based on emerging hypotheses.

Participants were also informed that the investigation had to be completed within a limited afternoon shift. In practical terms, each rehearsal was restricted to a maximum duration of approximately six hours. This time constraint was communicated diegetically through Captain Miller, who established that Detective Stoker had only the afternoon to review the case, question the suspects, and prepare an accusation. The time limit was intended to prevent unlimited exploration and to require participants to balance further investigation against the need to prepare a sufficiently coherent final theory.

The police computer was represented through facilitated role-play. The facilitator performed the role of the evidence-locker assistant, João, who received participant requests for additional reports and provided available case material. Participants could also direct certain requests to the Captain, who functioned as an institutional gatekeeper for reports that could not be accessed directly through the evidence system. Where a requested document was available and relevant to the current investigation, it was supplied to the participant. Where it was unavailable, premature, or outside the intended scope of the rehearsal, the facilitator provided an in-world explanation and redirected the participant towards available evidence channels where appropriate.

After reviewing the initial material, participants could request to question either suspect. To initiate an interrogation, they indicated the suspect they wished to bring into the interrogation room. During questioning, participants were free to formulate their own questions, but were asked to ground each inquiry or confrontation in one or two pieces of evidence. The selected evidence was intended to communicate the investigative relation or theory being pursued — for example, questioning a suspect’s memory, establishing their access to the weapon, challenging an alibi, suggesting a motive, or exposing a contradiction between testimony and documentary evidence.

During interrogations, each role-played character was also governed by a patience threshold. Participants could pursue calm inquiries or more forceful confrontations, but repeatedly presenting unrelated evidence, attempting to pressure a character without a supported theory, or using coercive actions reduced the relevant character’s patience. The facilitator communicated escalating resistance through the character’s responses. If a participant exhausted an NPC’s patience, the interaction could be interrupted, redirected, terminated, or escalated into a forced final confrontation depending on the character and action involved. 

The purpose of this rule was not to punish participants for reaching an incorrect interpretation. Rather, it was intended to distinguish exploratory but evidence-based reasoning from repeated or coercive pressure unsupported by the available case material. Participants could still pursue alternative theories, but they were expected to communicate those theories through relevant evidence and proportionate investigative actions.

The facilitator role-played the suspects according to prepared character material describing what each character knew, believed, concealed, and was willing to disclose. NPC responses were not intended to confirm a single correct answer. Instead, the response depended on the relevance and coherence of the evidence-based reasoning participants presented. An inquiry could produce clarification, denial, evasion, emotional resistance, partial disclosure, or new information. A sustained and appropriately supported confrontation could lead to a narrative “slip,” in which a character unintentionally revealed additional material, challenged the detective, or altered the participant’s understanding of the case.

For example, participants who pursued the pharmacological and psychological implications of Somnoryl-μ could prompt Elara to describe her dream experience and invite the detective to take the substance. This encouraged the participant to initiate the dream sequence, which formed a parallel mode of inquiry within the rehearsal. Dream material was not presented as direct proof of the murder. Instead, participants were expected to interpret its symbolic traces in relation to the documentary evidence, suspect testimony, and theories developed during the waking investigation. The dream sequence could therefore complicate or reframe an existing theory, rather than resolving the case independently.

Certain evidence requests also involved delayed delivery. Reports requiring additional laboratory work, trace comparison, or extended institutional processing could not always be supplied immediately. Participants therefore had to decide whether to wait for a potentially important report, continue investigating other available material, or proceed to a final accusation with the evidence already obtained. Captain Miller periodically reminded participants of the remaining time, particularly as the session approached its conclusion. 

The sessions concluded when participants considered their investigation sufficiently developed or when the available time ended. Participants then presented their theory to the Captain, identifying the suspect or suspects they believed should be held responsible and explaining the evidence supporting that judgment. The resulting ending was based on the coherence, evidential support, and institutional sufficiency of the case presented, rather than on whether participants had discovered an inaccessible underlying truth. Participants could consequently reach different outcomes, including a conviction of Kae for murder, a finding of Elara’s responsibility for manslaughter alongside Kae’s obstruction or gun-related offences, or the detective’s dismissal for failing to present a sufficiently supported case.

Throughout each session, the facilitator recorded participant requests, evidence combinations, dialogue strategies, stated theories, responses to NPC interactions, and final accusations. These interaction logs were supplemented by observation notes. Following the session, participants completed an evaluation form addressing clarity of the Two-Slot system, perceived fairness, evidence use, dialogue, dream engagement, frustration, perceived agency, satisfaction with their conclusion, and suggested improvements.

Sessions lasted approximately four to six hours, depending on the number of participants, the degree of evidence exploration, the amount of time spent in interrogation, and whether the session reached a final confrontation. Audio and video were also recorded [only retain this sentence if participants gave appropriate consent] to document the tabletop evidence interactions and enable later review of observations not captured in the live written log.

## 5.2 Workshop Design & Elements

### 5.2.1 Narrative Reconstruction and Scope Reduction

The first narrative iteration of the project was situated within a larger mystery world centred on an addictive dream-inducing substance and the disappearance of a young woman. The player assumed the role of a detective investigating both the physical traces left in a small town and the symbolic traces encountered while dreaming. Investigation alternated between two states. While awake, the player explored locations, collected evidence, questioned residents, interpreted rumours, and attempted to uncover information that characters deliberately concealed. While dreaming, the player entered symbolic environments representing the fragmented memories and emotional states of both the detective and the missing woman.

This initial direction established many of the themes that continue throughout the project. It introduced unreliable memory, altered perception, institutional neglect, and the relationship between symbolic and factual forms of evidence. At the same time, it expanded the narrative beyond what could realistically be implemented and evaluated within a playable prototype. The mystery required multiple locations, a large cast of characters, interconnected disappearances, several dream environments, and a broader social structure organised around the shared use of the substance. Rather than supporting the research question, this scale risked dispersing attention across too many narrative systems at once.

The narrative was therefore reconstructed around a single investigative case. This was not a rejection of the original fictional world, but a reduction that preserved the elements most relevant to the research. The dream-inducing substance remained part of the narrative, although its role changed significantly. Instead of functioning primarily as world-building, it became an epistemic condition that destabilised testimony, memory, intention, and certainty. The dream sequences continued to exist as symbolic spaces where players interpreted traces that could not be accessed through conventional investigation, while the waking investigation shifted towards institutional documents and suspect interrogations rather than open exploration across the town.

Reducing the scope also changed the structure of evidence. The original concept relied heavily on environmental exploration, rumours, and conversations distributed across many characters. In the revised version, evidence was condensed into a smaller corpus composed of reports, testimonies, forensic documents, and interactions with two central suspects. This created a case that was manageable to prototype while still containing enough ambiguity to support competing interpretations of the same event.

The reconstruction followed five successive design decisions. The first was identifying which elements exceeded the practical limits of the prototype, including the number of locations, characters, and parallel investigations. The second was extracting the thematic core that connected the project to its research questions, particularly unreliable recollection, institutional failure, dream experience, and evidential uncertainty. The third was condensing these themes into a bounded mystery centred on one murder investigation. The fourth was redesigning evidence as interpretive material instead of a sequence of progression gates. The final decision was translating theory-building into interaction by making conversations depend on the player's interpretation of evidence rather than on predefined dialogue options.

The resulting narrative focused on a single murder involving a limited number of characters, a contained setting, and a carefully selected body of evidence. This smaller case was not intended to simplify the mystery into a linear investigation. Instead, it created a situation where different explanations could remain plausible for a significant portion of the investigation, allowing players to construct and revise their own theories as new information became available.

The reduction in scope was therefore both a production decision and a methodological one. By limiting the narrative while preserving ambiguity, the rehearsal created a controlled environment for exploring the relationship between evidence, interpretation, dialogue, and player agency. This allowed the prototype to focus on the central research question without the additional complexity of building an entire fictional world.

### 5.2.2 Evidence Reports as Indexical Clues

A central concern during the narrative reconstruction was redefining the role of evidence within the investigation. In many detective games, evidence functions as a confirmation mechanism. The player discovers a clue, the game assigns it a fixed meaning, and progress occurs when that clue is presented at the predetermined moment. Although this structure gives players responsibility for collecting information, the interpretation of that information remains largely authorial.

The early rehearsal approached evidence differently by treating reports as indexical clues rather than complete explanations. Each report pointed towards an event, condition, relationship, or absence without resolving its meaning independently. Instead of explaining the case, evidence established fragments that only became meaningful through their relation to other fragments.

The evidence corpus consisted primarily of forensic reports, toxicology results, witness statements, police memoranda, behavioural observations, and institutional documentation. Each document fulfilled several possible investigative functions. A report could establish chronology, indicate physical presence, suggest impairment, expose an inconsistency, reveal institutional assumptions, or imply a possible motive. Its significance did not emerge from the document alone, but from the network of relations created by the player.

A toxicology report, for example, could establish that a character was under the influence of the dream-inducing substance. That information alone did not determine whether the character acted intentionally, remembered events accurately, concealed information, or lacked responsibility for a particular action. Likewise, a witness statement could establish presence at a location without establishing causation, motive, or guilt. Even official police memoranda communicated more than procedural information, since they also revealed the institutional preference for a straightforward and prosecutable explanation of the crime.

This approach treated evidence as polysemic. A single report could support multiple interpretations without implying that every interpretation was equally valid. Interpretations remained constrained by the available evidence, but their validity depended on how players connected reports, identified inconsistencies, and justified their reasoning within the investigation.

The investigative activity therefore extended beyond collecting documents. Players were expected to decide what a report meant in relation to another report, what assumptions it supported or challenged, and how that interpretation could be used during an interrogation. The relevant question was no longer simply which evidence had been found, but what that evidence implied when considered alongside other traces within the case.

This distinction was important because it repositioned evidence as the material through which players performed investigative reasoning. Narratively, the reports reconstructed the fragmented history of the case and the perspectives of its characters. Mechanically, they became resources for constructing questions, identifying contradictions, and developing theories about what had happened. The rehearsal therefore shifted detective gameplay away from discovering a hidden authorial answer and towards constructing a defensible interpretation from incomplete information.

### 5.2.3 Rehearsing the Two-Slot Epistemic Interaction System

The bounded mystery also served as the first narrative rehearsal of the Two-Slot Epistemic Interaction System introduced in the previous chapter. Rather than evaluating interface usability or implementation details, the rehearsal explored whether participants could express investigative reasoning through evidence-based interactions before those interactions were translated into a digital prototype. The purpose was not to test whether participants could discover a single correct combination of clues. Instead, it was to examine whether evidence could function as material through which participants formulated questions, suspicions, contradictions, and provisional theories.

The Two-Slot Epistemic Interaction System should not be understood as requiring two pieces of evidence for every interaction. The two slots represent the system’s capacity to support either an individual evidential action or a relation between two evidential objects. A participant could present one report, image, physical evidence card, or previously obtained document when that item was relevant to the current character, situation, or investigative intention. The same participant could also place two items in relation when they wished to communicate a more specific or complex argument. The system therefore treated the second slot as optional but potentially significant: it enabled participants to externalise the relation they believed existed between two traces.

An individual evidence item could already possess epistemic value within a particular context. Presenting the firearm to Kae, for example, could support a question concerning his access to the weapon, his explanation for its concealment, or his relation to the homicide. Presenting the witness statement about the barefoot figure to Elara could prompt a question concerning her injuries, memory, whereabouts, or relationship to the crime scene. In these cases, the evidential object did not automatically determine the meaning of the interaction. Its significance depended on the participant’s stated intention, the character being questioned, the information already available, and the preceding conversational context.

The second slot enabled a different form of interaction. By presenting two pieces of evidence together, participants could make a relation between traces explicit. This relation could involve corroboration, contradiction, temporal sequence, causal implication, comparison, or tension between different accounts of the case. For example, Kae’s initial statement that Elara needed to work the following morning could be related to the employment report showing that she was not scheduled to work. The significance of this interaction did not lie in either report independently, but in the contradiction established between them. Similarly, Elara’s medical record concerning her injuries could be related to the trace report linking material found on her feet to the crime-scene environment. This relation could support a stronger question about her possible presence in the alley than either document could establish alone.

The interaction model therefore depended on four connected elements: the evidence selected by the participant, the possible relation between one or two evidence objects, the context in which the interaction occurred, and the investigative intention communicated by the participant. The player was not expected to identify a predetermined evidence pairing in order to unlock the next narrative event. Instead, they were expected to consider what an available item might mean in the current context and, where relevant, whether another piece of evidence could clarify, challenge, or strengthen that meaning.

This distinction was especially important in dialogue. NPC responses were not designed to depend solely on whether the participant had selected the correct item or pairing. A character could respond differently to the same evidence depending on whether it was used to request clarification, question a memory, challenge an alibi, suggest a motive, expose an inconsistency, or accuse the character of concealment. Presenting a single report could therefore initiate a meaningful line of inquiry, while presenting two related reports could communicate a more developed theory or confrontation. The purpose of the system was to make the participant’s interpretation visible enough for the character response to engage with it.

For example, relating the time-of-death report to a toxicology result did not automatically produce a single conclusion. The same evidential relation could be used to question Elara’s awareness, assess her capacity during the relevant period, challenge the reliability of her recollection, or consider whether impairment altered the interpretation of her actions. However, the toxicology report could also be presented independently if the participant wished to ask about Elara’s drug use, memory gaps, emotional state, or understanding of the substance. The two-slot structure therefore expanded the expressive range of the system without making two-item combinations a mandatory condition of investigative agency.

This shifted the role of NPC conversations within the investigation. Characters could deny accusations, reinterpret evidence, redirect attention towards another suspect, withhold information, or react emotionally to the participant’s emerging theory. These responses produced additional material for interpretation rather than immediate confirmation or failure. Dialogue became resistant because participants had to consider whether their evidence was relevant to the interaction, whether their proposed relation was sufficiently supported, and whether the character’s response challenged the theory they were attempting to develop.

The rehearsal therefore created an investigative cycle in which evidence generated questions and interpretations; those interpretations could be tested through individual evidence presentation or through relations between multiple traces; dialogue generated new testimony, resistance, or contradiction; and these outcomes became material for later investigation. Progress emerged through the successive revision of the participant’s theory rather than through the completion of a predetermined sequence of evidence combinations or dialogue options.

The purpose of this rehearsal was to evaluate a specific design proposition. The question was not whether participants reached a correct solution or discovered a required pair of clues. Instead, it was whether they could use one or two evidential objects to articulate meaningful investigative intentions, and whether NPC responses could sustain, complicate, or challenge that reasoning through conversation. The rehearsal therefore established the conceptual foundation for the later digital implementation of the Two-Slot Epistemic Interaction System: a system in which evidence retains individual epistemic value, while the optional relation between two pieces of evidence enables players to formulate more explicit and complex interpretations of the case.

### 5.2.4 Case Setting, Characters, and Knowledge Distribution

The early rehearsal was situated within Oakhaven Central – 4th District Station, located in the Gallows Creek neighbourhood of the fictional city of Oakhaven. The decision to locate the entire case within a police station was both a narrative and a practical design choice. It reduced the need for open-world exploration while preserving the institutional structure through which evidence is collected, classified, interpreted, and converted into an accusation. The police station therefore operated as a contained investigative environment: a space in which participants could access reports, request further information, question suspects, and eventually present their conclusions to an institutional authority.

The location was organised around four connected spaces. The Evidence Room contained the police-computer system through which participants could request reports from different departments and examine the available case material. The Interrogation Room provided the space in which participants questioned Elara Vance and Kaelen “Kae” Ward. The Observation Room was occupied by Captain Miller and Officer “Buck” Buckley, representing the institutional perspective of the police department and serving as a point of access for requests that could not be made through the evidence system. Finally, the Corridor linked these spaces and represented the movement between evidence gathering, institutional consultation, and suspect confrontation.

Participants assumed the role of Detective Julian Hammond Stoker, referred to as Detective Stoker. Stoker was characterised as a recently appointed homicide detective who had previously distinguished himself through the resolution of a murder case in the rural area where he grew up. His professional identity was designed around inquiry and confrontation: he sought inconsistencies in testimony, used evidence to place pressure on accounts of events, and attempted to obtain information that characters were unwilling to disclose directly. The case at Oakhaven Central represented an important test of his position within the department. If he failed to prepare a sufficiently credible accusation, he risked being removed from the homicide unit and returned to his previous post.

This framing gave the final accusation a narrative and institutional consequence. Participants were not simply asked to identify a culprit as the final answer to a puzzle. They were required to construct a case that Captain Miller considered sufficiently coherent, supported, and actionable. The workshop thus distinguished between a participant’s personal suspicion, the evidence available to them, and the institutional threshold required to hold one or both suspects responsible.

The central case concerned the death of Arthur Penhaligon and the arrest of two suspects: Elara Vance and Kaelen “Kae” Ward. Elara and Kae were designed as a couple whose relationship had been shaped by dependency on Somnoryl-μ, a fictional illicit substance with sedative, dissociative, and dream-inducing effects. The substance was important not only because it motivated economic precarity and dependency, but because it complicated interpretation of memory, intention, perception, and responsibility. Users could experience vivid dreams, fragmented recollection, altered awareness, and uncertainty about the boundary between waking experience and dream experience. These effects created the epistemic conditions necessary for a case in which testimony could be emotionally sincere while remaining factually incomplete or unreliable.

Kae was constructed as a character associated with weapon possession, prior robbery, financial pressure, and concealment. His history and possession of the firearm made him a plausible suspect, while his concern for Elara and attempts to manage police attention complicated the distinction between self-protection, protection of another person, and direct guilt. Elara was constructed as a character whose physical state, altered memory, and relationship to the crime-scene evidence made her equally central to the investigation. Her intoxication and apparent dissociation raised questions about whether physical presence could be equated with intention, whether memory loss constituted deception, and how far responsibility could be assigned to an individual acting under an altered state of consciousness.

Arthur Penhaligon was designed as an ordinary victim rather than as a character with a clear connection to an elaborate conspiracy. His death initially appeared to be associated with the impoverished and crime-affected environment of Gallows Creek, Kae’s history of robbery, and the couple’s economic dependence on Somnoryl-μ. However, the available evidence did not establish a simple motive or a single linear reconstruction. The victim’s remaining wallet, the weather conditions, the limitations of the witness testimony, the physical traces recovered from the suspects’ apartment, and the contradictory accounts provided by Kae and Elara allowed multiple theories to remain plausible throughout the investigation.

A complete designer-facing case model was prepared before the rehearsal. This model established the chronology of the homicide, the suspects’ relationship, the history of the firearm, the events surrounding the police visit and subsequent arrest, the information known by each character, and the narrative conditions under which different role-played responses could occur. It also established the relationship between Somnoryl-μ, Elara’s altered state, Kae’s concealment of information, and the wider dream-related mystery suggested through restricted documents and dream material.

The complete case model was necessary for maintaining consistency during facilitated interrogation. Since the facilitator role-played the police-computer assistant, Captain Miller, Elara, and Kae, it provided a shared basis for determining what each character could plausibly know, conceal, deny, misunderstand, or reveal during a given interaction. It also enabled the facilitator to respond to participant theories without simply confirming or rejecting them as correct.

Participants did not receive access to this complete narrative model. Their investigation was restricted to the participant-accessible case: starter reports, requested documents, photographs and physical evidence representations, institutional memoranda, dialogue responses, dream sequences, and the consequences of their own evidence combinations. This distinction between the underlying case model and the information available to participants was deliberate. The rehearsal was not designed to reward access to a hidden definitive explanation. Instead, it examined whether participants could construct and defend a coherent interpretation from incomplete, ambiguous, and sometimes contradictory material.

The dream sequence formed an additional layer within this distribution of knowledge. Participants could enter a symbolic dream space by using the auto-injector with a Somnoryl-μ vial. The sequence did not provide a direct factual reconstruction of the murder or reveal an unambiguous solution to the case. Instead, it placed Detective Julian Stoker in an empty black space, where he encountered a distant glowing entity that addressed his desire to know the truth.

The entity’s dialogue framed truth not as information that becomes immediately clear once it is seen, but as something that may remain difficult to perceive, comprehend, or accept. It described human perception as limited and unstable, suggesting that people seek other realities because existence itself is paradoxical and difficult to confront directly. The dream sequence therefore functioned as a symbolic encounter with epistemic desire: Julian seeks the truth, yet is confronted with the possibility that seeing something does not necessarily mean understanding it, and that understanding it does not necessarily mean accepting it.

These symbolic statements did not function as institutional proof and could not independently justify an accusation. However, they could encourage participants to reconsider the certainty of their existing assumptions, the reliability of testimony, the meaning of altered consciousness, and the difference between direct observation and interpretation. The dream–awake loop therefore expanded the investigation without collapsing uncertainty. It introduced a subjective and symbolic mode of knowing that could be considered alongside documentary evidence and suspect testimony, while preserving the distinction between suggestive experience and evidence that could be defended within the institutional framework of the case.

This setting and character structure created a bounded but interpretively open case. The station restricted spatial scope, the two suspects created a focused field of responsibility, and the evidence corpus supplied multiple routes through which participants could formulate theories. At the same time, the distinction between designer knowledge, participant-accessible information, and participant interpretation ensured that the workshop could test the project’s central proposition: whether detective-game agency can emerge through the construction, testing, and revision of evidence-based theories rather than through the discovery of a single predetermined answer.

### 5.2.5 Evidence Corpus and Institutional Request Channels

The evidence corpus was designed as the primary material through which participants could construct, test, and revise theories about the homicide. Rather than receiving all available information at the beginning of the session, participants began with a limited initial case folder and gained access to further reports through a system of institutional request channels. This structure was intended to transform evidence acquisition into an investigative activity. Participants were required to decide not only what a report might mean, but also what kind of information they needed next and which institutional department could plausibly provide it.

The initial case folder established the basic conditions of the investigation. It included arrest reports for Elara Vance and Kaelen Ward, the homicide incident report, an official police memorandum, and the witness statement of Meredith Gable. Participants also received a set of non-report evidence items, including representations of the firearm, fired projectile, wet hooded sweatshirt, Somnoryl-μ vials, prescription vial, victim’s wallet, footwear impressions, crime-scene photograph, surveillance image, suspect portraits, and the previous detective’s note. These initial materials supplied the primary factual and institutional framing of the case while deliberately leaving its central causal relations unresolved.

The starter evidence was designed to support immediate but competing readings. The firearm and Kaelen’s history could support a theory focused on weapon control, prior violence, robbery, or concealment. Elara’s wet hoodie, bare feet, physical condition, and altered state could support a theory centred on her presence at the scene, intoxication, impaired agency, or unreliable memory. The witness account, footwear material, surveillance gap, and crime-scene image introduced uncertainty concerning timing, visibility, the number of people present, and the reliability of environmental traces. Participants could therefore form an early suspicion, but the initial material was not intended to justify a final accusation without further investigation.

The request channels were not intended simply to hide content behind a progression gate. Their purpose was to encourage participants to formulate an information need. Instead of asking generically for “more clues,” participants had to decide whether a particular question required a forensic, medical, pharmacological, or institutional perspective. 

This structure also made the police institution visible within the investigation. Reports were not presented as neutral fragments of truth. Their form, accessibility, and wording reflected the department that produced them. A medical report could frame Elara through impairment and pathology; an evidence-and-custody record could foreground Kae’s prior criminality and weapon ownership; a police memorandum could favour an immediately prosecutable explanation; and a forensic report could give material traces an appearance of objective certainty while still requiring contextual interpretation. The evidence system thus reinforced the project’s interest in the difference between factual traces, institutional framing, and participant interpretation.

Several documents could be requested through more than one relevant route or could become meaningful only after another report had been obtained. This allowed participants to develop an investigation incrementally. For instance, pharmacological reports concerning Somnoryl-μ could lead to further material about the substance’s psychological effects, while a victim background profile could open access to a previous robbery complaint. The evidence corpus was therefore organised as a network of related possibilities rather than a single linear sequence. Participants could pursue a weapon-focused route, a medical and toxicological route, a witness and crime-scene route, a prior-criminality route, or a dream-related pharmacological route, depending on the theory they were developing.

The interaction records show that participants engaged with these request channels in varied ways. Some systematically combined departmental tapes with evidence objects to obtain further reports and build a structured case. Others used exploratory combinations, repeated unsuccessful requests, or attempted to request information outside the designed scope, such as external financial records, phone calls, or additional witness contact. These outcomes were valuable to the rehearsal because they revealed both successful understanding of the evidence system and expectations that the future digital prototype would need to address through clearer affordances, feedback, and interaction boundaries.

The complete evidence corpus included reports concerning ballistics, clothing, toxicology, medical intake, footwear impressions, surveillance, prior criminal conduct, employment history, victim background, and restricted clinical information about Somnoryl-μ. A full inventory of evidence identifiers, request combinations, and report descriptions is provided in Appendix X. The main function of the corpus was not to provide participants with an exhaustive factual reconstruction of the case. Instead, it was to provide sufficiently rich but incomplete material from which different evidence-based theories could be constructed and brought into dialogue with the suspects.

### 5.2.6 Physical Materials and Workshop Interface

The low-fidelity rehearsal translated the proposed digital investigation system into a physical tabletop environment. Rather than reproducing the final interface visually, the workshop materials were designed to make the intended evidence economy, information flow, and Two-Slot interaction model tangible and observable. This enabled participants to handle, compare, organise, and present evidence while allowing the facilitator to document how theories developed across the session.

The central material consisted of printed evidence reports, photographs, object representations, evidence cards, institutional-channel tapes, and a cardboard interface representing the Two-Slot Epistemic Interaction System. Evidence reports were printed as individual police, medical, forensic, or institutional documents. Physical evidence that would be represented as three-dimensional objects or inspectable images in the digital prototype, such as the revolver, projectile, hooded sweatshirt, drug vials, wallet, footprints, surveillance material, and portraits, was represented through printed images or cards. Each item was identified through an evidence code and descriptive name, enabling participants to refer to the same object consistently during evidence requests and suspect questioning.

The two evidence slots were represented through a cardboard base containing two marked spaces. Participants placed evidence cards in these spaces to communicate the relation they wished to investigate. The material interface was deliberately simple: its purpose was not to test a final user-interface design, but to externalise the act of combining epistemic objects. A participant could place an institutional-channel tape in one slot and a related evidence card in the other to request a report. During interrogation, participants could place one or two evidence cards in the slots to support a question, challenge a statement, or formulate a confrontation.

This physical arrangement made the player’s proposed logic visible. Instead of selecting a pre-written dialogue line, the participant assembled an evidential relation before speaking. The cards therefore acted as a temporary representation of an investigative hypothesis. For example, the combination of the Controlled Substances Unit tape and the Somnoryl-μ vial communicated a request for pharmacological information, while the combination of the revolver and a ballistics report could support a question concerning weapon control, opportunity, or the relation between the firearm and the homicide. The facilitator could then interpret the stated intention of the participant alongside the selected materials and respond through the relevant role-played character.

In addition to the designed evidence interface, participants were provided with blank paper, notebooks, sticky notes, clips, and pens. These items were included because the case contained a substantial quantity of information and because theory construction was expected to occur through comparison, annotation, and revision. The materials were not accompanied by a prescribed case-board layout or an enforced method of organisation. Participants were free to construct their own external representations of the case.

This decision produced varied forms of evidence organisation. Some participants arranged documents into piles according to suspect, evidential category, or perceived connection. Others wrote evidence identifiers on sticky notes, constructed chronological sequences, drew diagrams, or kept notes on questions they wished to pursue. In one session, participants used several rows of tables to organise their reports and drew a diagram connecting the ballistic trajectory to the relative height of the suspects. In another, a duo divided their activity between reading evidence aloud and recording notes, before jointly preparing their final case.

These practices were important because they made theory building visible without requiring a dedicated digital mind-map system. The workshop did not provide participants with pre-authored connections between clues. Instead, participants created provisional relations materially through the placement, grouping, annotation, and comparison of evidence. Their use of the physical space therefore formed part of the observational data: it showed which reports participants considered related, which items became central to their theories, and when they revised or abandoned an investigative direction.

The physical materials also revealed limitations relevant to later digital development. Some participants found the quantity of documents difficult to manage, requested additional table space, or needed to write evidence identifiers before using them during dialogue. Others forgot the availability of particular characters or interaction opportunities, suggesting that the digital prototype would require clearer visual cues, better evidence retrieval, and more explicit feedback about available actions. Questionnaire feedback similarly indicated that participants wanted clearer information about interactable characters, a more visible distinction between Inquiry and Confrontation, and smoother ways to explore possible evidence requests.

The workshop interface should therefore be understood as both a representational tool and an experimental instrument. It represented the intended digital mechanics at a reduced level of fidelity, while also making participant behaviour observable before implementation. The evidence cards, two-slot board, institutional tapes, and organisational materials allowed the rehearsal to examine whether participants could use material evidence to form theories and express investigative intentions. The findings from these interactions informed the later design of the digital evidence interface, dialogue Logic Chains, and feedback systems.

### 5.2.7 Dream–Awake Rehearsal Materials

The workshop retained a reduced version of the Dream–Awake Epistemic Loop proposed in the original design. Although the wider initial concept included multiple explorable dream environments, the early rehearsal condensed this system into a single symbolic dream sequence that participants could enter during the investigation. The purpose of this sequence was not to interrupt the detective case with a separate narrative event. Instead, it was designed as a parallel investigative space through which participants could encounter material that complicated their interpretation of the waking evidence.

Access to the dream sequence was represented through two physical items: the detective’s auto-injector and a Somnoryl-μ vial. Participants could choose to combine these items and use the substance during the investigation. This action was not required for the completion of the case, but it created an optional shift in the mode of investigation. The decision to enter the dream was therefore itself meaningful. Participants could treat it as a risky attempt to access information unavailable through police reports and interrogation, as an act of curiosity concerning the substance, or as a possible response to uncertainty in their existing theory.

The dream sequence was facilitated as a symbolic narrative encounter rather than as a factual reconstruction of the homicide. Participants encountered dream imagery, dialogue, and symbolic traces associated with altered consciousness, memory fragmentation, dependency, fear, responsibility, and the relationship between Elara’s condition and the wider mystery surrounding Somnoryl-μ. The materials presented in this state were intentionally ambiguous. They did not identify a murderer, provide direct proof, or replace the evidential work required in the waking investigation.

Instead, dream material was designed to function as a form of symbolic evidence. Participants were expected to consider whether an image, statement, entity, or emotional encounter in the dream changed the meaning of the documentary evidence available to them. For example, dream-related material could suggest that Elara’s behaviour should be interpreted through impaired consciousness, parasomnia, dependency, manipulation, or fragmented memory. However, participants still had to relate these suggestions to waking evidence such as toxicology results, medical reports, witness statements, physical traces, and suspect testimony before they could use them to construct an accusation.

The dream sequence was connected to the waking evidence corpus through reports concerning Somnoryl-μ, Luminaquel, toxicology, and restricted clinical material. Participants who pursued the pharmacological route could obtain further information about the drug’s effects on sleep, perception, memory, and the distinction between dream and waking states. This made the dream sequence part of a broader evidential chain rather than an isolated supernatural event. The system was designed so that an investigation into the substance could lead participants towards dream material, while dream material could motivate renewed attention to reports that had previously seemed peripheral or ambiguous.

This loop was intended to create a distinction between symbolic suggestion and institutional proof. The dream could expand what participants considered possible, but it could not independently satisfy the Captain’s requirement for a sufficiently supported case. A dream experience could therefore prompt a participant to reconsider Elara’s memory, Kae’s concealment, the meaning of the drug evidence, or the ethical implications of an accusation. However, that interpretation still had to be developed through the available reports and dialogue interactions.

The dream materials also provided a means of testing whether participants would voluntarily pursue a non-institutional form of knowledge. The police reports, laboratory channels, and Captain represented official procedures for establishing what counted as credible evidence. The dream sequence offered a contrasting mode of inquiry grounded in subjective experience, emotion, symbolism, and uncertain memory. By placing both modes within the same investigation, the rehearsal explored whether participants could navigate the tension between what seemed psychologically meaningful and what could be defended within an institutional accusation.

Participant engagement with the dream material varied. Some entered the dream sequence primarily out of curiosity or in response to Elara’s challenge, while others pursued the pharmacological evidence chain before taking the substance. Some participants described the dream sequence as engaging but struggled to connect its meaning to the homicide. Others used it to develop more complex theories concerning Elara’s altered state, sleepwalking, and the relation between the dream material and the note left by the previous detective. Catarina and Chico, for example, were the only group to connect a dream entity to the previous detective’s note, while Joana and Mafalda combined evidence about the drug, Elara’s condition, and their own interpretation of sleepwalking to construct a theory of impaired agency.

These differences were valuable to the rehearsal. They suggested that the dream sequence could generate curiosity, emotional engagement, and alternative interpretive paths, but also showed that the transition between symbolic material and waking evidence required clearer support. The dream–awake loop was therefore retained as an important component of the later digital prototype, while the workshop findings informed the need for more explicit links between dream traces, evidence reports, dialogue Logic Chains, and the player’s evolving theory of the case.

### 5.2.8 Time Constraint and Deferred Evidence

Time was incorporated into the rehearsal as an investigative resource rather than as an external limit imposed only for practical workshop management. At the beginning of each session, Captain Miller informed participants that Detective Stoker had the duration of the afternoon to investigate the case, question the suspects, and submit a sufficiently supported accusation. In practical terms, each rehearsal was limited to a maximum of approximately six hours. This established a diegetic deadline while also ensuring that participants could not indefinitely continue requesting evidence or delaying their final judgment.

This mechanic operationalised the Window of Opportunity (Kairos) system.. Rather than treating time as a uniform countdown applied equally to every action, the rehearsal used it to create moments where a specific decision, such as waiting for a delayed report, choosing to interrogate now rather than later, deciding when a theory was sufficiently supported, carried a cost that could not be recovered once the moment had passed. The temporal design therefore aimed to produce kairotic pressure where a series of situated windows in which a particular investigative opportunity was available only for a limited duration before circumstances shifted. 

The time constraint was designed to address a central tension of detective gameplay. A case with ambiguous evidence benefits from allowing players to explore competing theories, but unrestricted access to information can remove the need to prioritise, commit, or accept uncertainty. The rehearsal therefore required participants to make decisions about the value of further investigation. They had to decide which reports were worth pursuing, whether a particular line of inquiry justified the time required, when to stop gathering information, and when their existing theory was sufficiently coherent to present to the Captain.

Time also affected the availability of selected reports. Some documents could be retrieved immediately through the relevant institutional channel, while others represented analysis or processing that would take time within the fictional police procedure. This included reports such as the trace analysis associated with Elara’s injuries and the crime-pattern material concerning the wider series of robberies. When participants requested such reports, they could choose to wait for the result, continue investigating other available evidence, question a suspect, or proceed with their existing theory. The delayed reports were therefore designed to create an opportunity cost rather than simply function as inaccessible content. Each delayed report therefore constituted a window of opportunity in the Kairos sense, a report requested early enough could return in time to inform an interrogation or the final accusation, while the same report requested too late, or never pursued at all, effectively closed off that avenue of interpretation for the remainder of the session. 

This structure created different investigative approaches. Some participants prioritised the immediate construction of an accusation from the available evidence. Others invested time in additional forensic, pharmacological, or contextual reports in an attempt to reduce uncertainty and strengthen a developing theory. In several sessions, participants waited for delayed evidence because they believed that it could clarify a key contradiction or reinforce a particular interpretation. In other cases, participants spent substantial time pursuing uncertain requests, red herrings, or dialogue paths that did not provide sufficient information to support a final accusation.

The consequences of time use were made explicit at the end of the investigation. Participants who assembled a coherent and sufficiently supported case before the deadline could present their theory to Captain Miller and receive an outcome corresponding to their interpretation of responsibility. Participants who reached the deadline without a sufficiently developed case could be dismissed from the homicide unit. The “fired” outcome was therefore not intended as a conventional failure state based on identifying an incorrect culprit. Instead, it represented an institutional consequence for failing to convert the available material into an actionable argument within the time available.

The time limit also created pressure to commit to an interpretation even when uncertainty remained. Participants were not required to obtain every report or resolve every contradiction before making their accusation. Rather, they had to decide which doubts were acceptable, which evidence was sufficiently reliable, and whether further investigation was likely to change their conclusion. This supported the project’s broader distinction between complete factual truth and institutionally sufficient proof. A participant could produce a defensible accusation without accessing all available material, while a participant who continued searching indefinitely could lose the opportunity to present any case at all.

Captain Miller reinforced this temporal pressure through periodic in-world reminders. As the session approached its conclusion, the facilitator notified participants that the available time was diminishing and encouraged them to prepare for the final confrontation. In the later stages of each rehearsal, a more intense noir-jazz soundtrack was introduced to mark the approaching deadline and strengthen the affective sense of investigative urgency. This audiovisual cue did not alter the available evidence or impose an automatic punishment. Its purpose was to communicate the changing dramatic state of the investigation and to encourage participants to transition from open exploration towards evidential commitment.

The session records demonstrate that time meaningfully shaped participant behaviour. Some groups reached the final confrontation with an organised and evidence-backed theory, while others ran out of time with unresolved contradictions or insufficiently developed accusations. The records also show participants waiting for delayed reports, such as trace analysis and crime-pattern material, when they considered those documents necessary to strengthen a case. Post-session responses further indicate that time pressure was experienced as part of the investigation rather than as a purely external interruption, although participants varied in whether they considered the available time sufficient.

The time mechanic therefore served three related purposes within the rehearsal. First, it required participants to prioritise investigative actions under uncertainty. Second, it made the acquisition of further information consequential by attaching a temporal cost to selected reports. Third, it required participants to convert an evolving theory into a final institutional judgment. These functions informed the later digital design by demonstrating that time pressure could support epistemic agency when it encouraged prioritisation and commitment rather than merely accelerating the player through a predetermined path.

### 5.2.9 Patience, Escalation, and Forced Confrontation

In addition to the overall time constraint, the rehearsal incorporated a Patience System to model the social limits of investigation. This system was designed to prevent interrogation from becoming an unlimited source of information and to position dialogue as a form of epistemic resistance. Suspects and institutional characters were not intended to respond indefinitely to repeated accusations, unsupported pressure, or coercive actions. Instead, their willingness to continue participating in the interaction depended on how participants used the evidence available to them.

Each role-played character was assigned a notional patience threshold. Participants could approach a character through calm inquiry, using evidence to request clarification or test a developing theory, or through confrontation, using evidence to challenge an inconsistency, accusation, or concealment. Neither inquiry nor confrontation was inherently preferable. Their effectiveness depended on whether the participant could establish a relevant relation between the selected evidence, the character being questioned, and the investigative intention being pursued.

Patience was reduced when participants repeatedly pressured an NPC with evidence that did not support the question being asked, continued to present the same irrelevant material after receiving no meaningful response, or attempted to force an outcome without constructing an evidential argument. This rule was not intended to punish participants for pursuing an alternative theory or reaching an incorrect conclusion. Instead, it differentiated between a theory that could be argued from the available evidence and a pattern of interaction in which the participant attempted to obtain information through repetition, arbitrary accusation, or coercion.

The system was communicated through role-played responses rather than through an explicit numerical meter. As patience decreased, a character could become defensive, frustrated, evasive, emotionally distressed, or unwilling to continue answering. This approach preserved the narrative framing of the interaction while allowing participants to perceive that their method of questioning carried social consequences. The relevant feedback was therefore not simply that an evidence combination was invalid, but that the character interpreted the participant’s conduct as unconvincing, hostile, repetitive, or unfair.

Captain Miller functioned as an institutional boundary within this system. If a participant repeatedly pressed a suspect without a sufficiently supported line of reasoning, the Captain could intervene and stop the interrogation. The participant was then required to return to the available evidence, reconsider the case, and prepare a more coherent approach before continuing. This intervention occurred during one rehearsal session, where a participant repeatedly questioned a suspect using evidence that did not meaningfully support the intended inquiry. The intervention redirected the participant from repeated pressure towards renewed evidence review, demonstrating the intended function of the system: to make unsupported confrontation less productive than theory-led investigation.

The system also accounted for more severe forms of escalation. The revolver was designed as a physical evidence object that could be presented within dialogue, but its use as an instrument of intimidation carried a different consequence from its use as documentary or forensic evidence. If a participant attempted to pressure Elara by pointing the gun at her, the interaction immediately escalated to a forced final confrontation. Rather than allowing the participant to continue interrogating indefinitely through coercion, the system required them to present their case to Captain Miller with the evidence they had accumulated at that point.

This interaction occurred in one session. After repeated frustration with the interrogation system and the perceived lack of relevant responses, a participant improvised the loading of the fired projectile into the revolver and pointed it at Elara. The workshop transitioned immediately to the final confrontation. Because the participant had not constructed a sufficiently coherent and supported case, the resulting outcome was the dismissal of Detective Stoker from the homicide unit. This outcome was important because it demonstrated that coercive escalation did not function as a shortcut to information or as a mechanically advantageous strategy. It instead transformed uncertainty into institutional accountability before the participant had prepared an adequate theory.

Kae’s character architecture included a different possible escalation path. If participants constructed and sustained a sufficiently coherent confrontation centred on Elara’s responsibility, Kae’s protective relationship with her could cause him to provide a false confession. This confession was designed as a narrative “slip”: it did not establish factual truth independently, but represented Kae’s attempt to redirect institutional responsibility away from Elara. The confession could provide participants with a powerful route towards one possible ending, while also introducing a new interpretive problem concerning motive, credibility, protection, and self-sacrifice.

Although this specific false-confession path was supported by the workshop design, it was not successfully triggered during the recorded rehearsal sessions. It should therefore be described as a designed affordance rather than as an observed finding. Its inclusion remains relevant to the workshop design because it demonstrates how the Patience System was intended to generate different forms of escalation: institutional interruption, coercive forced closure, emotional resistance, and strategically misleading disclosure. The digital prototype could later implement these responses through explicit dialogue Logic Chains, argument-strength conditions, and narrative flags.

The Patience System therefore had three related functions. First, it made dialogue a limited social resource rather than an unlimited mechanism for extracting exposition. Second, it encouraged participants to prepare and communicate evidence-based theories before escalating to confrontation. Third, it ensured that aggressive or coercive conduct carried narrative and institutional consequences rather than functioning as an optimal strategy. The system supported the project’s broader argument that detective gameplay should reward the construction and testing of interpretations, while also requiring players to take responsibility for the social and ethical consequences of how those interpretations are expressed.

## 5.3 Sessions & Participants (tabela demográfica, duração dos workshops, etc)

The early design rehearsal was conducted through thirteen low-fidelity role-playing sessions involving nineteen participants. Participants were recruited through an informal convenience-sampling process. The researcher contacted friends through direct messages or arranged sessions through informal conversation, adapting the workshop schedule to participant availability. No prior experience with detective games, role-playing games, or ambiguous narrative media was required. Participants therefore entered the rehearsal with varied levels of familiarity with investigative gameplay and detective fiction.

Participants were aged between 20 and 26 years. The sessions were conducted in a range of everyday settings, including university classrooms, participant homes, and one bar. This variation resulted from the informal recruitment process and practical availability rather than from an intentional comparison between locations. Sessions were conducted individually, in pairs, and once in a group of three participants. No compensation or financial incentive was provided. Participants who were audio- or video-recorded gave consent for the recording of the session. Participant identities were anonymised in the research records and are referred to in this dissertation only through session labels.

The rehearsal sessions were designed around the diegetic premise that Detective Stoker had one afternoon to investigate the case and present a sufficiently supported accusation to Captain Miller. In practice, each session was planned to take up to approximately six hours, although actual durations varied according to the number of participants, the location, the degree of evidence exploration, the use of dream material, the length of interrogations, and whether the session reached a final confrontation. Because exact starting and finishing times were not consistently recorded, duration is reported only as an approximate workshop constraint rather than as a precise comparative measure.

Eleven of the thirteen sessions reached a final accusation or institutional outcome. The completed sessions generated four broad outcome categories: three sessions ended with Detective Stoker being dismissed for presenting an insufficient case; three found Elara responsible for manslaughter and Kae responsible for obstruction; three resulted in Kae’s conviction for murder and Elara’s release; and two found Elara responsible for manslaughter while identifying Kae’s responsibility for gun possession. The workshop generated three forms of data. First, the facilitator recorded evidence requests, evidence combinations, interrogation strategies, participant theories, dream-sequence engagement, and final outcomes in detailed interaction logs. Second, observation notes were produced during and after each session and later coded through the thematic-analysis process. Third, participants were invited to complete an anonymous post-session questionnaire. Ten individual questionnaire responses were collected. The informal and exploratory nature of the rehearsal created limitations. Group size, session location, duration, and participant familiarity with detective media were not controlled. Not every participant completed the questionnaire, and because the forms were anonymous, responses cannot always be mapped confidently to a specific session or participant. These limitations mean that the findings should not be interpreted as statistically generalisable. Instead, they provide qualitative design evidence about how participants encountered, understood, negotiated, and sometimes challenged the proposed investigative systems.

### 5.4.1 Analytical Approach and Coding Process

The workshop material was analysed to understand not only which final outcomes participants reached, but also how they approached the investigation, interpreted evidence, interacted with the suspects, and responded to the constraints of the low-fidelity prototype. The analysis focused on the processes of investigation rather than treating conviction outcomes as an isolated measure of success. A participant who reached a favourable institutional outcome could still have misunderstood a mechanic or relied on a narrow evidential path, while a participant who was unable to complete the case could nevertheless demonstrate meaningful theory-building, engagement with the dream sequence, or productive criticism of the system.

The analysis drew upon three complementary sources of qualitative material. The first consisted of detailed interaction logs produced during each workshop session. These records documented evidence requests, departmental tape combinations, report acquisitions, delayed-report requests and waiting decisions, dialogue actions, uses of the Two-Slot interface, dream-sequence entry, stated theories, responses to time reminders, final accusations, and recorded session outcomes The second source consisted of facilitator observation notes, including participant comments, moments of uncertainty, material organisation practices, emerging theories, emotional reactions, and instances in which prototype limitations influenced the session. The third source consisted of ten anonymous post-session questionnaire responses. These responses provided participant reflections on system clarity, evidence use, perceived agency, dialogue, frustration, narrative immersion, satisfaction with final outcomes, and suggested improvements.

The analysis used a hybrid deductive–inductive thematic approach. The deductive component was informed by the research questions and the systems under rehearsal. Since the workshop was constructed to test evidence interpretation, evidence combination, interrogation, dream engagement, temporal pressure, and final judgment, the initial coding framework included categories such as System Understanding, System Confusion, Theory Building, Dialogue Strategy, Dream Engagement, Narrative Inference, Frustration, Temporal Pressure, Deferred Evidence Decision, Forced Commitment, and Ending Outcome. These codes enabled the analysis to identify whether the intended mechanics were understood and whether they supported the form of epistemic agency proposed by the project.

The inductive component developed through repeated engagement with the workshop material. During the review of interaction logs, observation notes, recordings, and questionnaire responses, it became clear that some relevant forms of participant behaviour were not adequately captured by the initial system-oriented categories. Additional codes were therefore used to describe material organisation, requests outside the intended scope of the prototype, attempts to circumvent the interaction rules, environmental exploration, meta-level comments about the workshop design, and differences between solo and collaborative reasoning. Codes including Physical Organization, Out-of-Scope Request, Exploit Attempt, Role Division, Cooperative Synergy, and Cooperative Conflict were particularly important because they revealed how participants adapted the prototype, interpreted its boundaries, and constructed theories together.

The unit of analysis was an individual observation, action, statement, or interaction sequence rather than an entire participant or session. A single observation could receive more than one code where it addressed several relevant aspects of the rehearsal. For example, a participant who repeatedly confronted a suspect with unrelated evidence could be coded as demonstrating System Confusion and Dialogue Strategy, while the resulting negative reaction or abandoned investigative path could also be coded as Frustration or Dead End. Conversely, a participant who compared several reports, linked their contents to a suspect’s testimony, and then altered their accusation could be coded simultaneously as Theory Building, Narrative Inference, and Aha Moment.

This approach was necessary because the workshop did not produce cleanly separated behaviours. Participants often learned through trial, combined successful and unsuccessful strategies, or shifted between immersion in the fictional investigation and meta-level reflection on the prototype’s rules. For this reason, the coding process did not treat behaviours as mutually exclusive. It instead sought to preserve the relationship between evidence use, player intention, system response, emotional reaction, and subsequent action.

The coding process was iterative rather than linear. Initial codes were applied while reviewing the session material and were then compared across sessions to identify repeated patterns, differences, and contradictions. The codebook was refined as the analysis progressed when the initial categories did not sufficiently capture a relevant dimension of participant behaviour. This was particularly important for the temporal structure of the rehearsal. Although final accusations and outcomes were recorded from the beginning, later review showed that these outcomes did not adequately describe whether participants had acted voluntarily, had waited for or bypassed delayed evidence, had changed their strategy because of diminishing time, or had been compelled to present a theory before they considered it complete. The addition of Temporal Pressure, Deferred Evidence Decision, and Forced Commitment therefore enabled a more precise analysis of the Kairos-inspired Window of Opportunity system.

The resulting coded observations were grouped according to their relevance to the project’s central concern: whether detective gameplay could support player-led interpretation rather than merely require recognition of a designer-defined answer. Patterns were reviewed in relation to the workshop’s major systems, including the evidence corpus, institutional request channels, evidence-based interaction model, patience and confrontation system, dream–awake loop, time constraint, delayed evidence, and final accusation.

The analysis did not use code frequency as a measure of statistical significance. A behaviour occurring in only one session could remain analytically relevant if it exposed a major limitation or possibility within the design. For example, one participant’s use of the revolver to intimidate Elara triggered the forced-confrontation rule and exposed how the Patience System treated coercion as a narrative and institutional risk rather than as a shortcut to information. Similarly, the collaboration of Kate and Chico (S07) in connecting dream material to the previous detective’s note occurred in only one session, but it provided important evidence that the dream sequence could support cross-domain interpretation when participants established a meaningful relation between symbolic and documentary material.

Several limitations also shaped the analysis. The workshops were conducted in informal settings and differed in participant format, duration, and level of facilitator intervention. The facilitator simultaneously operated the evidence system, Captain Miller, the suspects, and the dream-sequence material, meaning that some responses were necessarily shaped by facilitation judgment. Prototype errors and unclear interaction boundaries also influenced specific participant theories and behaviours. In addition, questionnaire responses were anonymous and were not completed by every participant, preventing a reliable one-to-one connection between every form response and a specific workshop session. These limitations do not invalidate the workshop material, but they require the findings to be interpreted as situated design evidence rather than generalisable claims about detective-game players.

The thematic analysis developed six recurring patterns that connected participant behaviour to the project’s research questions. The first concerns the ways in which participants used evidence as material for constructing theories rather than merely collecting information. The second examines system legibility, friction, and the boundaries participants encountered when attempting to request reports or interrogate suspects. The third analyses dialogue as epistemic resistance, including the role of patience, confrontation, and institutional intervention. The fourth considers the relationship between dream engagement, symbolic interpretation, and narrative immersion. The fifth examines how solo and group formats produced different forms of collaborative reasoning and disagreement. Finally, the sixth considers how participants responded to uncertainty when required to transform an incomplete theory into an institutional accusation. Together, these themes provide the basis for the following analysis of how the rehearsal supported, complicated, and refined the proposed detective-game systems.

### 5.4.2 Theme 1 — Evidence as Theory-Building Material

A central pattern across the workshop sessions was that participants did not treat the evidence corpus only as a collection of documents to read or items to unlock. Instead, many participants actively transformed reports, images, and physical objects into material for constructing provisional theories about the case. Evidence became meaningful when participants compared it with other reports, arranged it spatially, connected it to suspect testimony, or used it to justify an inquiry or confrontation. This behaviour supports the central premise of the project: that detective-game evidence can function as material for interpretive work rather than as a sequence of fixed progression keys.

The physical workshop format made this process particularly visible. Participants were given access to reports, evidence cards, photographs, stationery, sticky notes, clips, and table space, but they were not provided with a pre-authored case board or a prescribed method for organising the case. Several participants nevertheless created their own systems for externalising the investigation. They placed related reports into groups, wrote evidence identifiers on notes, arranged materials according to suspect or evidential category, and created informal spatial diagrams to compare possible explanations. These practices indicated that participants were attempting to reduce the complexity of the evidence corpus by making relationships between traces visible.

The session involving Helen and Andrew (S09) offered a particularly clear example. They spread evidence across three rows of tables, created piles based on perceived relationships, and attached identifying notes to help them track individual documents. They also adopted the Motive, Means, and Opportunity framework while constructing their theory. Rather than accepting the reports as isolated fragments, they attempted to connect the ballistic trajectory, suspect characteristics, witness material, drug evidence, and police reports into a structured account of what may have occurred. Their use of a diagram to relate the downward bullet trajectory to the comparative height of the suspects demonstrated an attempt to convert forensic detail into a causal inference about the possible shooter.

Similar practices occurred in other sessions. Jane and Mafs (S11) began by closely examining the initial reports and establishing a timeline before committing to a suspect theory. Their attention to time, sequence, intoxication, and the conditions surrounding Elara’s return to the apartment supported a later interpretation of impaired agency and possible sleepwalking. Rodri (S03) used the available materials to construct a chalkboard-style organisation of the suspects and evidence. Julie and Steph (S13) divided their activity between reading files and recording notes before developing a theory and preparing their final case. These examples show that theory building was not limited to a single preferred play style. Participants used different material strategies, such as chronology, spatial arrangement, note-taking, suspect comparison, and verbal discussion, to make the case manageable.

The evidence-request system also encouraged participants to formulate information needs rather than passively receive an exhaustive case file. Participants who used the institutional tapes purposefully often began with a question or emerging theory. A participant who suspected that Elara’s physical condition was central to the case could request medical material relating to her injuries and toxicological state. A participant pursuing Kae’s possible involvement could access his initial police interview, weapon record, prior criminal history, or later crime-pattern material. In these cases, the request itself formed part of the theory-building process, where selecting a departmental channel and an evidence object expressed what the participant believed required further investigation.

This process was visible in the more systematic sessions. Jane and Mafs (S11) initially attempted direct dialogue with limited evidence, but after receiving insufficient results they returned to the evidence corpus and requested additional reports. They then pursued connections between Elara’s medical condition, toxicology, drug use, and the restricted information concerning Somnoryl-μ. Their final accusation held Elara responsible for manslaughter while identifying Kae’s role in obstruction of evidence. The outcome was not produced by a single decisive document. It resulted from a progressive interpretation of Elara’s physical presence, impaired state, memory gaps, and Kae’s concealment of relevant information.

The workshop also showed that theory construction involved revision rather than only accumulation. Several participants began with an early suspicion of Kae because of the gun, his criminal record, his prior robbery behaviour, and his apparent lies to the police. As they acquired further material, some redirected attention towards Elara’s bare feet, injuries, wet hoodie, drug use, and altered memory. Other participants retained their suspicion of Kae even after accessing evidence that could support Elara’s involvement. For example, Gui (S08) obtained extensive material relevant to both suspects, including evidence associated with Elara’s physical presence and Kae’s confession route, but ultimately considered Kae’s account more credible and convicted him for murder.

This variation is important. It indicates that the same broad evidence corpus did not mechanically lead all participants to the same conclusion. Participants assigned different relevance to physical traces, prior criminality, toxicology, witness testimony, emotional responses, and suspect behaviour. Their final theories reflected not only what information they accessed, but how they prioritised and connected that information. The findings therefore support the project’s attempt to design evidence as a set of constrained but interpretively open traces.

Questionnaire responses reinforced this pattern. Participants frequently described their most satisfying moments as occasions when several pieces of evidence became connected, when an apparently minor report led to another document, or when they used a chain of material to expose a suspect’s lie. One participant described the satisfaction of showing evidence that contradicted Kae’s claim about Elara’s work schedule. Another described the progressive discovery that “simple and minor details” could lead to further information and reveal wider connections within the case. These responses suggest that participants valued the process of assembling evidence into a theory, rather than only the final accusation. Appendix X

However, the evidence corpus did not automatically produce coherent theory construction for every participant. Some participants focused heavily on a single clue, such as footwear impressions or the firearm, without relating it sufficiently to the wider case. Others pursued reports or external information that was unavailable within the scope of the prototype, such as contacting Arthur’s wife, calling an insurance company, tracing the weapon’s serial number, or obtaining financial records. These interactions should not be interpreted simply as player error. They revealed that the evidence materials successfully encouraged participants to think beyond the available reports, but they also exposed a mismatch between the investigative possibilities participants expected and the limited scope of the rehearsal.

The absence of a dedicated mind-map interface did not prevent participants from externalising their reasoning. Instead, the tabletop format enabled spontaneous forms of case construction through physical organisation, note-taking, diagrams, and collaborative discussion. This does not demonstrate that a visual case board would have no value in a future game. Rather, it suggests that the more important design requirement is not the presence of a particular interface, but whether the evidence system encourages players to establish, test, and revise relationships between traces. In the rehearsal, this activity emerged through the combination of material reports, institutional request channels, Two-Slot selection, and interrogation.

The main design implication for the digital prototype is that it should preserve the visibility of these relationships without reducing them to an automated deduction system. Players need to be able to revisit reports, compare evidence, recognise previously discovered relations, and understand why a particular combination may support a question or confrontation. At the same time, the system should avoid automatically resolving the meaning of those relations. The evidence interface should support the player’s theory-building process while leaving responsibility for interpretation and final institutional judgment with the player.

### 5.4.3 Theme 2 — System Legibility, Friction, and Investigative Boundaries

Unlike Themes 1, 3, 4, and 6, which examine whether the underlying design propositions succeeded, this theme examines whether the low-fidelity materials communicated those propositions clearly enough to be fairly tested.

The workshop demonstrated that participants were generally able to understand the central premise of the Two-Slot Epistemic Interaction System: evidence could be combined to request further information, formulate an inquiry, or support a confrontation. However, understanding the overall concept did not always produce confidence in its detailed operation. Participants frequently encountered uncertainty concerning which objects could be combined, which characters or systems remained available for interaction, what forms of questioning were permitted, and why a particular attempt produced no meaningful response. This created an important distinction between the intended complexity of the investigation and friction caused by unclear system boundaries.

Several participants understood the evidence-request system quickly. They recognised that departmental tapes represented different institutional perspectives and that combining a tape with an evidence object could produce a report relevant to that object. Participants successfully requested toxicology, medical, forensic, custody, and background information through this system. In the interaction logs, these successful combinations often became the basis for more developed theories, particularly when participants pursued the medical and pharmacological material surrounding Elara’s condition or the criminal and custody records associated with Kae.

Nevertheless, participants did not always understand why certain combinations were invalid or what made one approach more relevant than another. In some sessions, participants repeatedly attempted combinations that resulted in “None,” “Nope,” or equivalent negative feedback. While exploratory failure was intended to be part of the investigative process, the workshop revealed that feedback needed to distinguish more clearly between different types of failure. An invalid departmental request, a report that had already been obtained, a combination that lacked a meaningful relation, and a report that was unavailable at that stage of the investigation were not always communicated differently enough. As a result, some participants interpreted a failed combination as arbitrary rather than as information about the logic of the system.

This ambiguity was especially visible when participants moved from requesting reports to questioning suspects. The workshop allowed participants to formulate their own questions, but required those questions to be grounded in relevant evidence. Because the limits of this rule were not always fully defined, especially in the early sessions, some participants attempted to ask broad questions that were only loosely connected to the evidence placed in the slots. Others repeatedly presented a single document, such as a police report or the firearm, without developing a stronger evidential relation to support the pressure they were applying. In these situations, participants could experience the lack of an NPC response as a failure of the prototype rather than as feedback about the weakness of the theory being presented, while others exploited this design vulnerability to gather information without presenting the related evidence to their question.

The session involving John and Fred (S12) demonstrated this tension particularly clearly. Both participants quickly understood the evidence-request channels and actively pursued pharmacological, forensic, and contextual material. However, they adopted incompatible expectations for interrogation. One participant attempted to establish context gradually, while the other pursued immediate confrontations, improvised intimidation strategies, and asked whether bluffing or other role-playing techniques could be used. Because the dialogue boundaries had not been sufficiently formalised, the participants attempted actions that were plausible within tabletop role-playing conventions but outside the intended scope of the prototype. Their frustration was therefore not only a result of incorrect evidence use; it emerged from a mismatch between the open-ended expectations encouraged by role-play and the more bounded logic of the evidence-based interrogation system.

Requests outside the intended scope of the rehearsal were a recurring pattern. Participants asked whether they could contact Arthur Penhaligon’s wife, call telephone numbers found in the victim’s wallet, trace the firearm’s serial number, obtain financial records, contact the witness again, inspect external police files, or access additional forensic material. These requests were often reasonable from the perspective of a detective investigation. They demonstrated that participants accepted the fictional world and treated the case as a space in which further investigative actions should be possible. At the same time, they revealed the limits of the low-fidelity prototype: the workshop could not simulate every plausible police procedure, and participants did not always know which actions had been designed as available affordances.

These out-of-scope requests should not be interpreted simply as failures to follow instructions. They are evidence that the rehearsal generated a meaningful investigative mindset. Participants were not only attempting to identify the designer’s intended combination; they were proposing additional investigative routes based on their developing theories. However, the digital prototype will need to make its boundaries clearer without reducing this sense of possibility. A player should understand which characters, evidence systems, and investigative actions are currently available, while still being encouraged to form questions and pursue non-obvious connections.

Material and interface friction also affected participants’ understanding of the system. In the physical rehearsal, some participants needed to write evidence identifiers on sticky notes before they could reference an item during interrogation. Others forgot that Captain Miller remained available as an interactable institutional character, partly because his presence was less visually represented than the suspects. Questionnaire responses similarly requested clearer visual indicators for available characters, a more explicit reminder of the distinction between Inquiry and Confrontation, and more accessible ways to identify when evidence-request options had been exhausted.

These observations are important because they identify problems with the representation of the system, rather than problems with the underlying design proposition. The workshop provided evidence that participants could understand evidence combination and derive satisfaction from using evidence to obtain further information. However, the material prototype did not always communicate its possible actions, interaction states, and consequences clearly enough. In a digital implementation, the player should not need to memorise evidence codes, remember hidden available characters, or infer whether a failed request reflects an invalid relation, unavailable information, or a report that has already been acquired.

Prototype errors further complicated the distinction between intended ambiguity and unintended confusion. In one early session, a report incorrectly implied that Elara worked at the same location as Arthur, encouraging a false motive theory that was not part of the designed case. In another session, medical information concerning Kae’s injection marks appeared inconsistent with evidence of active drug use, producing confusion about his physical state. A further session received an incorrect image file where a different report was expected, while in another relevant confrontation Kae did not produce the prepared confession response. These incidents must be acknowledged because they influenced participant theories and outcomes. They should be treated as rehearsal findings about the need for content consistency and state-management reliability, not as evidence that participants were unable to reason with ambiguity.

The theme therefore distinguishes between productive ambiguity and unproductive opacity. Productive ambiguity occurred when reports supported several plausible theories and participants had to decide how to relate them. Unproductive opacity occurred when participants could not identify available actions, understand why a request failed, distinguish between inquiry and confrontation, or determine whether a contradiction originated in the fiction or in the prototype materials. The former supported epistemic agency; the latter risked turning reasoning into trial-and-error.

The main implication for the digital prototype is that it should make the system’s affordances and feedback more explicit while preserving interpretive openness. The Two-Slot interface should visibly distinguish between available evidence, institutional request channels, inquiry, confrontation, and final accusation. Failed combinations should provide contextual feedback, such as indicating that the selected objects do not establish a relevant relation, that further information is unavailable through the selected department, or that the report has already been obtained. Such feedback should clarify the operational rules without telling players what a clue ultimately means or which theory they should pursue.

Similarly, the digital dialogue system should clearly communicate that an inquiry requires evidence-based relevance and that confrontation represents a higher-stakes escalation. The player should be able to recognise which NPCs are currently available, what evidence they have already used, and whether their current line of questioning is approaching a social limit. These improvements would preserve the workshop’s central finding: players were willing to investigate actively and think beyond the immediately available content, but they required a legible system that could distinguish meaningful investigative uncertainty from avoidable interface confusion.

### 5.4.4 Theme 3 — Dialogue as Epistemic Resistance

The workshop was designed to treat dialogue not as a neutral delivery mechanism for information, but as a site of epistemic resistance. Participants could not progress simply by asking every available question or presenting evidence indiscriminately. Instead, they were expected to use reports, physical evidence, and prior testimony to formulate an investigative intention: clarifying an inconsistency, questioning a suspect’s memory, challenging an alibi, establishing access to the weapon, or testing a theory of responsibility. The quality and relevance of this evidential relation shaped how the role-played NPCs responded.

This approach was intended to distinguish the rehearsal from conventional dialogue structures in which players select pre-authored lines such as “accuse,” “press,” or “ask about evidence.” Participants were free to articulate their own questions, but they were required to ground these questions in one or two relevant evidence objects. The evidence combination therefore operated as an attempt to communicate what the participant believed the relationship between two traces implied. A participant presenting Kae’s initial police interview alongside Elara’s employment verification, for example, could argue that Kae had lied about Elara needing to work the following morning. The relevant interaction was not simply the possession of either report, but the accusation that emerged from their relation.

Several workshop sessions indicated that participants found dialogue satisfying when the role-played response appeared to follow from reasoning they had constructed. In the questionnaire responses, participants described positive moments when they used evidence to identify a contradiction, observe a suspect becoming nervous, or pressure Kae over an inconsistency in his account. One participant described confronting Kae with evidence that contradicted his statement about Elara’s work schedule and experiencing the exchange as rewarding because it resulted from their own speculation and evidence gathering. Another described the successful presentation of an evidence chain as satisfying because it made the suspect’s denial appear weaker.

The interaction logs provide similar examples. Jane and Mafs (S11) initially approached dialogue with individual reports and broad questions, but recognised that this did not produce sufficient information. They then returned to the evidence corpus, acquired further medical and pharmacological reports, and used the resulting material to construct more focused lines of questioning. Their later use of evidence relating to Elara’s physical traces and Somnoryl-μ prompted Kae to react protectively towards Elara and provided further information relevant to their final theory. Their final outcome, where they held Elara responsible for manslaughter while identifying Kae’s obstruction of evidence, was therefore developed through repeated cycles of evidence review, dialogue, and revision rather than through one decisive confrontation.

Dialogue also enabled participants to test whether an evidential theory could survive contact with a character’s self-protective account. Kae could deny involvement, redirect attention towards Elara, conceal his weapon history, or offer partial information that complicated the player’s understanding of his motivations. Elara could respond through uncertainty, disorientation, denial, altered recollection, emotional detachment, or disclosure related to her drug use and dream experience. These reactions were designed to ensure that evidence did not immediately resolve the case. Instead, a response could become new material for interpretation, requiring participants to assess whether a denial was credible, whether a contradiction indicated guilt, and whether an emotionally distressed response should be interpreted as manipulation, fear, impairment, or incomplete memory.

The Patience System was central to this form of resistance. Each character had an implicit threshold governing their willingness to continue responding to questions. Participants could use calm inquiry to gather information or escalate to confrontation when they believed they had sufficient evidence. However, repeatedly pressing a suspect with unrelated evidence, attempting to force a response without an argument, or relying on intimidation reduced the character’s patience. The system was not designed to penalise incorrect theories; participants could construct theories that ultimately differed from the facilitator’s case model. Instead, it was designed to penalise unsupported pressure and repeated interaction that did not demonstrate a meaningful relation between evidence, question, and character.

Captain Miller functioned as an institutional mediator within this system. When participants repeatedly pressured a suspect without sufficient evidential grounding, the Captain could intervene and suspend the interrogation, requiring them to reconsider the available material before continuing. This occurred in one session, Gui (S08), where repeated confrontation of Elara, who was already devastated by his decision to present to her Kae’s confession tape, did not produce a coherent investigative direction. The intervention redirected the participant from unproductive confrontation towards renewed evidence review. This was significant because it demonstrated the intended operation of the system: dialogue resistance did not merely block progress, but redirected the participant towards theory construction.

The session involving John and Fred (S12) revealed the limits of this system in its low-fidelity form. Both participants displayed strong interest in the investigation and requested substantial amounts of forensic, pharmacological, and contextual material. However, they adopted incompatible dialogue approaches. John tended to prepare questions and establish contextual information, while Fred pursued aggressive confrontation, suggested interrogation techniques outside the intended system, and eventually used the revolver as a tool of intimidation. The use of the firearm triggered the forced-confrontation condition. Rather than functioning as an effective shortcut to information, this action brought the investigation immediately to a final institutional judgment before the participants had constructed a sufficiently coherent case. The result was Detective Stoker’s dismissal.

This outcome demonstrated an important design principle: coercion was not treated as an optimal investigative strategy. The workshop did not reward the participant for increasing pressure through the physical threat of the gun. Instead, it transformed that action into an institutional consequence, requiring the participant to take responsibility for the case they had assembled. This connected the social mechanics of interrogation to the broader concern of epistemic agency. Players were given freedom to construct and express theories, but they were also required to consider the consequences of how those theories were communicated.

At the same time, the rehearsal showed that dialogue resistance could become frustrating when the system’s rules were not sufficiently legible. Some participants interpreted an unproductive response as evidence that a suspect was withholding information, while others interpreted it as a failure of the prototype or an indication that they had selected the wrong evidence. Participants were particularly frustrated when they believed they had presented incriminating material but received no new information in return. This distinction is important: resistance becomes meaningful only when the participant can understand why the character resists. If the relation between the evidence combination, the question, and the response remains opaque, dialogue may feel arbitrary rather than strategically difficult.

The workshop therefore suggests that dialogue can function as an epistemic obstacle when three conditions are present. First, participants must be able to form and communicate a recognisable evidence-based intention. Second, NPC responses must register that intention by denying, redirecting, revealing, or emotionally reacting to the theory being pursued. Third, participants must receive sufficiently clear feedback to distinguish a productive obstacle from an unintelligible system failure. When these conditions were met, dialogue became a process through which participants tested and revised theories. When they were absent, participants either repeated evidence, moved towards out-of-scope actions, or disengaged from the intended investigative logic.

The intended false-confession path for Kae illustrates a further possibility of this approach. The character architecture allowed Kae to make a false confession if participants constructed a sufficiently strong theory that positioned Elara as responsible for the murder. This confession was not intended to resolve the case as factual proof. Instead, it was designed as a new epistemic problem: Kae’s attempt to protect Elara could be interpreted as guilt, loyalty, manipulation, self-sacrifice, or an attempt to control the institutional narrative. Although this specific path was not successfully triggered during the recorded sessions, it remains relevant as a designed affordance later implemented through the Logic Chain system. It should therefore be understood as part of the workshop design rather than as an observed result.

The main implication for the digital prototype is that dialogue should continue to respond to relationships between evidence rather than to isolated item presentation. However, the digital Logic Chain system must make the connection between player intention, NPC resistance, and resulting information more consistent and legible. Inquiry should reward careful theory development through partial clarification or new contradictions. Confrontation should carry greater social risk and require stronger evidence support. Patience should operate as a visible but diegetically meaningful constraint, ensuring that players cannot substitute repetition, arbitrary accusation, or coercion for investigative reasoning.

### 5.4.5 Theme 4 — Dream–Awake Interpretation and Narrative Immersion

The dream–awake loop was designed to extend the investigation beyond institutional documents, physical traces, and suspect testimony. While the waking investigation provided police reports, forensic material, toxicology results, and interrogation responses, the dream sequence introduced a parallel mode of inquiry based on symbolic association, altered memory, emotional experience, and uncertainty concerning consciousness. The workshop explored whether participants could treat dream material as meaningful investigative input without mistaking it for direct factual proof.

Participants accessed the dream sequence by combining the auto-injector with the Somnoryl-μ vial. This action was optional. Participants could complete the investigation without taking the drug, but doing so enabled access to a symbolic encounter connected to Elara’s altered state, the history of Somnoryl-μ, and the wider dream-related mystery. The voluntary nature of this action was important. It allowed the workshop to observe whether participants considered the dream sequence a meaningful investigative opportunity, an act of curiosity, an emotional risk, or an unnecessary diversion from the institutional case.

Dream engagement varied substantially across sessions. Some participants entered the dream sequence because they had pursued the pharmacological material surrounding Somnoryl-μ and wanted to understand the drug’s effects more deeply. Others entered after Elara’s dialogue response challenged Detective Stoker to experience the substance directly. In these cases, the dream was not treated merely as an atmospheric reward. It became part of an ongoing theory about Elara’s memory, her physical condition, her relationship to the crime, and the possible effects of intoxication or dissociation.

Jane and Mafs (S11) demonstrated one of the strongest integrations of dream and waking interpretation. Their investigation combined Elara’s medical condition, the toxicology evidence, the Somnoryl-μ reports, and the restricted substance material. After encountering the information connected to the drug, they accepted Elara’s challenge and entered the dream sequence out of curiosity rather than frustration. Their interpretation of Elara’s impaired agency was also informed by the relationship between her physical traces, altered recollection, drug exposure, and the possibility of sleepwalking. Jane’s personal familiarity with sleepwalking contributed to their discussion, allowing the pair to construct a somnambulism theory even before accessing the document that made this possibility more explicit.

Kate and Chico (S07) provided another important example. They entered the dream sequence and were the only group to connect the dream entity to the note left by the previous detective. This connection led them to request further information concerning the note and the earlier investigation. Their final case did not rest solely on the dream material; they still used the waking evidence to argue that Elara had acted while impaired. However, the dream sequence expanded their interpretation of the case beyond a simple question of physical guilt. It encouraged them to consider the relationship between Elara’s altered state, the detective’s own position, and the wider hidden structure implied by Somnoryl-μ.

For other participants, the dream sequence created engagement without immediately producing a usable interpretation. Steph (S04) entered the dream after becoming frustrated with an unproductive line of questioning. She described the dream narrative as compelling, but struggled to determine what it meant for the murder case or how it could be connected to the available reports. This outcome does not indicate that dream engagement failed. It shows that symbolic material can attract attention and generate narrative immersion while still requiring stronger bridges back to the evidential logic of the waking investigation.

Questionnaire responses similarly suggest that the dream sequence was often memorable. One participant described taking the drug and connecting Elara’s altered state to the murder as the most satisfying moment of the investigation. Another described the discovery that the case was more complex than a conventional murder investigation as particularly engaging, specifically identifying the decision to take the drug as the moment when this wider complexity became visible. These responses indicate that the dream sequence contributed to the sense that the narrative world extended beyond the immediately available police reports and suspect testimonies.

However, the workshop also revealed that dream material did not automatically become part of every participant’s theory. Helen and Andrew (S09) did not pursue the pharmacological chain deeply enough to develop a dream-related interpretation. Gui (S08) acquired extensive evidence relevant to both suspects but chose not to enter the dream sequence, ultimately focusing instead on Kae’s weapon access, criminal history, and confession-related material. Other participants entered the dream but remained uncertain about whether its contents should be understood as evidence, metaphor, memory, hallucination, or information about the wider hidden narrative.

This variation was consistent with the intended role of the Dream–Awake Epistemic Loop. The dream sequence was not designed to provide an obligatory hidden answer or a superior route to the correct conclusion. If entering the dream guaranteed a better outcome, it would reduce symbolic investigation to another progression gate. Instead, the dream was intended to offer an alternative interpretive lens. It could make participants reconsider the reliability of memory, the meaning of Elara’s behaviour, the ethical implications of attributing guilt, or the limits of institutional evidence. Its value depended on how participants related symbolic traces to the waking evidence corpus.

The workshop therefore revealed a productive tension between narrative immersion and investigative legibility. Dream material created curiosity, emotional intensity, and a sense that the fictional world contained meanings beyond the immediate crime. Participants often responded to this material as an invitation to think more deeply about the substance, the suspects, and the previous detective’s case. At the same time, the symbolic nature of the sequence meant that participants sometimes lacked a clear method for translating dream content into a subsequent waking action. Without such a bridge, the dream could remain narratively compelling but mechanically disconnected.

The design implication is not that dream material should become more explicit or should reveal factual answers directly. Doing so would undermine the intended distinction between symbolic suggestion and institutional proof. Instead, the digital prototype should provide clearer opportunities for players to bring dream-derived concerns back into the waking investigation. A dream image or dialogue fragment could activate a new line of inquiry, make a previously ignored report appear more relevant, alter the interpretive context of a toxicology result, or unlock a specific but non-conclusive question for Elara or Kae.

For example, after encountering symbolic material associated with dissociation or loss of bodily control, the player could be encouraged to reconsider Elara’s toxicology report, medical intake, and the trace evidence found on her feet. The system should not state that these materials prove sleepwalking or remove responsibility from Elara. Instead, it should allow the player to formulate a new question concerning memory, awareness, intention, or capacity. In this way, dream material would remain interpretively open while becoming more visibly connected to the evidence-based dialogue system.

The findings suggest that the dream–awake loop can support narrative immersion and epistemic agency when it operates as a reciprocal process. Waking evidence motivates the pursuit of dream knowledge; dream material complicates the interpretation of waking evidence; and the participant returns to reports and dialogue with a revised theory. The low-fidelity rehearsal showed that some participants were able to perform this movement successfully, while others required clearer affordances to understand how symbolic experience could become an actionable investigative question.

### 5.4.6 Theme 5 — Collaborative and Divergent Investigative Practices

Although the workshop was designed primarily to rehearse the evidence and dialogue systems rather than to compare individual and group play, the variation between solo, duo, and trio sessions produced a significant pattern. Participants did not investigate in a uniform manner. Some approached the case through careful individual reading and gradual theory revision, while others distributed the investigative work across multiple people. Collaborative sessions could generate productive exchanges in which one participant’s observation enabled another participant’s inference. However, they could also create disagreement, reduced individual autonomy, and competing interrogation strategies.

The workshop included six solo sessions, six duo sessions, and one trio session. These formats emerged from participant availability rather than from an experimental comparison. They should therefore not be interpreted as controlled conditions. Nevertheless, the interaction logs show that group format influenced how evidence was read, organised, discussed, and presented.

In several duo sessions, participants spontaneously divided the practical and cognitive labour of investigation. Rather than both performing the same task, one participant often read a report aloud while the other took notes, organised materials, searched for a relevant clue, or questioned an emerging interpretation. This was particularly visible in the session involving Julie and Steph (S13). They worked across from one another, with one participant reading evidence files while the other recorded notes and maintained a developing overview of the case. Their division of activity supported the construction of a coherent theory and contributed to their final conclusion that held Elara responsible for manslaughter and Kae responsible for gun possession.

Helen and Andrew (S09) similarly transformed the available physical materials into a collaborative investigation space. They spread reports across several tables, created groups of documents based on perceived connections, labelled material with identifying notes, and used diagrams to examine the ballistic trajectory in relation to the suspects. Their work was not simply parallel reading. The process involved recurring discussion of Motive, Means, and Opportunity, shared attention to the timeline, and the collective construction of possible relationships between the gun, bullet angle, physical evidence, and suspect characteristics.

The strongest examples of collaboration involved what was coded as Cooperative Synergy: moments where one participant contributed an observation that another developed into a more substantial inference. Jane and Mafs (S11) provide a clear case. Their investigation combined close reading of reports, attention to temporal sequence, and discussion of Elara’s condition. Jane drew on personal knowledge of sleepwalking, while Mafalda’s engagement with detective-fiction conventions helped develop the possibility that Elara had acted in a somnambulant or dissociative state. Together, they arrived at this theory without first accessing the restricted document that explicitly developed the relationship between Somnoryl-μ, altered consciousness, and dream-related impairment.

Kate and Chico (S07) also produced an insight that neither participant appeared to develop independently. After entering the dream sequence, they connected the dream entity to the note left by the previous detective. This connection led them to investigate the previous case more closely and helped them interpret the dream material as part of the wider narrative structure rather than as an isolated surreal event. Their collaboration did not merely make the investigation faster. It enabled a cross-domain relation between dream symbolism, documentary evidence, and the detective’s history that did not occur in the other recorded sessions.

Collaborative play did not always improve the investigation. The session involving John and Fred (S12) demonstrated how different investigative expectations could become a source of conflict. John tended to gather contextual information and prepare questions before escalating. Fred more frequently pursued immediate confrontations, suggested improvised interrogation strategies, and attempted to create pressure through the revolver. Their approaches did not converge into a shared theory. Instead, the different methods repeatedly competed for control of the interrogation and contributed to frustration, unclear dialogue use, and a final forced confrontation before a coherent case had been prepared.

Questionnaire feedback also revealed that collaboration could limit individual exploration. One participant in a group session stated that they did not see the value of having two people play simultaneously because they felt unable to pursue their own ideas without first explaining them to the other participant and obtaining agreement. This response is important because it complicates the assumption that more participants necessarily produce better theory building. Shared investigation can encourage discussion and distribute cognitive labour, but it can also require negotiation before action, suppress tentative hypotheses, or cause one participant’s preferred strategy to dominate the group.

Solo sessions produced a different form of investigative practice. Individual participants had greater control over evidence requests, interrogation pacing, theory revision, and final judgment. In some cases, this supported sustained and methodical engagement. Gui (S08), for example, accessed a large portion of the evidence corpus, organised material through notes, requested further reports, and pursued an extended interrogation path before ultimately convicting Kae. His final theory remained distinct from the interpretations reached by other participants, even though he had access to evidence that could also support Elara’s involvement.

Other solo participants showed that individual control did not eliminate uncertainty or friction. Lena (S10) initially appeared cautious about using the system and had limited familiarity with detective-game conventions, but gradually became more independent in requesting evidence and communicating intentions through dialogue. Her final case against Kae emerged from this gradual learning process, although she did not fully pursue the evidence related to Elara’s physical presence. Ricky (S02) also investigated independently and used evidence consistently during questioning, yet moved between Kae-focused and Elara-focused theories as new reports complicated his existing interpretation.

These examples indicate that divergent investigative practices were not simply determined by player number. They were shaped by prior familiarity with detective media, confidence in using the evidence system, willingness to take risks, personal knowledge brought into the session, available time, and the particular reports each participant chose to prioritise. Group format amplified these differences by making them visible through discussion, labour division, agreement, and conflict.

The design implication for the digital prototype is that the core investigation must remain fully playable and intelligible for one person. The formal game system should not require a second participant to remember evidence, identify contradictions, organise material, or challenge an emerging theory. One player must be able to access reports, review dialogue history, organise information, retain questions, compare evidence, construct a theory, and submit a final accusation without external assistance. This is necessary because the intended prototype remains structurally a single-player detective experience: one player controls the detective, selects evidence, initiates dialogue, and takes responsibility for the final institutional judgment.

However, the workshop also suggests that single-player control does not prevent collaborative interpretation. In several duo sessions, participants spontaneously shared the cognitive labour of investigation. They read reports aloud, took notes, organised evidence, proposed competing explanations, and developed inferences through discussion. In some cases, this produced insights that did not appear to emerge from either participant independently. The collaborative connection between dream material and the previous detective’s note, for example, demonstrates how discussion can support cross-domain interpretation between symbolic experience, documentary evidence, and the wider narrative structure. The digital prototype should therefore not be understood as exclusively solitary in use, even if it contains no formal multiplayer system.

Rather than implementing a separate cooperative mode at this stage, the design should remain compatible with co-located shared investigation. The interface should make evidence, report contents, dialogue history, discovered relations, pending questions, and unresolved contradictions sufficiently visible for a second person to follow and discuss. A player who shares the experience with another person should be able to pause, revisit material, compare reports, explain a proposed connection, and negotiate a possible theory without the system prematurely resolving that interpretation for them. These features would support collaborative cognitive effort as an emergent social practice around a single-player system, rather than making collaboration a mechanical requirement or a separately authored game mode.

The workshop also demonstrates that collaborative play requires caution. Discussion can support theory construction, but it can also delay action, restrict one participant’s willingness to pursue uncertain ideas, produce competing investigative priorities, or result in one person dominating the interaction. The digital prototype should therefore preserve the formal player’s ability to act independently while allowing evidence and reasoning to remain externally legible. The objective is not to design a system in which two players must agree before progress can occur, but to create an investigation that can be independently played and meaningfully discussed.

This distinction should be examined in later digital playtesting. The low-fidelity rehearsal indicates that collaborative interpretation may be valuable for an epistemic mystery game, but it does not establish whether the digital interface will support this practice effectively. Subsequent tests can compare individual sessions with co-located shared sessions in which one participant controls the game while both participants are encouraged to read, discuss, organise, and interpret the available traces. This would make it possible to examine whether the digital case-construction tools support independent reasoning while also enabling productive shared reflection without making the system dependent on collaboration.

### 5.4.7 Theme 6 — Kairos, Uncertainty and Institutional Judgment

The final recurring theme concerned the relationship between uncertainty, temporal constraint, and institutional judgment. The rehearsal did not ask participants simply to identify the factual truth of the homicide. Instead, it required them to decide whether the evidence they had gathered was sufficient to accuse one or both suspects within the institutional setting of Oakhaven Central. Participants were not given complete access to the underlying case model, and they were not expected to resolve every contradiction before acting. They were required to transform a developing interpretation into an accusation that could be evaluated by Captain Miller.

This requirement was central to the workshop design. The final confrontation was intended to represent an epistemic commitment rather than a conventional end-of-level answer. Participants had to decide which evidence they considered reliable, which ambiguities they could accept, whether further investigation was likely to alter their conclusion, and whether their existing theory could be defended as institutionally sufficient. The distinction between complete factual truth and a sufficiently supported accusation was therefore important. A participant could construct a plausible and defensible case without obtaining every available report, while another participant could continue gathering material until the available investigative opportunity had passed without ever presenting a coherent accusation.

The workshop outcomes demonstrate that the same broad evidence corpus did not lead all participants to the same institutional judgment. Three completed sessions resulted in Kae being convicted of murder while Elara was released. Three sessions concluded that Elara was responsible for manslaughter and that Kae had obstructed evidence. Two sessions resulted in Elara being held responsible for manslaughter while Kae was charged with gun possession. A further three completed sessions ended with Detective Stoker being dismissed because the case presented to Captain Miller was considered insufficiently supported. These outcomes should not be read as a simple distinction between correct and incorrect solutions. Rather, they demonstrate that participants assigned different relevance to physical traces, witness testimony, drug evidence, prior criminality, suspect behaviour, emotional responses, and contradictions between accounts.

The variation in outcomes indicates that participants were not merely following a single hidden route through the evidence. Some gave priority to the firearm, Kae’s criminal history, his apparent dishonesty, and his connection to the robbery pattern. Others gave greater significance to Elara’s bare feet, the injuries documented during arrest, the wet hoodie, her toxicology results, her fragmented memory, and the trace evidence linking her injuries to the crime-scene environment. Some participants attempted to formulate shared responsibility, interpreting Elara’s altered state as relevant to culpability while treating Kae’s concealment of the weapon, his false statements, or his protective behaviour as obstruction. The final accusation therefore required participants to decide not only what had happened, but what could be argued from the evidence available to them.

However, these judgments did not occur under conditions of unlimited time. The rehearsal was structured around the premise that Detective Stoker had one afternoon to investigate the homicide, question the suspects, request further information, and present a sufficiently supported accusation. In practice, sessions were planned to run for up to approximately six hours. This temporal limit had a practical function in making the workshop manageable, but it also served a design function. It operationalised the proposed Window of Opportunity, or Kairos, mechanic by making the value of an investigative action dependent on when it occurred.

In this sense, time was not designed as a uniform countdown that merely accelerated the player towards a predetermined ending. Instead, it was intended to create situated investigative opportunities. Participants could request reports that were available immediately, but some materials represented processes that would take time within the fictional police procedure. Trace analysis, archival material, crime-pattern reports, and information obtained through extended interrogation could become available only after a delay. Participants therefore had to decide whether a report was worth waiting for, whether they should continue investigating while it was pending, whether they should pursue another line of inquiry, or whether they already possessed enough evidence to move towards accusation.

These decisions constituted kairotic moments because they involved the relation between an opportunity and its timing. A report requested early enough could still influence a later interrogation or provide support for a final accusation. The same report requested later in the session might arrive only when there was insufficient time to interpret it, test it through dialogue, or integrate it into a coherent case. Likewise, continuing to question a suspect could generate an important contradiction or disclosure, but it could also consume time that might have been used to obtain additional forensic or contextual material. The workshop therefore required participants to assess not only what information they wanted, but when that information would still have practical value.

Several sessions showed participants making explicit or observable decisions of this kind. Gui (S08), for example, requested delayed information connected to the wider robbery pattern and Kae’s drug-related contacts. He chose to continue investigating while waiting for these reports rather than immediately moving to a final accusation. This approach enabled him to obtain further material, including Kae’s confession tape and information related to the robbery series. At the same time, the session demonstrates that accumulating more evidence did not automatically produce a settled theory. Gui accessed substantial material relevant to both Kae and Elara, but remained more convinced by the case against Kae. Even after acquiring evidence that could have supported Elara’s presence at the crime scene, he ultimately accused Kae of murder. The significance of this case is not that additional evidence produced the objectively correct answer, but that delayed information became part of an evolving interpretive process in which the participant continued to reassess the case.

Other sessions demonstrated the opposite strategic decision. Titi (S06) requested the trace analysis associated with Elara’s injuries but chose not to wait for the result before continuing the investigation. This did not necessarily indicate impatience or failure to understand the system. It represented a decision that the available evidence, dialogue opportunities, and remaining time could be used more productively elsewhere. The decision to wait or not wait was therefore itself meaningful investigative behaviour. It showed that participants were capable of treating information as something with an opportunity cost rather than as content that should always be collected before proceeding.

The session involving Julie and Steph (S13) similarly showed how delayed information could be incorporated into an organised theory-building process. The participants read and discussed reports carefully, divided the work between reading and note-taking, and prepared a theory before using dialogue to test it. They waited for a relevant report before proceeding with the final confrontation, but this delay did not prevent them from maintaining a coherent direction. Their final accusation held Elara responsible for manslaughter and Kae responsible for gun possession. In this case, waiting functioned as a calculated investment in evidential support rather than an interruption of play. The delayed report was understood as potentially valuable because it related to a theory already under construction.

The most direct evidence of temporal pressure occurred when participants recognised that the available investigation period was approaching its end and altered their behaviour accordingly. Helen and Andrew (S09), for example, became aware of the time, reviewed their evidence, and attempted one final interrogation before being required to present their case. Their final theory was not sufficiently developed, and they were unable to provide a coherent accusation against either suspect. The resulting dismissal of Detective Stoker did not simply indicate that they had failed to solve the case. It showed that they had reached a point at which further inquiry was no longer available and were required to act with the incomplete interpretation they had constructed.

This outcome demonstrates an important aspect of the Kairos mechanic. The window of opportunity does not guarantee that participants will make an optimal decision. Instead, it creates a condition in which the participant must recognise that a decision is necessary even when certainty remains unavailable. Helen and Andrew had organised their evidence extensively, created spatial groupings, taken notes, and attempted to relate the available material through a Motive, Means, and Opportunity framework. Their difficulty did not arise from a lack of engagement with the evidence. Rather, they were unable to transform their accumulated material into a sufficiently supported accusation before the temporal opportunity closed. This distinction matters because it shows that investigative effort, evidence collection, and final commitment are related but not identical activities.

Rodrigo’s (S03) session provided another clear example of forced commitment under temporal pressure. He developed a substantial case against Kae, drawing on the firearm, Kae’s statements, and other contextual evidence. However, he continued to retain doubts concerning Elara’s role, particularly in relation to the witness account and the evidence that she had been found barefoot with injuries consistent with the crime-scene environment. As the available time diminished, he had to decide whether to continue pursuing these unresolved questions or present the stronger case already available to him. He ultimately accused Kae of murder while Elara was released.

Rodrigo’s decision is analytically important because it was not a simple error caused by overlooking evidence. His observations indicate that the unresolved questions remained present in his reasoning. The time constraint altered the status of those questions: they did not disappear, but they could no longer be pursued indefinitely. The final accusation therefore represented an institutional judgment made under incomplete knowledge. This corresponds directly to the intended role of Kairos in the design proposal. The player is not asked to wait until all uncertainty has been eliminated. Instead, the player must recognise when the available evidence is sufficient to justify action, while accepting responsibility for the possibility that further investigation might have produced a different interpretation.

Ricky’s (S02) session similarly showed how temporal pressure could reshape the final stages of investigation. He engaged extensively with the evidence corpus, used reports to structure questions, revised his theory during the session, and eventually returned his attention to Elara after previously pursuing Kae. Near the end of the investigation, he wanted to continue exploring Elara’s involvement and initiated a final interrogation. This interaction led to Elara challenging him to take the drug and enter the dream sequence. Ricky accepted the challenge, entered the dream material, and then presented a final accusation in which Elara was held responsible for manslaughter and Kae for obstruction of evidence. The sequence demonstrates that a limited remaining window can encourage participants to make consequential choices about where to direct their final attention. The dream sequence was not simply an optional narrative diversion in this context. It became a late investigative opportunity that reframed the available evidence and contributed to the participant’s final interpretation.

At the same time, the workshop showed that temporal pressure could generate frustration. Gui (S08) expressed a desire to investigate the evidence and question the suspects further, but felt constrained by the lack of remaining time. In another session, a participant reported that with more time they would have attempted to resolve continuing doubts concerning the barefoot figure, the evidence of footprints, the relation between the suspects, and the motive for the shooting. These responses are useful because they show that the temporal limit was not invisible or purely administrative. Participants experienced it as part of the investigative condition. They understood that the case could potentially be explored further, yet had to decide whether the available material was enough to support an accusation.

This frustration should not be interpreted automatically as evidence that the Kairos mechanic failed. A detective investigation designed around ambiguity should not allow every contradiction to be resolved without cost, because doing so would reduce the need for prioritisation, commitment, and interpretive responsibility. The relevant question is whether participants understood the source of the limitation and whether they retained meaningful choices within it. When participants knew that time was limited, could identify possible investigative actions, and chose between waiting, questioning, gathering more evidence, or accusing, time pressure contributed to the intended experience of epistemic agency. It made the player responsible not only for interpreting evidence but also for deciding when an interpretation was strong enough to act upon.

Nevertheless, the rehearsal also revealed conditions under which temporal pressure could become unproductive. Some sessions were affected by unclear dialogue limits, uncertain interaction boundaries, prototype errors, or inconsistent facilitation. In these cases, time could be spent attempting interactions that were not clearly supported by the prototype rather than making purposeful investigative decisions. Participants sometimes did not know whether a failed action reflected an invalid evidential relation, an unavailable report, an exhausted dialogue path, or an unintended limitation of the low-fidelity system. When this occurred, the temporal constraint risked being experienced as avoidable friction rather than as a meaningful opportunity cost.

The session involving John and Fred (S12) provides an important example. Both participants showed strong engagement with the case, made numerous requests, and attempted to construct explanations around the drug evidence, the footwear impressions, the firearm, and the possibility that both suspects were involved. However, their investigation was also affected by unclear dialogue boundaries, incompatible collaborative approaches, sustained attention to misleading evidence, and a facilitation failure in which Kae did not produce an intended confession response. Their frustration escalated into the use of the revolver as an intimidation device, which triggered a forced final confrontation before they had constructed a sufficiently supported accusation. The resulting dismissal was partly consistent with the intended consequence of coercive escalation, but it cannot be treated as pure evidence that time pressure alone produced a meaningful kairotic decision. The session was also shaped by prototype limitations that made the dialogue system difficult to interpret.

This qualification is necessary for understanding the workshop findings responsibly. The rehearsal suggests that time can create meaningful investigative pressure, but only when the player can understand the available action space. A participant must be able to distinguish between uncertainty that belongs to the case and opacity that results from the interface or facilitation. Productive uncertainty occurs when several interpretations remain plausible and the participant must decide which evidence to prioritise. Unproductive uncertainty occurs when the participant cannot determine what actions are possible, why an interaction failed, whether information is pending, or whether the system has responded consistently. The former supports epistemic agency; the latter risks turning investigation into trial-and-error.

The final accusation therefore functioned as both a narrative event and an institutional evaluation of the participant’s reasoning. Captain Miller did not simply reveal whether the participant had selected the intended culprit. Instead, the Captain assessed whether the participant could articulate a coherent relation between the evidence gathered, the interpretation proposed, and the accusation made. Participants were required to move from individual fragments of information, such as a firearm, a wet hoodie, toxicology evidence, a witness statement, a confession, a trace report, or an inconsistent alibi, to a claim concerning responsibility. The final confrontation made this transformation visible. It required players to take ownership of their theory rather than merely receive the game’s explanation of what had happened.

This process also had an ethical dimension. The case involved uncertainty concerning Elara’s awareness, memory, and capacity at the time of the homicide; Kae’s possible role in providing drugs, concealing evidence, and redirecting responsibility; and the difference between physical involvement, legal culpability, and institutional proof. Participants could not treat the final accusation as a neutral mechanical selection. Their interpretation had consequences for how the suspects were understood: as intentional perpetrators, impaired agents, accomplices, protectors, manipulators, or individuals caught within a larger pattern of drug use and concealment. The workshop therefore positioned accusation as an act of responsibility under uncertainty.

The findings suggest that the Window of Opportunity mechanic can support epistemic agency when it is implemented as a structure of prioritisation and commitment rather than as a simple timer. The relevant design function is not to punish players for reading slowly or to force premature decisions arbitrarily. Rather, it is to make investigative actions consequential by ensuring that time spent pursuing one possibility cannot also be spent pursuing every other possibility. Requesting a report, waiting for analysis, revisiting a contradiction, interrogating a suspect, entering a dream sequence, and submitting an accusation should each carry different temporal and narrative implications. This gives the player responsibility for the path taken through the case without requiring the system to reduce the investigation to a single correct route.

For the later digital prototype, this implies that the temporal system should make opportunities and consequences legible. Players should be able to recognise which reports are pending, which investigative routes remain available, which characters can still be approached, and whether a decision may close or alter a future path. The system should communicate the changing state of the investigation through diegetic means such as institutional reminders, changes in character availability, report-processing status, environmental shifts, sound design, and narrative escalation. However, it should avoid presenting time as an arbitrary external punishment. The aim is to preserve the sense that an investigation is a process of making situated judgments with incomplete information.

The digital implementation should also distinguish between productive delay and unnecessary friction. A delayed report should create a meaningful decision because its potential evidential value is understandable, not because the player has been prevented from accessing information without explanation. Similarly, a final confrontation should feel like the consequence of the player’s investigative priorities, accumulated evidence, and previous dialogue choices, rather than an abrupt interruption caused by unclear system rules. If players are required to act before all uncertainty is resolved, they should still understand why the moment of commitment has arrived and what alternatives they have chosen not to pursue.

Overall, this theme indicates that the rehearsal supported the project’s argument that detective gameplay can be organised around interpretation, prioritisation, and accountable commitment. Participants did not only collect evidence or attempt to identify a hidden answer. They developed provisional theories, pursued and abandoned investigative routes, waited for or bypassed delayed information, responded to approaching deadlines, and made accusations despite remaining uncertainty. The rehearsal does not provide generalisable evidence that all players will experience temporal pressure in the same way. However, it provides design evidence that a kairotic structure can make time part of the epistemic activity of investigation. When supported by clear affordances, reliable feedback, and meaningful alternatives, the Window of Opportunity can transform time from an external limit into a mechanism through which players decide what matters, when further inquiry is justified, and when they are willing to take responsibility for a theory.

## 5.5 Summary of Results

[State what the workshop established and what it changed] 

-What supported the design proposal, what failed, what you revised before digital prototyping-

The early design rehearsals provided an initial qualitative evaluation of the proposed detective-game systems before their translation into a digital prototype. The purpose of the workshops was not to validate a finished game, establish statistically generalisable findings, or identify a single optimal investigative path. Instead, the rehearsals examined whether participants could engage with the central design proposition of the project: that detective gameplay can be organised around the interpretation, comparison, testing, and revision of evidence-based theories rather than around the discovery of a hidden designer-defined answer.

The findings suggest that participants did not treat the evidence corpus merely as a collection of documents to unlock or read. Across the sessions, participants actively organised reports, created notes, constructed timelines, grouped material by suspect or theme, drew diagrams, compared contradictory accounts, and developed provisional explanations of what may have occurred. These practices were especially visible when participants externalised their reasoning through physical organisation. Evidence piles, sticky notes, diagrams, tables, clips, and handwritten records allowed participants to make relationships between reports visible and to return to them during later stages of the investigation. This supports the central premise that detective-game evidence can function as material for theory construction rather than as a sequence of fixed progression keys.

At the same time, the workshop demonstrated that interpretive freedom requires a legible interaction structure. Participants could often understand the general logic of the evidence-request system and the value of using evidence during dialogue. However, they also encountered uncertainty concerning which actions were available, which characters could be approached, why certain combinations failed, how inquiries differed from confrontations, and whether an unsuccessful interaction resulted from a weak theory, a system boundary, or a prototype error. These moments showed the importance of distinguishing productive ambiguity from unproductive opacity. Ambiguity was productive when participants had enough information to construct several plausible interpretations and were required to decide which one to pursue. Opacity was unproductive when participants could not understand the operational conditions of the system itself.

The rehearsal also showed that dialogue could function as a meaningful form of epistemic resistance. Participants were not simply given information by NPCs after presenting an item. Instead, the relevance of their evidence, the clarity of their investigative intention, the character being addressed, and the prior state of the conversation shaped the response. When participants could use evidence to formulate a coherent question or challenge, dialogue generated clarification, denial, emotional resistance, partial disclosure, or new contradictions. These outcomes became additional material for interpretation. However, the workshops also revealed that this system required more explicit feedback in the digital prototype. A player should be able to understand that a character is resisting a weak, unsupported, repetitive, or coercive line of reasoning without the game automatically revealing which conclusion they should reach.

Dream engagement provided a further form of interpretive possibility. The dream sequence was memorable for several participants and could encourage curiosity, emotional engagement, and alternative readings of Elara’s condition, memory, and relationship to the case. In its most productive form, the dream–awake loop encouraged participants to reconsider documentary evidence through symbolic material without treating the dream as direct factual proof. The workshop also showed that symbolic material did not automatically become actionable investigative knowledge. Some participants entered the dream sequence but struggled to determine how it related to the murder, while others developed more complex theories by connecting dream material to pharmacological evidence, altered consciousness, or the previous detective’s notes. The revised prototype therefore needs to provide clearer bridges between symbolic interpretation, waking evidence, and available dialogue actions while preserving the dream’s ambiguity.

The sessions involving more than one participant revealed both the value and the limits of collaborative investigation. Some participants spontaneously divided the practical and cognitive labour of the case by reading reports aloud, taking notes, organising evidence, or challenging each other’s developing theories. In several cases, discussion supported insights that did not appear to emerge from one participant independently. However, collaboration could also produce disagreement, unequal control over the investigation, conflicting priorities, and reduced opportunities for an individual player to follow uncertain or speculative ideas. The later digital prototype should therefore remain self-sufficient as a single-player experience while making the player’s reasoning visible enough to support optional co-located discussion and shared interpretation.

Finally, the workshop established the importance of the temporal structure later described as the Window of Opportunity, or Kairos, mechanic. Participants had to decide whether further reports were worth pursuing, whether delayed information justified waiting, whether an unresolved contradiction required another interrogation, and when their available theory was sufficiently coherent to present to Captain Miller. The deadline did not simply end the workshop. It could transform the status of evidence by making particular investigative opportunities available only for a limited period. Participants were sometimes required to commit to an accusation while retaining doubts, alternative theories, or untested lines of inquiry. This created a form of institutional judgment under incomplete knowledge. The resulting findings suggest that time can support epistemic agency when it creates meaningful prioritisation and commitment, but only when the available actions, pending reports, and consequences of delay are sufficiently legible.

Taken together, the workshop findings did not confirm that every proposed system was already effective. Instead, they identified the conditions under which the systems could better support player-led interpretation. The subsequent revision therefore focused on four connected objectives: improving the internal consistency of the evidence corpus; clarifying the relationship between evidence, institutional requests, dialogue, and player intention; expanding the narrative and spatial context in which evidence could be encountered; and providing better support for the externalisation, revision, and communication of player theories.

The low-fidelity rehearsal was consequently treated as a formative Research-through-Design stage. Its value lay not only in identifying successful interactions, but also in making design limitations visible before they became embedded in the digital prototype. The revised narrative and interaction structure described in the following chapter emerged directly from this process of observation, correction, and iteration.
