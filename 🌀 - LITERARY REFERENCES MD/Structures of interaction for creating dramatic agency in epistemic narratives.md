## Structures of interaction for creating dramatic agency in epistemic narratives: Return of the Obra Dinn and Telling Lies as design exemplars

Brad Rittenhouse, T.M. Gasque, Kevin Xu Tang * , Janet Murray

Digital Integrative Liberal Arts Center, Georgia Institute of Technology, Atlanta, GA, United States

<!-- image -->

## ARTICLE INFO

Keywords: Interactive narrative Epistemic immersion Dramatic agency

## ABSTRACT

Epistemic narratives, according to Marie-Laure Ryan, are driven by our need to know something. Interactive epistemic narratives raise the question of how to provoke and satisfy this need while hiding information from an active consumer, an interactor who is expecting the characteristic pleasure Janet Murray has defined as dramatic agency. In this paper we analyse two approaches to creating the experience of dramatic agency in epistemic interactive narratives, the spatial navigation of Lucas Pope's Return of the Obra Dinn (2018) and the video database of Sam Barlow's Telling Lies (2019). For both interactive narratives we identify characteristic design strategies and the challenges and opportunities they offer for the development of the genre, and we discuss the light they shed on the problem of closure in epistemic narrative.

## 1. Dramatic agency in epistemic narratives

In recent years, interactive narrative has been recognized as a distinct genre independent of, though often overlapping with, video games [1,2]. As such it has its own aesthetics and can be assessed by how well the mechanics of interaction reinforce the narrative experience. To the extent that mechanics and narrative diverge, they display ludonarrative dissonance [3]; to the extent that they converge, the inter­ actor experiences dramatic agency, in which the mechanics of interac­ tion map tightly to the experience of arousing and satisfying narrative curiosity. Dramatic agency need not entail affecting the events of a story; it can be produced by navigating a story through a set of choices that engage the intent of the interactor to actively investigate a particular narrative thread [4]. Much of the controversy over ludology versus narratology has reflected the awkwardness of interrupting gameplay and suspending agency in order to introduce story content, as with cut scenes. But it is also true that the wrong game mechanics can become a jarring distraction from a compelling interactive story. In order to reinforce the experience of dramatic agency, the mechanics must be transparent and congruent with the narrative situation. Although ludonarrative dissonance is usually invoked to express discontent at the ways in which narrative disrupts game expectations, accepting interactive narrative as its own genre allows us to reverse the lens to ask how game mechanics and other strategies of interaction disrupt or reinforce dra­ matic agency.

Marie-Laure Ryan has identified one form of narrative which has been particularly fruitful for digital storytelling as "epistemic," i.e. stories in which our engagement is "driven by the need to know" [5]. Detective stories are the model for epistemic storytelling. Within the diverse meta-genre of interactive narrative, epistemic narratives allow interactors to enact their narrative curiosity through the navigation of fixed narrative fragments of an unchangeable event. They may take the form of detective stories like the Frogware Sherlock Holmes games [6] or Capcom's Phoenix Wright: Ace Attorney series [7], in which the interactor assembles incriminating evidence, confronts the murderer, and achieves closure by bringing a villain to justice. Or they may refuse closure, like the postmodern hypertext rhizomes of the late 20th century (e.g. Michael Joyce's Afternoon [8]) that have no clear ending, as an affir­ mation of the epistemic openness of all systems of meaning.

The main design challenge for interactive detective story structure is giving the interactor the illusion of open exploration of a puzzling fictional universe, while sequencing the revelation of information so that suspense is maintained. Suspense is dissipated when the exploration does not open up narrative questions, and also when the answers to those questions are prematurely revealed. Just as fighting games are structured by levels of difficulty such that increasingly difficult oppo­ nents must be defeated before reaching the final big boss opponent, adventure games are often structured as a set of puzzles located in vir­ tual spaces within the game world (as in Ken and Roberta Williams' Mystery House [9]). In a detective story, information may be hidden by embedded clues in the dialog trees of non-player characters (as in Phoenix Wright), linked to events that happen at assigned moments on the fictional timeline (as in Deadline [10] or The Last Express [11]); or attached to objects that are hidden in puzzle-protected areas of a limited space (as in Gone Home [12]). The challenge for all of these strategies is to create and reward narrative curiosity through the careful pacing of information revelation. The pacing of revelations is complicated by interaction because the designer must provide multiple options that all result in sustained narrative interest without premature revelation. This purpose is often met by gating systems.

Just as games are often divided into levels with access limited until a puzzle is solved, a villain is defeated, or a coordination skill is mastered, so interactive stories are often regulated by narrative gates. Our concept of gating systems has parallels with Clara Fern´andez-Vara's definition of indexical storytelling: "a strategy to construct the narrative of the game, based on leaving traces and affecting the space, either on the part of the designer or the player" [13]. Gates as we understand them are inten­ tionally designed to prevent access to later story segments. However, they also serve the dual purpose of enticing curiosity and signifying to interactors the next step that they should take. Like how a physical door signifies from where to enter a room, narrative gates act like Fern´andezVara's indices that "help the player situate herself in the world, and relate her experience in the game with her previous knowledge." They simultaneously bar access to certain content while beckoning players to discover that hidden content through their efforts.

Epistemic stories based on the exploration and interpretation of an archive of narrative segments must include such gates while sustaining the illusion of free exploration based on clues. This paper will look closely at two examples of epistemic narratives and the differences in their gating structures: Lucas Pope's Return of the Obra Dinn [14], structured as an adventure game, and Sam Barlow's Telling Lies [15], structured as a searchable video archive (Fig. 1).

## 2. Corpses as narrative gates in Return of the Obra Dinn

Return of the Obra Dinn is an adventure-style game in which the player is placed in the role of an insurance investigator tasked with discovering the fates of 60-odd ship passengers of a recently reappeared ghost ship. Present-tense exploration is anchored in the physical exploration of the ghost ship, which is filled with hidden corpses. Story revelations are significantly organized by an interactive object, a 135page notebook (see Fig. 2) complete with "Table of Contents," group portraits of the ship's passengers, and chapter sections separating the game's narrative into individual arcs.

Obra Dinn opens in 1807 Falmouth with the player on a rowboat docked next to the eponymous ship, which has mysteriously returned to port passenger-less after three years at sea. As an insurance investigator for the East India Company, the player must navigate the threedimensional ship and piece together the events that led to its doom. Aided by a magic compass that transports the player to the moment of any discovered corpse's demise, she also physically navigates 3D, frozen, tableaux vivants which serve as layered narrative vignettes giving glimpses into the ship's death spiral (see Fig. 3).

It is in both navigating the present-day ship to find new corpses/ cases, and in physically maneuvering in and inspecting the frozen vi­ gnettes that the player constructs a sense of agency as the investigator on the case. Within the fictional world, only the player character has the power to navigate this grisly scene and, by sheer close observation and careful deduction, uncover the truth of how and why dozens of people met their end aboard the ship. In Obra Dinn physical corpses are the gates to greater story content. Finding a corpse triggers the appearance of one of the death scene vignettes that make up the chapters of the larger story, and new corpses appear in the physical world of the ship after discovering them in a vignette. They are generally encountered based on physical proximity but in a complex chronological order that sends the truth-seeking interactor back and forth around the ship to review vignettes and match details in order to piece together a coherent temporal sequence and identify the same individual across vignettes.

The notebook, in which the player records their steadily growing knowledge, serves as a consistent anchor for the cognitive work of assembling the intricately interlocking pieces of the narrative. Reference to the notebook allows the interactor to reorient themselves as they backtrack to re-examine, but the notebook in Obra Dinn does not behave like an ordinary paper object. It is the interface through which the player manipulates information, providing templates with multiple choices to fill in who perished (or disappeared), how they met their fate, and at whose hands. The notebook, significantly referred to as a "catalogue" ingame, provides some expected digital affordances, allowing players to surface all vignettes containing a specific character with a single click, to bookmark important flashbacks, and to easily link characters as they appear physically in the game world with the information recorded about them in the log. Through the design affordances of the notebook, the epistemic work of information retention, management, and manipulation becomes tightly integrated with the transparent gameplay of spatial navigation. Movement through the notebook and through the ship are gated into chapters that map onto physical spaces, but successive retracing of the physical space is motivated by the need to connect the narrative segments.

One of the major successes of Obra Dinn is its ability to use this gating, counterintuitively, to increase the player's sense of agency and engagement. Once the player boards the ship from the rowboat, she has access to a single body and thus a single vignette. The hatches to the lower decks are closed, almost literally gating the player's progress. After activating and investigating the first tableau, the player gains ac­ cess to the Captain's quarters and four more bodies/vignettes, one of which triggers the opening of the main hatch, in turn giving the player access to the gun deck and two more bodies/vignettes (see Fig. 4). These initial bodies serve as narrative starting points, bringing the player (usually) into the start or the end of a chapter in the game's journal. After the player investigates this initial body's tragic vignette, a mystical light springs from her mystical compass, leading her to the next body in this chapter, usually detailing events either immediately before or after the demise of the prior soul (see Fig. 5). There is some mechanical freedom here: players can abandon one chain for another from within the limited selection of bodies not currently gated. But the narrative impetus to complete a sub-story favors a chapter-by-chapter investigation.

The physical world of Obra Dinn is highly stylized in black and white line drawings, which increases the sense of other-worldliness within a romanticized past, helping to build openness to the magical mechanics of investigation by time-travel. Obra Dinn's spatial design reinforces narrative immersion and makes the gating of narrative segments by corpses doubly pleasurable because the physical discovery of another body leads to the narrative revelation of a new plot segment. The sense of freedom from being allowed to move around the beautifully drawn and distinctively stylized ship is therefore balanced by the directing guidance of the light which provides direction to the spatial process of discovery.

As Derrida writes in Archive Fever: "It is to have a compulsive, re­ petitive, and nostalgic desire for the archive, an irrepressible desire to return to the origin, a homesickness, a nostalgia for return to the most archaic place of absolute commencement" [17]. In fictional game nar­ ratives, players have no reference for the origin, the Platonic truth of a story, outside of socio-juridical hegemonies. Participatory feedback mechanics, therefore, are all the more important in orienting players in archival game narratives. In Obra Dinn, a notebook pops up after each vignette asking the player a very simple series of questions: "Who is this?", "How did they die?", "Who or what killed them?". The player then chooses answers from a context-sensitive list of options (about 60 options for each question, so guessing is not practical), and "fates"-the correct combination of answers for a given character-are only confirmed and "locked in" when the player submits three accurate so­ lutions. "Locking in" results in the player's guesses converting from pinned notes in the book to being inscribed on the page, and three dits being animated on a progress tracker at the end of the journal (see Figs. 6,7). This is a gating mechanic that controls the pace of revelation and also offers reinforcement of action - and hence the experience of dramatic agency - throughout the gameplay, rather than waiting until the end when all the corpses have been found and enough of the iden­ tifiable passengers have been correctly named to trigger one of the endings.

This small bit of participatory feedback has quite a large impact on the player's sense of progress and involvement in the narrative. She feels as though she is producing catharsis repeatedly, irrespective of the ending of the overarching narrative, and magnified by mechanical, sound, and visual design of the feedback surrounding player progress.

Discovering all available corpses provides the player with the chance to finish their investigative work and leave the ship, and for those who successfully make the right deductions through the corpse-and-notebook navigation, Obra Dinn offers narrative closure by explaining all the mysteries (supernaturally, in accordance with the ghost ship genre). On choosing to end the game, players are rewarded based on their perfor­ mance in the form of an insurance document listing the fates of the ship's passengers and the potential fines or rewards that are imposed on the person's estate. For those who barely complete any fates, the document is mostly empty, and the investigator receives a letter berating them for their performance. An average performance receives a satis­ factory ending, and solving every single fate reveals to the player a final epilogue that shows the fates of three passengers who could not be discovered up to that point. In its conclusion, Obra Dinn rewards players for their investment in the game and provides their actions up to that point a sense of finality and dramatic import.

## 3. Keywords trails as narrative gates in Telling Lies

Since human experience is temporal, and there is usually something that happens last in any sequence of events, it is hard to write a story or to experience even the most labyrinthian story structure without imposing a temporal sequence of beginning-middle-end. A database or video archive, however, is the opposite of a temporally-ordered sequence. It exists in the misnamed "random" access present. That is, we can separate the sequence in which we call things up from their linear order (which is what we mean by "random") by calling them up through metadata (which is highly specific and opposite of the ordinary meaning of "random"). Lev Manovich has pointed out "As a cultural form, database represents the world as a list of items and it refuses to order this list. In contrast, a narrative creates a cause-and-effect trajectory of seemingly unordered items (events)" [18]. Despite this opposition, Sam Barlow has created two well-received fixed story interactive narratives that use database search of a video archive as the predominant game mechanic. This approach poses two related design challenges. First, how can the designer allow access across the temporal restructure without cutting short the satisfying experience of narrative curiosity by rewarding it prematurely? Second, how can the designer constrain access to some segments while maintaining the transparency of interface mechanics that makes for the successful experience of dra­ matic agency?

In Telling Lies, the player is tasked with exploring a database of 169 component narrative video segments before the end of the night (see Fig. 10). This database documents more than a year of the life of David Smith, undercover FBI Agent and is composed of telephone conversa­ tions (with each side recorded separately), along with a few social media posts and surveilled group encounters. Together these videos form four storylines, all of which center around the downward trajectory of David. David begins as a faithful husband, loving father, and idealistic lawenforcement officer charged with investigating eco-activists. He then commits a series of betrayals that culminate in a violently destructive and suicidal act of eco-terrorism. In Telling Lies, keywords act as nonphysical gates to later narrative content, and its gates are enforced through the design of the script and unveiling of important keywords.

Telling Lies' potential for multiple player narratives through the constructed narrative is by design. The algorithm driving the search engine only returns five videos with the search term and does not show or hide specific videos based on player progression. It is not trying to show you new things. It is trying to hide chronologically later videos using the same search term. The search terms that reveal later sections are like Easter eggs hidden in select segments - they are unique terms that you are not likely to guess unless you have gone through a lot of the story. For example, we don't find out that David Jones' real name is David Smith until a very late scene, and "Smith" is the only keyword we personally found to unlock the final suicide scene.

This gating technique uses unique search terms the same way that Obra Dinn uses hidden bodies and locked doors. In Telling Lies, the gates are enforced by the design of the searching algorithm and the dramatic script. Design documents that Sam Barlow shared with us include an elaborate spreadsheet cross-listing every word in the script and every video segment. It makes clear that the narrative script was designed so that earlier segments have more successful search terms than later ones.

In two design documents that Sam Barlow shared with the authors of this paper, the story elements are ordered in a table with dramatic scenes (in temporal order) forming the rows, and 4 columns for each of the main characters, David and the three women he is involved with: his wife Emma and the mother of his young daughter; Ava, the young activist David seduces and impregnates; and Maxine, a sex cam performer with whom he has salacious conversations. (They have other names in the design document and the plot developments are different from the final version, but the characters and major events are the same). The conversations are divided into 8 sections labeled A-H, each of which asks a question in each of the story threads (see Fig. 11). The final story provides a date and time stamp for all of the conversations, creating a linear timeline that runs from August 2017 through November 2018.

The scenes are skillfully written, directed, and acted to elicit narra­ tive curiosity about what is being said on the other end, so as to prompt the interactor to further investigation. For example, the game begins with a preloaded search term "LOVE" which pulls up a clip of David

## 4. The problem of closure in epistemic narratives

A key design challenge for epistemic interactive narrative is how to create a sense of an ending, the narrative "closure" that comes from feeling that the complete story has been witnessed and understood. Since both Obra Dinn and Telling Lies offer stories in fragments that can be navigated in multiple ways, and whose layered meanings may be overlooked on any single transversal, the interactor can be left with a feeling of dissatisfaction after leaving the experience. Many people never finish video games, and in the case of narrative games they can consult YouTube videos or game criticism sites to find out more about what they missed. In other words, they can switch from interactors experiencing dramatic agency to viewers experiencing closure, or at least a satisfying sense of saturation, as described in Hamlet on the Hol­ odeck, Chapter 4, in which all the possible permutations of a situation have been exhausted and it loses its emotional appeal, like a repetitive make-believe scenario a child exhausts and outgrows.

Differences in the games' formats inform these different approaches and solutions. The database narrative structure of Telling Lies limits the degree to which the player can interact with the narrative and, thus, the immersiveness of the experience. Mirroring the film medium, the narrative exists in pre-recorded live-action videos and the player's "verbs" are limited to "searching", "playing", and "scrubbing", with some limited ability to record information in a Notepad-like program. Other important elements that are limited (working from Murray's ontology) in Telling Lies are the spatial and the participatory.

Obra Dinn, on the other hand, locates the player in two compelling physical realities, the mysterious ship and the magical vignettes. Obra Dinn offers the sense of closure of winning a game. If you identify 58 of the names you open the gate to the variant of a final scene that gives you a clue to the rest of the puzzle, and the sense of closure is emblemized in the completion of the catalogue of the dead. The structure of the story told in Obra Dinn is a picaresque adventure tale without a hero picaro. The hero is the detective, and the narrative satisfaction lies in the frame story the player character enacts. The narrative is therefore the win­ ning/losing of the game of identification, the cleverness of observation, the matching wits against the designer of the game. The pleasure of Obra Dinn relies much more on the game-structures than on the intrinsic in­ terest of the characters who are too many and too short-lived to indi­ vidually engage us. The satisfaction is in winning, more than in learning what happened, because the final solution to the mystery does not deepen our understanding of the characters or moral universe they inhabit.

In contrast, the satisfactions of Telling Lies derive from the narrative structure. We care what happens to each of the main characters and what happens to them at the end of the story, and even in their lives after the main timeline of the story. The events on the timeline make up four stories, each with their own climax, and all four main characters emerge from the events in a dramatically different place than they are in at the beginning. The women's stories are all survivor tales, and David's story, which takes up 96 of the 169 segments, is, as we said above, similar to a classic Aristotelian tragedy. But one might argue that one effect of the database structure is to undermine the sense of catharsis and closure that David's story might otherwise have produced. David is a deluded, arrogant hero who goes from a high position to total destruction by way of a series of crises and revelations that lead to devastating selfknowledge. But we don't finish the story when we experience his sui­ cide. We know that we are finished when we have explored the situation from every angle, creating the experience Murray identified as "satu­ ration," [4] or as Alex Navarro described it in a "Quick Look" for the Giant Bomb website, "You will know when you are satisfied when you know you are satisfied" [19].

Each of these women whom David has exploited gets an epilogue after the ending of the game, showing how their lives turned out years later, and all three pointedly say that they refused to look at the video with his final message to them, because of their disgust with his patterns of betrayal. The player sees only one of these epilogues on any one playthrough, based on which woman's videos they spent the most time with, but this teaser motivates replay, encouraging us to return to the game and watch more videos in order to see them all and experience all the epilogues. There are therefore four different endings to the action of the story, but none of them closes off the possibility of further explo­ rations of the archive, and while they all exist within the fabula of the narrative, only one exists within the experiential syuzhet of a playthrough.

Telling Lies, as a result of its loose gating structure, takes a laissez faire approach that can leave players feeling lost and unsure whether they are making progress. To be sure, this can be a desired effect, particularly in the database narrative genre where creators may be trying to evoke a feeling of informational sublimity. However, the absence of well-defined gates can lead to floundering, a state in which interactors lack direction on how to traverse the database and, upon discovering they have no narrative direction, experience growing frustration. Interactors in Telling Lies can easily find themselves locked in a dead-end with no new videos and no obvious breadcrumbs leading to a new trail of videos, forcing them to randomly probe the database for new clues. Further failed searches then lead to frustration and a decrease in the interactor's active creation of belief.

Consistent and well-designed participatory feedback, at least in this case study, seems to contribute, rather than detract, from the ergodic pleasures of piecing together a mystery. Aarseth defines ergodic litera­ ture as requiring nontrivial effort to traverse a text:

"If ergodic literature is to make sense as a concept, there must also be nonergodic literature, where the effort to traverse the text is trivial, with no extranoematic responsibilities placed on the reader except (for example) eye movement and the periodic or arbitrary turning of pages" [20].

One oft-noted joy of video games is their ability to structure and gamify progress, thus providing players who may find the sense of real life "progress" elusive a more concrete feeling of "getting things done." There is likely more work to be done on the implementation of light­ weight design around database narrative games, work that Barlow has already contributed much to, but in such a new genre, as Murray notes in Inventing the Medium, the proliferation of conventions often counterin­ tuitively contributes to the genre beginning to feel more "naturalistic." Ergodic literature, for instance, can rely upon well-established conven­ tions, aesthetic devices, symbol systems, tropes, etc. and be fairly confident that experienced readers, at least, can call upon a common literary culture to decode difficult texts.

Obra Dinn's notebook helps reveal design methods that could alle­ viate the pitfalls of the floundering found in Telling Lies via its merger between these ludic and narrative structures. The deduction rating in the game serves as a visual indicator for the difficulty of deducing who the individual is and how they died. This, oddly enough, serves as a semiotic representation of a potential ergodic relationship a player can develop with a character. Low deduction rating provides more narrative based character appreciation as they often have very easy to observe relationships or character identification. One such example is the cap­ tain's wife, who we first discover lying in the captain's quarters as we hear the final sad moments of his life before he kills himself. However, as the deduction rating increases the ergodic nature of the experience, using both narrative and game clues to make deductive leaps in logic, means that players can form an attachment that isn't wholly narrativeor gameplay-based, but a merger of the two. One such character is the New Guinean topman Maba, who has no speaking role in the narrative, but is a personal favorite of one of the researchers due to his omni­ presence, despite being a non-named character to the narrative. This allows for the higher deduction characters to still retain some minor level of narrative relevance instead of simply being game pieces to solve the puzzle aspect of the detective fiction. Nonetheless, many of the bit characters are hard to distinguish among, and guessing is a common solution to determining their identities. Thus, the combination of active creation of belief with the gamelike struggle to maintain flow through the experience creates a comprehension of the database that combines potentially painful disjointed aspects of the experience to achieve something more than the separate parts should achieve.

## 5. Conclusion

In All Data Are Local Yanni Loukissas warns us that vast databases often decontextualize information, but data always belong to specific places and social structures and are influenced by how they were collected and by whom:

Data are useful precisely because they provide unfamiliar perspectives, from other times, places, and standpoints that we would not be able to access otherwise. The strangeness of data is its strength [21].

The strangeness of data is well-suited to fictional narrative. Furthermore, the process of exploring datasets prompts an investigative mindset as individuals try to find contextualization for the sea of in­ formation in which they are submerged. Telling Lies invites the player into the experience with no specific task for direction, leaving the player to stumble through its robust dataset driven purely by narrative curi­ osity to find out what happened next or what happened before that led to the current scene. Obra Dinn provides a framework narrative structure of an explicit investigatory task and uses visual details to pique interest and direct attention. It is a tour de force in embedding complex narrative information in an original and highly condensed visual format. Obra Dinn also uses a common detective game feature of the Notebook in a highly original way to provide a powerful navigation and feedback mechanism.

The essential pleasure of the narrative archive form is the episte­ mological quest, the discovery of more information about a series of complex events in which the paths induce specific focuses of narrative curiosity, and the open-ended traversal and assembly of the narrative fragments into one or more coherent sequences. Both Pope and Barlow have proven that the exploration of a narrative archive can produce the experience of dramatic agency. The contrast in the satisfactions afforded by their different approaches suggests that transparent game mechanics, such as a detective's notebook, and standard dramatic strategies, such as limiting the number of characters and focusing on their individual motivations, can enhance the experience of dramatic agency. The emphasis on the detective coming up with a winning solution privileges more game-like approaches, and the emphasis on the revelation of the hidden story privileges more narrative solutions, but there is no reason why these double satisfactions cannot be more powerfully combined in a more story-driven but cognitively challenging interactive narrative design. The tension between ludic and narrative elements is not inevi­ table. It is the result of separate traditions of practice. The tantalizing pleasures of these two interactive narratives make it clear that there is much to build upon as the genre continues to develop.
